Two young clerks, Hamilton and Hoffman, each earn six hundred dollars a year, yet their financial situations are starkly different. Hamilton has saved one hundred dollars for a vacation to Niagara Falls and the White Mountains, while Hoffman is deeply in debt and cannot understand how saving so much is possible.

Hamilton examines Hoffman’s spending and calculates the annual cost of his seemingly minor indulgences: cigars, billiards, ale and oysters, theater and opera visits, jewelry, transportation, sweets, drives, and excursions. Together with basic expenses, these purchases bring Hoffman’s yearly spending to nearly eight hundred dollars—about two hundred dollars more than his income.

Shocked and ashamed, Hoffman realizes that small, habitual expenses have accumulated into serious debt while providing little lasting benefit. Hamilton urges him to practice foresight, restraint, and careful budgeting. Recognizing that his habits threaten both his finances and self-discipline, Hoffman resolves to change his conduct and live within his means.

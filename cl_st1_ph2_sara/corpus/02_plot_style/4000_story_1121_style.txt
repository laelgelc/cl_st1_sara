The story uses a late-nineteenth- or early-twentieth-century realist style, combining social observation with melodramatic tragedy and touches of ironic humour. Its setting is rendered through concrete details of neighbourhood commerce—candy jars, oysters, charcoal, knitting, cobblestones and cramped shops—while the narrator situates these details within a broader portrait of class, ethnicity and local custom. The New Orleans milieu is especially marked by references to Prytania Street, the French Quarter and “lagniappe,” giving the prose a strong sense of place.

The narration is third-person omniscient and openly authorial. Rather than remaining neutral, the narrator interprets characters, generalises about the neighbourhood and occasionally addresses the reader directly, as in “you must admit.” The voice is observant, sardonic and socially knowing, often presenting communal gossip as narrative fact. It moves freely between an external account of events, commentary on local attitudes and brief access to the wife’s suppressed feelings. This creates the impression of a story told by someone intimately familiar with the street and its collective memory.

Characterisation is strongly contrastive and physically emphatic. Tony is repeatedly enlarged through images of Hercules, Samson, a Titan and a “fallen giant,” while the woman is described as thin, pale, pinched, worn and almost erased. Their unequal bodies symbolise their unequal power. Repeated motifs reinforce this opposition: Tony growls, roars, drinks and occupies space; his companion sighs, shivers, creeps, pleads and knits. Her incessant needlework becomes both a nervous habit and an emblem of endurance, monotonous labour and emotional repression. The “stifled sigh,” dull eyes, worn fingers and dingy shawl recur as compressed signs of her condition.

The diction alternates between vivid, elevated description and colloquial speech. Narrative passages employ ornate comparisons, Latinate phrasing and mythological or biblical allusions, whereas dialogue is blunt, idiomatic and sometimes dialectal. Contractions, elisions and phonetic spellings—such as “Gimme fi’ cents” and “want ’em”—produce an audible street-level texture. Tony’s speech is dominated by commands, insults and refusals; the woman’s by hesitant questions, repetitions, broken syntax and appeals. Their verbal patterns dramatise domination without requiring extensive psychological explanation.

Sentences vary between compact narrative statements and long, accumulative descriptions. Lists of objects, occupations and repeated routines create a sense of crowded material life and years passing without change. Parallel constructions such as “No matter what…” and recurring phrases such as “life went on pretty much as before” establish habitual, almost mechanical rhythms. Dashes, semicolons and parenthetical interruptions allow the prose to move between action and commentary, while repetitions in the climactic dialogue intensify the wife’s desperation.

Structurally, the story begins with an ordinary shop transaction, broadens into an extended social and biographical portrait, and then narrows around Tony’s final illness. Much of the middle is iterative, summarising years of abuse and routine rather than presenting a sequence of singular scenes. Once Tony becomes bedridden, the pace slows and scenes are dramatised more fully. His illness initially suggests liberation, but the arrival of John redirects the plot toward the final revelation: the woman called “Tony’s wife” by the entire neighbourhood was never legally or sacramentally married to him. The ending is abrupt and bleak, converting a social label into a cruel irony and leaving her labouring hands as the final image.

The tone blends pity, grotesque comedy and fatalism. Tony’s exaggerated masculinity can be comic, particularly when gout renders him helpless, but the humour continually borders on cruelty. The wife’s laughter at the doctor’s prognosis provides a brief eruption of long-repressed joy, yet it does not lead to freedom. Dramatic irony governs the conclusion: death removes the abuser but also enables his last act of dispossession. The story’s emotional force comes from this reversal and from the gap between the neighbourhood’s casual assumptions and the woman’s legal vulnerability.

The prose also reflects the period’s ethnic and racialised habits of representation. Characters are repeatedly reduced to national or religious types, and expressions such as “little Jew girl,” “German,” “daughter of Erin” and broad claims about Italians function as shorthand. These labels help construct the story’s heterogeneous urban setting, but they also carry stereotypes and prejudices characteristic of its historical context. Class prejudice is treated more critically: the supposedly refined neighbourhood condescends to tolerate the shopkeepers while depending on their goods and labour.

Overall, the style is picturesque urban realism sharpened by authorial irony, symbolic physical contrasts and a melodramatic final turn. Its most distinctive features are the gossipy omniscient narrator, sensuous local detail, recurring domestic motifs, mythic comparisons applied to ordinary people, sharply differentiated dialogue and a tragic ending centred on the social and legal powerlessness of an abused working-class woman.

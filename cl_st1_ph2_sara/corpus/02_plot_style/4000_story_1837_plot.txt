A narrator visits his brilliant, eccentric friend Emberlin, who announces that he has solved an obscure literary mystery: Ben Jonson’s remark that “Eupompus gave splendour to Art by Numbers.” Drawing on a rare scholarly work, Emberlin recounts how Eupompus, a successful Alexandrian portrait painter, abandoned conventional art after becoming obsessed with counting and convinced that numbers were the only certain reality.

Eupompus began painting enormous, meticulously enumerated scenes, including thirty-three thousand black swans and crowds arranged in mathematical patterns. His admirers, the Philarithmics, treated counting as contemplation. His art grew increasingly abstract as he tried to depict ideas, pure number, and ultimately the totality of God and the universe. Before completing his final work, he killed two disciples with a hammer and then leapt to his death.

Four months after telling this story, Emberlin has himself become a devoted Philarithmic. He compulsively counts tiles, footsteps, distances, and other trivial quantities, while every conversation turns to numerical facts and speculations. His friends’ warnings fail, and his formerly rich intellect deteriorates into a narrow obsession. The narrator concludes that Eupompus may have killed himself not because he had finally gone mad, but because a sudden return of sanity revealed the absurdity and degradation into which his numerical fanaticism had led him.

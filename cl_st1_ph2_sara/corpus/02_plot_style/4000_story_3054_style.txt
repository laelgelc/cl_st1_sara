The story uses a polished Edwardian comic-satirical style, combining country-house realism with a tightly controlled progression towards macabre farce. Its social world is upper-middle- and upper-class British: bridge, shooting, inherited property, parliamentary campaigning and Alpine holidays supply both the setting and the standards by which competence is judged. Period diction such as “rubber,” “prospective brother-in-law,” “hied them away” and “husband-elect” establishes the milieu without extensive description.

The narration is third-person and largely aligned with Rupert’s perspective. Although the narrator has access to his motives and resentments, the voice remains sufficiently detached to expose the disproportionate severity of his judgments. Characterisation is economical and caricatural. The unnamed fiancé is reduced to the recurring epithet “the Sheep,” while his “defensive” or “apologetic smile,” ineptitude and useless remorse become refrain-like identifiers. Rupert is defined by competence, pride, grief and proprietorial anxiety; Kathleen remains comparatively lightly drawn because her main structural role is to connect the two men and complete the final reversal.

The dominant tone is urbane, ironic and increasingly dark. Long, ceremonious sentences describe foolishness with mock-gravity, creating humour through the contrast between elevated language and petty or absurd conduct. The narrator favours aphoristic generalisation, as in the meditation on “human sheep,” and often enlarges an individual defect into a sardonic observation about human nature. Biblical, military and melodramatic allusions—“an army with banners,” “dwell in the land and possess it,” and the indestructible “hero of a melodrama”—lend comic grandeur to incompetence. Euphemism and understatement sharpen the cruelty: hatred is a mere “gleam,” electoral disaster is “damage,” and death becomes the Sheep’s “definite” disappearance beneath the ice.

Dialogue is brief, socially restrained and adversarial. Rupert’s anger appears through clipped precision and “polite bitterness,” whereas the Sheep speaks in excuses, platitudes and cheerful misreadings of danger. Repetition gives these exchanges cumulative force: variants of “I’m so sorry” recur after failures at cards, shooting and politics, making apology itself evidence that nothing has been learned. The narrative voice frequently follows dialogue with a dry corrective or damning interpretation, so that the Sheep’s account of himself is continually undercut.

Structurally, the story is episodic and cumulative. Four set pieces—bridge, shooting, electioneering and skating—repeat the same behavioural pattern on an escalating scale. In each, the Sheep ignores or misunderstands a clear situation, causes harm, and retreats behind his smile. The consequences rise from a lost game to the killing of a cherished rare bird, the loss of an election and finally mortal danger. This repeated pattern gives the plot the logic of a comic proof: each episode confirms Rupert’s judgment while making the eventual catastrophe feel both surprising and inevitable.

The ending depends on reversal, delayed rescue and morally ambiguous poetic justice. Rupert genuinely attempts to save the man he despises, but the yard-dog prevents him from arriving in time. The animal’s intervention is described as delighted “guardian service,” converting accidental obstruction into grotesque usefulness. The final paragraph withholds overt moral comment. Kathleen marries the sensible Malcolm Athling, a child named Robbie restores Rupert’s broken family line, and the dog becomes his cherished companion. This serene domestic coda makes the outcome more chillingly comic because it reveals that the Sheep’s death has resolved every major problem in Rupert’s life.

Overall, the prose is formal, syntactically elaborate and epigrammatic, with a preference for balanced clauses, parenthetical qualifications, precise social observation and sudden terse judgments. Its humour is based on repetition, incongruity, understatement, caricature and ruthless narrative economy. The style avoids sentimentality even when touching on bereavement, love or death; emotional material is converted into motive, irony and structural payoff. The result is a compact black comedy in which social polish masks aggression, competence is treated as a moral virtue, and chance delivers the brutal resolution that decorum would never permit the characters to seek openly.

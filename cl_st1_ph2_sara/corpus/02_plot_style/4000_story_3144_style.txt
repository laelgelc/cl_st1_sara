The story adopts the style of a late nineteenth- or early twentieth-century didactic animal tale for children. It combines the conventions of a barnyard fable with realistic details of horse behaviour, anatomy, training, and farm work. Its central purpose is moral instruction: the Bay Colt’s resistance to advice causes avoidable discomfort, while patience, obedience, self-discipline, and willing work bring maturity and happiness. The lesson is stated openly rather than left implicit, especially in the narrator’s general observations about young animals learning to do right.

The narration is third-person omniscient, with free access to the animals’ thoughts, feelings, and motives. The narrator is warm, confident, and gently authoritative, frequently addressing the reader as “you” or appealing to shared knowledge through phrases such as “you know.” This creates the atmosphere of an adult storyteller speaking directly to children. The narrative voice regularly pauses the action to interpret behaviour, explain practical facts, or formulate a moral principle. It treats the horses as both animals and social analogues for people: they live in stalls, wear bits, pull ploughs, and display equine gestures, but they also reason, gossip, advise, sulk, feel pride, and negotiate family relationships in recognisably human ways.

The tone is affectionate, humorous, and mildly admonitory. The Bay Colt’s faults are presented as foolish rather than wicked, and the story never becomes harsh even when he suffers the consequences of his stubbornness. Humour arises from understatement, mock solemnity, and the translation of human social habits into horse terms. Examples include the Dappled Gray invoking the loss of the “black pits” in his teeth as proof of wisdom, the Colt’s pride in having unsplit hoofs, and the observation that two-year-olds often look more important than ten-year-olds and feel more so. The narrator’s irony remains gentle and protective, maintaining sympathy for the Colt while exposing his vanity and contrariness.

The diction is clear, domestic, and accessible, with occasional specialised equestrian vocabulary such as “halter,” “cribbing,” “curb-bit,” “snaffle,” “furrows,” and “plow.” Technical terms are embedded in explanatory contexts, so they enrich the farm setting without impeding a young reader. Capitalising animal names and categories—“Horse,” “Colt,” “Cows,” “Hogs,” and “Sheep”—gives the barn community the dignity of a miniature society. Characters are identified chiefly by colour, age, kinship, or physical trait rather than by personal names: the Bay Colt, the Gray Colt, the Dappled Gray, and the Blind Horse. This lends the story an archetypal, fable-like quality.

Sentence structure is generally fluent and moderately long, often linked by coordinating conjunctions and explanatory clauses. The prose has an oral storytelling rhythm, with repeated “and,” “but,” “for,” and “then” carrying events forward. Longer narrative sentences supply context and moral commentary, while dialogue is brisk, colloquial, and character-revealing. Repetition is used for emphasis and for a child-friendly cadence, as in “the next day, and the next day, and the next,” or the Gray Colt’s emphatic “I won’t go! I won’t go!” Contractions and idiomatic phrases—“Pooh!,” “Guess I know a thing or two,” “that’s all there is about it”—make the animals’ speech lively and informal.

Dialogue occupies a large part of the story and establishes character through contrasting verbal habits. The Bay Colt speaks boastfully, defensively, and in absolutes, repeatedly insisting that he can stop whenever he chooses or will never submit. The Gray Colt speaks modestly and sympathetically, acknowledging difficulty without using it as an excuse. The older horses employ proverbs, practical wisdom, restrained praise, and patient correction. Their speech reinforces a stable moral hierarchy in which age and experience deserve respect, although the elders ultimately teach most effectively by withdrawing unwanted advice and allowing experience to provide the lesson.

The plot is episodic but tightly unified around the Bay Colt’s maturation. An opening barn conversation introduces his cribbing and resentment of correction. A substantial flashback recounts his earlier fence-jumping and the humiliating wooden poke that cured him of running away. The narrative then returns to the present and develops the larger test of being broken to harness. Parallel treatment of the two cousins creates a clear contrast: the Gray Colt accepts guidance and progresses with relatively little pain, whereas the Bay Colt resists and develops a sore mouth. The climax is inward rather than dramatic, occurring when he recognises that his suffering is self-inflicted and resolves to change. The conclusion confirms his transformation through useful work, communal acceptance, and his mother’s forgiving maxim, “Colts will be Colts.”

Characterisation is broad, exemplary, and behaviour-based. The Bay Colt embodies youthful pride, contrariness, impulsiveness, and eventual capacity for self-correction. The Gray Colt serves as his foil, showing that goodness is not the absence of temptation but the decision to resist it; her admission that she “aches” to run away gives her virtue psychological credibility. The Dappled Gray is gruff but benevolent, the Blind Horse quietly wise, and the mothers concerned but patient. The farmer is depicted as firm, calm, and kind, never punishing in anger. This supports the story’s paternalistic worldview: legitimate authority is assumed to be benevolent, and submission to it becomes a route to competence and fulfilment.

Imagery is concrete and restrained, grounded in sensory details of barn and field: teeth grating on wood, a stem of hay dropping in sudden silence, ears moving restlessly, lines tightening against a bit, an apple core offered as a reward, and straight furrows opening behind a plough. Similes are simple and rural, such as a whispered breath moving through the stalls “like the wind passing over a wheatfield.” Animal gestures—snorting, whinnying, tossing the head, stamping, switching the tail, and arching the neck—function like human facial expressions and keep the anthropomorphism anchored in observed equine behaviour.

The dominant themes are obedience, maturity, self-mastery, responsibility, and the dignity of work. The story distinguishes coercive goodness from chosen goodness: the Bay Colt does not truly mature merely because he is restrained, but because he understands the consequences of his conduct and begins correcting himself. It also stresses that advice is motivated by affection, that freedom without discipline can become self-defeating, and that useful participation in a community produces pride and belonging. Overall, the style is earnest, orderly, reassuring, and explicitly moral, designed to entertain children through personified animals while guiding them toward socially approved habits of patience, humility, and willing cooperation.

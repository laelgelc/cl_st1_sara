The story adopts the manner of a formal, old-fashioned courtly fable, combining mock-historical narration with comic suspense and philosophical ambiguity. Its setting is deliberately exotic and only loosely specified: palaces, camels, sherbet, ceremonial dress, a cimeter and a “semi-barbaric” monarchy create a stylised storybook world rather than a realistic culture. This remoteness allows arbitrary royal power and ritualised violence to be treated with playful detachment.

The narrator is omniscient, composed and faintly ironic. Events are recounted as though they belong to an established chronicle, with phrases such as “it so happened,” “at the latest accounts” and “it is well known.” This air of documentary authority contrasts humorously with the absurdity of the institutions described. The narration rarely comments directly, instead allowing inflated ceremony, extreme obedience and euphemistic language to expose the king’s capriciousness. The recurring description “semi-barbaric” is especially ironic, since sophisticated court etiquette coexists with the constant threat of execution.

The diction is elevated, ceremonious and archaic. Words and phrases such as “deputation,” “demeanour,” “beauteous,” “concourse,” “boon,” “obeisance,” “whereupon” and “drawing nigh” imitate the register of romance, legend and nineteenth-century prose. Characters address one another through elaborate honorifics—“Most noble officer,” “Most noble strangers” and “your Highness”—while violence is concealed behind decorous titles and formulas. The executioner’s designation as the “Discourager of Hesitancy” is the clearest instance of comic euphemism: lethal coercion is converted into an almost bureaucratic court appointment.

The syntax is generally expansive and carefully balanced. Long, multi-clause sentences establish circumstances, enumerate possibilities and reproduce chains of reasoning. Repetition and parallel construction are prominent, particularly in the prince’s internal debate: “Would she not…? Would she not…?” Such patterns dramatise indecision while making the argument increasingly circular. Short sentences are then used as comic or suspenseful closures. “The prince looked at him, and proceeded to be measured for a coat” compresses threat, submission and punchline into a single dry statement. Likewise, the repeated whisper “I am here” gains force through its brevity and its contrast with the surrounding ornate prose.

Dialogue occupies much of the narrative and is highly theatrical. The king speaks in commands, interruptions and sudden roars, conveying impatience and absolute authority. The prince uses cautious, rational and comparatively modern language, even asking to assess the women’s “relative intellectual development,” which makes him seem reasonable but also slightly pompous. Courtiers answer through resigned maxims such as “What our king thinks right, that will he do.” These distinct speech patterns create character economically: the king is impulsive, the prince analytical, the officials ceremonially obedient, and the Discourager courteously menacing.

The structure is recursive and framed. A deputation arrives to demand the solution to an earlier unresolved dilemma, but the officer responds by telling another tale built on the same principle. The embedded narrative occupies almost the entire story and culminates in a second forced choice between outwardly plausible alternatives. Rather than supplying an answer, it makes the original uncertainty conditional upon a new uncertainty. The ending therefore loops back to the opening question and deliberately frustrates narrative closure.

Suspense is produced through delayed information and restricted perception. During the wedding, the prince is blindfolded, so both he and the reader must rely on touch, sound and inference. Sensory details—the rustle of silk, the softness of a hand, the sweetness of a voice and the razor edge of the cimeter—heighten immediacy while withholding decisive visual knowledge. Once the blindfold is removed, the problem becomes interpretive rather than sensory: the prince must decode smiles, frowns, blushes and silence. The narrative repeatedly narrows his options while accelerating the deadline, culminating in the king’s ten-second ultimatum.

The pacing alternates between leisurely ceremonial description and abrupt pressure. Preparations, costumes and protocols are described at length, but moments of choice are punctuated by commands, threats and interruptions. Enumerations—of craftsmen, courtiers, refreshments and possible interpretations—slow the action and build comic excess. By contrast, the Discourager’s appearances sharply restore the physical stakes. This alternation sustains both humour and tension.

The humour is predominantly dry, satirical and incongruous. Politeness accompanies brutality; marriage resembles a judicial ordeal; and the prince’s desire for thoughtful courtship is answered by tailors, a blindfold and an executioner. The Discourager handles his weapon “as tenderly as though it had been a sleeping infant,” a simile that combines menace with absurd delicacy. There is also verbal play in phrases such as “raptures rampant,” and situational comedy in the prince’s immediate obedience whenever the cimeter appears.

At the thematic level, the story explores uncertainty, interpretation, coercion and the human appetite for definitive answers. Smiles and frowns prove radically ambiguous because each can support opposite conclusions. Rational analysis does not resolve the problem; it multiplies possibilities until authority imposes action. Choice is therefore presented as superficially free but actually constrained by incomplete knowledge and mortal danger. The tale also satirises arbitrary government: the king turns personal wishes into law, while courtly ceremony disguises compulsion as generosity and justice.

Overall, the style is that of an elegant paradox or literary puzzle. Its signature features are formal omniscient narration, archaic and courtly diction, nested storytelling, extended rhetorical reasoning, deadpan comic understatement, euphemistic menace, carefully timed repetition and an unresolved ending that transfers the burden of judgment from the characters to the reader.

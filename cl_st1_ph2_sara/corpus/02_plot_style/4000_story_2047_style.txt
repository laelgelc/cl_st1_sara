The story adopts the manner of a late-Victorian or Edwardian moral-psychological tale, narrated retrospectively by an urbane, self-deprecating man of letters. Its first-person narrator is both participant and witness: close enough to Cyril Harjohn to interpret his conduct emotionally, but sufficiently limited to preserve the central mystery of his self-destructive attraction. He presents himself as a worldly “hack-journalist,” yet his voice repeatedly reveals tenderness, idealism, and a taste for moral generalisation. The distance between the older narrator and his younger self creates a double perspective. Past events are recalled with warmth and immediacy, while the mature voice interrupts them with rueful qualifications such as “Had I known the world better,” stressing belated understanding and tragic inevitability.

The prose is formal, polished, and highly rhetorical. Long, balanced sentences alternate with short aphoristic declarations, producing a rhythm of reflection followed by emphasis. Parallel constructions are especially prominent: “Between a junior resident master…” is answered by “Between a struggling journalist…,” while contrasts such as childhood and adulthood, saint and animal, battlefield and vineyard, and angel and devil organise the narrative’s thought. The narrator frequently converts particular experience into epigram: childhood is “an over-rated period”; an assured income may be “the grave of ambition”; “we dream our poems, not live them.” These maxims give the voice confidence and elegance, though they also expose its tendency to understand people through sweeping moral categories.

The diction blends cultivated literary language with colloquial masculine speech. Elevated passages contain biblical cadence, religious vocabulary, classical allusion, and personification: Cyril is imagined as a martyr or soldier of civilisation; Elspeth is associated with Madonnas, saints, Artemis, and Endymion; Nature and Fate act as shaping powers. By contrast, conversations use idiomatic expressions such as “damned fool,” “play the fool,” “wrong ’un,” and “poor devil.” This movement between lofty narration and blunt dialogue dramatises the split at the heart of Cyril’s character. Social and institutional references—Brasenose, Guy’s, Bloomsbury, Fleet Street, Soho, Staple Inn, clubs and artistic gatherings—create a densely specific metropolitan world and establish class, education, and profession economically.

Description is selective and interpretive rather than neutral. Settings are usually conveyed through one or two sensory details: Soho restaurants are “dingy” and “garlic-scented,” the city moans like a tired child, and a London street is “small” and “evil-smelling.” Faces and bodies function as moral signs. Cyril appears haloed; Elspeth’s beauty ennobles those around her; Geraldine’s resemblance to her father is treated as evidence of inherited cruelty and greed. Female characters are therefore rendered as symbolic opposites more than fully autonomous individuals. Elspeth embodies spiritual purity and redemptive love, while Geraldine represents appetite, sensuality, social ambition, and destruction. This saint-versus-temptress scheme is deliberately stark and characteristic of the story’s period, as are its deterministic assumptions about physiognomy, heredity, gender, class, and ethnicity.

Dialogue carries much of the dramatic and psychological force. Early exchanges establish character through wit and social friction, but the crucial nocturnal conversation becomes a sustained confession. Cyril diagnoses himself more harshly than the narrator can, anticipating every objection and stripping idealism from his own motives. His speech grows repetitive and vehement—“down, down, down”; “give, give, give”; “my heart, and my brain, and my soul”—using accumulation to suggest panic and self-loathing. The narrator’s comparatively weak replies reinforce his helplessness. At other points, dialogue is used ironically: Geraldine states her mercenary intentions with brutal frankness, while the acquaintance who reports Cyril’s death does so in casual clubland language. The narrator’s final “Dear me… how very sad!” is deliberately inadequate, concealing the morally troubling relief signalled by the unspoken “Thank God.”

Structurally, the story begins as a compressed biography, moving rapidly from school to Oxford, London, professional ambition, and love. It then slows around the triangle formed by Cyril, Elspeth, and Geraldine. Foreshadowing is persistent: the narrator repeatedly announces that his earlier interpretations were mistaken, hints at Cyril’s concealed nocturnal life, and frames his inner division as fatal. The promised escape abroad appears to offer a recovery, but the coincidence of Geraldine’s voyage to Calcutta restores the tragic trajectory. Cyril’s death is reported indirectly and ambiguously. The shooting may be accidental, as the acquaintance assumes, but its timing and the narrator’s reaction invite the possibility of suicide without confirming it. This withholding gives the ending its force.

The prevailing tone combines nostalgia, affection, irony, melodrama, and moral pessimism. Comic observations—Fauerberg’s pupils are treated like crammed geese, and a policeman mistakes the friends’ emotional confrontation for drunken disorder—briefly relieve the gravity. Yet the narrative steadily darkens from fond reminiscence into fatalistic tragedy. Its central concern is divided human nature: lofty aspiration does not eliminate appetite, and may coexist with an equally powerful capacity for degradation. The style magnifies this conflict through antithesis, symbolic characterisation, biblical and classical imagery, authorial aphorism, and retrospective irony. The result is a polished confessional tragedy in which the narrator can explain nearly everything about his friend except the one fact that matters most: why clear moral knowledge proves powerless against desire.

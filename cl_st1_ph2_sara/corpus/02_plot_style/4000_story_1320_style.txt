The story uses a first-person retrospective narrator who participates in the journey but functions chiefly as an observant witness. His personality remains comparatively unobtrusive, allowing the surrounding landscape, the stranded travellers, and especially the exchanges between social classes to dominate. At intervals he shifts from concrete observation into aphoristic reflection, generalising from the immediate inconvenience to human folly, suffering, altruism, and the impulse to regulate other people’s lives. These reflections give the narration a moral and philosophical dimension, but they are repeatedly undercut by the characters’ bodily needs and petty behaviour.

The prose combines social realism with grotesque caricature. Characters are introduced through sharply selected physical details: Isaiah’s asymmetrical beard and enormous swelling, the judge’s red moustache, Mamaieff’s cunning eyes, and Kireelka’s bent, ragged, almost animal-like posture. Description frequently exaggerates bodies, clothes, and gestures until they become comic or disturbing. Isaiah’s pony is compared to a donkey, a poodle, a mortar, and a millstone, while Kireelka appears as though he has been “half masticated” by a monster. Such imagery makes the social world seem misshapen and absurd, yet the grotesquerie does not erase sympathy; it exposes degradation while preserving the characters’ complexity.

The natural setting is extensive, atmospheric, and symbolically charged. The thawing Russian landscape is rendered through a muted palette of grey, blue-grey, black, sandy, and leaden tones. Heavy clouds, damp air, muddy water, broken ice, and bare fields create a mood of exhaustion and suspended expectation. Nature is repeatedly personified as melancholy, oppressed, diseased, or awaiting release. The river resembles a body covered in scabs and sores, while the shifting ice becomes scales, wrinkles, teeth, and fragments of winter clothing. These metaphors give the landscape a physical, almost pathological presence. The blocked river also mirrors the social situation: movement is delayed, conversation circles without resolution, and apparent progress remains obstructed by entrenched attitudes.

The dominant tone is satirical, bleakly comic, and compassionate at once. Much of the humour arises from contradiction. The travellers solemnly debate education, poverty, discipline, and the future of the peasantry while remaining preoccupied with hunger, transport, status, and personal comfort. The judge denounces peasant inadequacy even as he depends on peasants to ferry him across the dangerous river and eventually eats Kireelka’s last loaf. Isaiah’s deferential agreement is transparently ironic, and Kireelka’s submissive answers conceal practical intelligence and quiet mockery. The satire therefore works dramatically rather than through direct denunciation: the privileged characters expose themselves through their own speeches.

Dialogue occupies a large proportion of the story and is used as the principal instrument of characterisation. Speech is socially differentiated. The judge is imperious, repetitive, self-contradictory, and fond of rhetorical questions and abstract pronouncements. Mamaieff echoes authority with ingratiating qualifications and ready agreement. Isaiah alternates between abuse, philosophical speculation, mock humility, and covert sarcasm. Kireelka speaks evasively and elliptically, often answering power with formulations such as “That depends upon circumstances” or “just as you wish.” His apparent simplicity is strategic: he avoids direct confrontation while allowing his superiors’ assumptions to defeat themselves. Exclamations, interruptions, dashes, repeated oaths, and unfinished clauses give the dialogue an agitated, spontaneous quality.

The syntax alternates between long, cumulative descriptive sentences and brisk conversational exchanges. Narrative sentences often accumulate participial phrases, parallel clauses, and successive visual details, producing a slow, panoramic movement suited to the journey and the waiting. By contrast, dialogue is clipped, jagged, and full of abrupt commands. This alternation controls the rhythm: meditative passages broaden the scene, while verbal skirmishes accelerate it. Repetition is important, particularly in the judge’s demands, Isaiah’s curses, the characters’ complaints of hunger, and the recurring observation that the boats or ice will arrive “soon.” The effect is one of circularity and delay.

The diction belongs to an older literary register, with formal constructions such as “whilst,” “ought not,” and “deigned to reply,” alongside Russian social and cultural terms including “verst,” “arshin,” “armiah,” “Zemstvo,” and “moujik.” Long explanatory character introductions and elaborate similes reinforce the nineteenth-century realist manner. The text also bears signs of translation into period English: some phrasing is ceremonious or syntactically stiff, and the dialogue occasionally combines Russian social idiom with English expressions such as “Devil take it all.” This creates a slightly foreignised texture rather than fully naturalised colloquial speech.

Structurally, the story is organised around a simple impeded journey. The travellers approach the river, discover that the ferry is unavailable, wait and argue, share the peasant’s bread, and finally cross. This minimal action provides a confined stage on which a social hierarchy can reveal itself. The delay is crucial: deprived of movement, the characters fill time with theories about the peasantry, while the silent peasant remains physically present as the object of their discussion. The climax is not the dangerous crossing but the final shouted request from Kireelka, which reveals that the “gentry” have eaten his last loaf. That closing line retrospectively concentrates the story’s irony: those who claim authority to diagnose and improve the peasant consume his meagre resources and leave him to replace them.

Characterisation is deliberately paradoxical. Isaiah is ugly, drunken, abusive, philosophically perceptive, and capable of satirical tact. Kireelka is heroic and thieving, pious and drunken, abject and cunning. The judge is educated and articulate but authoritarian, obtuse, and ruled by appetite. These combinations resist simple moral categories. The story repeatedly suggests that human beings possess “complex characters” to which many contradictory labels may apply. Its realism therefore depends less on psychological intimacy than on incongruous traits, revealing gestures, and clashes between professed principles and actual conduct.

Overall, the style is that of socially observant realist satire enriched by grotesque portraiture, symbolic landscape writing, and dramatic irony. Its governing method is juxtaposition: lofty political argument against hunger, paternalistic concern against exploitation, outward submission against inward mockery, and human pretensions against the indifferent power of the thawing river. The ending is concise and epigrammatic, converting a seemingly anecdotal travel episode into a pointed critique of class privilege and the hypocrisy of speaking for people whose material needs are ignored.

The story uses a vigorous frontier-adventure style shaped by physical action, harsh environment, black comedy, and moral irony. Its prose is muscular and kinetic, relying on forceful verbs such as “clawing,” “kicking,” “floundered,” “crashed,” and “lunging” to make violence feel immediate. Bodies, dogs, ropes, cookware, canvas, snow, and ice are repeatedly brought into collision, giving the action a coarse materiality. The setting is not merely descriptive background but an active moral and psychological force: cold, darkness, isolation, and monotony strip away social restraint and encourage “primal” behavior.

The narration is third-person omniscient, moving freely between close attention to Jan’s instinctive will to survive and broader commentary on the collective mentality of the men. At key points, the narrator interrupts the action with expansive reflections on madness, blood-lust, religion, law, and the influence of climate. These passages adopt a confident, aphoristic voice, as in the argument that conventional morality becomes unreliable in the Northland. The result is a mixture of naturalistic explanation and mythic generalization: behavior is presented as the product of environment, bodily pressure, accumulated deprivation, and primitive drives rather than calm ethical choice.

The tone combines brutality with broad farce. A near-lynching is treated with grotesque comic incongruity: the men complain that Jan will not “hang decent and peaceable,” debate etiquette and legal principles while wrestling on the ground, and discuss execution as though it were an inconvenient piece of practical work. The comedy depends on understatement, literal-mindedness, professional pride, and the contrast between mortal danger and casual banter. This humor does not remove the violence; instead, it makes the men’s distorted moral assumptions more conspicuous. The final revelation that the supposed murder victim is alive turns the entire execution into an elaborate, grimly comic mistake.

Dialogue dominates much of the story and strongly differentiates character. Speech is rendered through heavily phonetic dialects: Red Bill uses rough frontier contractions, Lawson speaks in nautical idiom, Mr. Taylor adopts ceremonious Southern rhetoric, and Jan’s German-accented English reverses consonants and simplifies grammar. These dialects create an oral, theatrical quality and allow characterization to emerge through rhythm and vocabulary rather than exposition. Mr. Taylor’s elaborate courtesy, Lawson’s commands, Bill’s practical hangman’s pride, and Jan’s combative literalism remain recognizable even amid the confusion. By modern standards, the accent spellings are conspicuously stylized and stereotyped, but within the story they serve as a principal comic and characterizing device.

Sentence structure shifts with narrative function. The fight scenes use rapid clauses, participles, shouted fragments, and cumulative action to generate momentum. Longer periodic sentences appear in descriptive and philosophical passages, where parallel constructions and repeated conjunctions lend the prose a sweeping, rhetorical cadence. Exclamations, commands, interruptions, and sentence fragments reproduce the disorder of group struggle. Repetition is especially important: Jan’s inward cry, “To live! To exist!”, reduces motivation to its biological core, while recurring appeals to neatness, decency, and procedure turn the lynching into absurd ritual.

Imagery is elemental and frequently animalistic. Jan is described as a “hairy devil,” a “cornered rat,” and a snorting sea creature; the struggling men resemble a “cyclopean, many-legged monster.” Such comparisons blur the boundary between human beings and beasts, reinforcing the naturalistic idea that extreme conditions expose instinct beneath civilization. At the same time, the prose occasionally enlarges the scene into tragic spectacle, as when the combat writes “a tragedy of human passion” across the white snow. The white, homogeneous landscape functions both visually and structurally: its emptiness seems to save Jan because there is initially nowhere to hang him, only for human ingenuity to transform oars into a gallows.

The story’s structure is built around escalation, reversal, and renewed conflict. It opens in medias res with Jan already resisting execution, delays the explanation of the alleged murder, then moves through escape, recapture, legal debate, and the practical problem of constructing a gallows. Jan briefly gains control through comic logic, but Lawson defeats that logic with technical improvisation. At the apparent climax, the dead man rises and invalidates the entire proceeding. The ending refuses reconciliation: Jan remains “the Unrepentant,” and the restored victim proposes another fight. This circular return to aggression emphasizes that the underlying violence was never solely about justice.

The governing themes are the fragility of civilization, the instability of law outside institutions, survival instinct, masculine pride, and the absurdity of improvised justice. Biblical language, constitutional principle, Southern honor, seafaring competence, and frontier custom are all invoked, but none supplies dependable moral authority. Law becomes a performance conducted by exhausted, excitable men, while religious maxims are treated ironically as luxuries of temperate climates. The overall style is therefore energetic, sardonic, naturalistic, and theatrically comic, presenting frontier violence as simultaneously terrifying, ridiculous, and environmentally conditioned.

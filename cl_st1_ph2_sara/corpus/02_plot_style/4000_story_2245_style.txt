The story uses a late-Victorian Gothic-adventure style, combining a supernatural haunting with the procedural logic of a colonial detective tale. It opens with a mock-archaic verse epigraph whose ballad diction—“the story saith,” “patient wraith,” “piteous thing”—announces the central image of a voiceless ghost pursuing recognition. The prose that follows is more conversational and concrete, but retains an antiquated flavour through formal constructions, emphatic repetitions, compressed moral observations, and occasional biblical or proverbial cadences.

The narrative is delivered retrospectively in the first person by an educated British observer. This narrator is personally involved but is not the principal investigator: he registers sensations, fear, and social discomfort, while Strickland supplies composure, interpretation, and decisive action. The resulting voice mixes eyewitness credibility with club-room anecdote. It often sounds as though an extraordinary colonial incident is being recounted to familiar listeners, with references to other adventures and to a shared administrative world. The narrator is candid about his fear and frequently undercuts tension with dry self-mockery, as when practical objections, dislike of snakes, food, sleep, and accommodation intrude upon the supernatural crisis.

A defining feature is tonal doubleness. Horror is narrated beside bureaucratic understatement, social comedy, and sardonic commentary. Imray’s disappearance briefly inconveniences the Empire only “in a microscopical degree”; a corpse is called “the arrangement under the cloth”; and Strickland treats murder, haunting, and confession with the manner of an official handling an awkward case. This euphemistic restraint makes the macabre material more disturbing because emotional reactions are displaced into irony, practical action, or terse dialogue. The story repeatedly contrasts immense human consequences with the impersonal continuity of imperial administration.

The syntax alternates between long, accumulating descriptive sentences and short declarative blows. Extended sentences catalogue possessions, weather, architecture, and animal behaviour, often adding clause after clause until a complete physical and social environment has been established. At moments of revelation, the prose contracts: “It is Imray”; “his throat is cut from ear to ear”; “He has killed.” Parallel phrasing and repetition provide oral momentum, as in the sequence of searches for Imray or the ghost’s “fluttering, whispering, bolt-fumbling, lurking, loitering” activity. Dashes, semicolons, and parenthetical qualifications allow the narrator to shift quickly between observation, explanation, and ironic aside.

The diction joins several registers. There is precise colonial and sporting vocabulary—bungalows, ceiling-cloths, mahseer-rods, dogcarts, rifles, the Club—alongside sensuous natural description and quasi-legal language. Dialogue between British characters is clipped, idiomatic, and understated, while exchanges with servants become ceremonially formal, using terms such as “Sahib,” “Heaven-born,” and “Presence,” as well as stylised scriptural inversions. This creates a strong hierarchy of voices and reflects the period’s colonial assumptions. Indigenous characters are frequently generalised through racial categories and presented as culturally opaque, superstitious, or deceptive; such framing is central to the historical style but is also reductive and paternalistic.

The setting is rendered through dense sensory detail. Heat, damp, darkness, rain, smells, animal sounds, and the bungalow’s hidden roof-space create an atmosphere of physical saturation and concealed corruption. Particularly effective images are forceful and unexpected: rain falls “like ramrods,” lightning spatters the sky like a thrown egg, and the rigid dog is compared to the wire-rope of a suspension bridge. The architecture functions symbolically as well as practically. Beneath the neat white ceiling lies a dark cavity full of vermin and a hidden corpse, making the bungalow an image of orderly colonial surfaces stretched over violence, fear, and incomplete knowledge.

Suspense develops incrementally through indirect evidence. The ghost is not clearly shown; instead, curtains move, chairs creak, doors are tested, breathing is heard, and Tietjens tracks an invisible presence. The dog acts as an emotional barometer and evidentiary witness, giving bodily confirmation to what the narrator cannot see. Repeated twilight scenes establish a threshold state between daylight reason and nocturnal haunting. The apparent supernatural mystery is eventually converted into material proof and criminal confession, yet the ghost’s role remains functionally real: it presses the living toward discovery and justice.

Characterisation is economical and often built from habits or possessions. Strickland’s irregular meals, weapons, investigations, and intimacy with his dog establish him as eccentric, competent, and unusually receptive to local realities. The narrator’s practical nervousness provides contrast. Tietjens is anthropomorphised without losing her animal force; she “lays information,” chooses where to sleep, and returns to her bed only when the disturbance is resolved. Even minor figures are defined by memorable gestures, ritual phrases, or occupational detail rather than extended psychological exposition.

The plot has a controlled investigative shape: disappearance, abandonment, haunting, discovery, interrogation, confession, punishment, and restoration. Its pacing is initially leisurely, devoting substantial space to the bungalow, the rains, Strickland’s household, and Tietjens. Once the body falls through the ceiling, scenes become faster and more dialogue-driven. The confession arrives through psychological pressure rather than elaborate deduction, and the snakebite supplies an abrupt, fatalistic substitute for formal execution. The final return of Tietjens to her bed gives the story a circular, quietly theatrical closure: the household resumes order after the concealed dead man has been acknowledged.

Overall, the style depends on the friction between rational modernity and older systems of belief. Official institutions solve the case only after a ghost, a dog, indigenous superstition, coincidence, and Strickland’s intuition have done the essential work. The prose neither wholly rejects the supernatural nor explains it away; instead, it places haunting and evidence within the same practical world. Its signature effect is therefore one of controlled uncanniness: vividly atmospheric horror narrated in the dry, socially assured voice of imperial anecdote.

The story’s dominant mode is comic parody, specifically a burlesque of sentimental melodrama, rural romance, and the moralistic “mortgage villain” tale. It adopts the stock machinery of popular melodrama—an innocent maiden, a virtuous young hero, a wealthy and moustache-twirling villain, an imperilled homestead, abduction, concealed gold, urban corruption, lost children, and miraculous reversals—then repeatedly frustrates the genre’s moral and emotional expectations. Heroism proves ineffectual, innocence is largely performative, romance is opportunistic, and the heroine ultimately succeeds through calculation and blackmail rather than virtue.

The narrator is conspicuously intrusive, knowing, and ironic. Instead of maintaining the solemnity of the action, the narration interrupts it with corrections, parenthetical qualifications, technical details, and deflationary asides: “Hardman, not the cat,” “adverb, not noun,” and “He had survived—this much was almost immediately evident.” These interventions create a continual double perspective. Events are described in the reverent language of melodrama, while the narrator simultaneously exposes their absurdity, artificiality, or economic motive. The voice often pretends to share the genre’s admiration for beauty, innocence, and nobility, but small factual intrusions reveal its scepticism.

The diction deliberately mixes florid archaism with modern slang, commercial language, scientific notation, and mundane specificity. Elevated phrases such as “beauteous blonde,” “beloved of my spirit,” “suppliant kneeling in the dust,” and “hark!” sit beside “I should worry,” “fast worker,” “ten-spot,” “poor dub,” and “and then some.” Chemical formulas, decimal measurements, prices, advertising rates, hardware-store jewellery, wages, and mortgage terms puncture romantic abstraction. This collision of registers is a principal source of humour: the story treats clichés as though they required accounting, chemical analysis, or literal clarification.

Sentences alternate between ceremonious, elaborately qualified narration and short comic blows. Long sentences accumulate descriptive incongruities and qualifications, while abrupt statements deliver the anticlimax: “And the poor dub did.” Parentheses, dashes, exclamation marks, ellipses, italics, and capital letters intensify the mock-theatrical effect. Repetitions such as “mine, mine, mine,” extravagant cries of “Oh!” and “Ah!,” and emphatic revelations like “GOLD!!” imitate the typography and emotional excess of cheap sensational fiction. Chapter endings frequently operate as punch lines rather than genuine suspense.

Dialogue is intentionally stilted, declamatory, and overloaded with endearments. Characters rarely converse naturally; they announce their narrative roles and emotions in speeches. Lovers address one another as “light of my life,” “my angel,” and “my precious,” while the villain openly calls attention to his own ruthlessness. Dialect spellings such as “muh wife,” “crool,” “me love,” and “chee-ild” parody stage pronunciation and stereotyped theatrical delivery. Bathetic details repeatedly invade these speeches, most memorably the heroine’s insistence that her engagement ring be bought from a hardware store selling imitation diamonds.

Characterisation is schematic by design. Names advertise function: Jack Manly is the nominal hero, Hardman the cruel capitalist villain, and the rustic Stubbs family embodies coarse rural practicality. Physical traits are generic melodramatic signs—curly golden hair, black eyes, a Roman nose, a riding-crop, and a twirled moustache—but these signs are undermined by jokes about hair dye, cheap cosmetics, age, and money. Characters possess little psychological continuity because they change loyalties whenever the plot or their financial advantage requires it. Ermengarde is the clearest example: she is introduced as an innocent maiden but gradually emerges as resilient, calculating, and highly alert to inheritance, property, and leverage.

The plot is episodic, rapid, and aggressively contrived. Each short chapter introduces another conventional complication—courtship, foreclosure, kidnapping, urban flight, betrayal, adoption, return, recognition—and resolves it with arbitrary convenience. Coincidence is not concealed but exaggerated until it becomes part of the joke. Characters forget obvious solutions, survive improbable accidents, encounter precisely the people needed for the next reversal, and disclose secrets with remarkable efficiency. The narrator’s brisk transitions—“And so,” “One day,” “But alas,” and “When lo!”—imitate formulaic serial storytelling and keep the pace breathless.

Much of the comedy depends on bathos and anti-climax. Tears flow “like white ale”; an abducted heroine is guarded by a “hideous old hag,” yet her father hesitates to advertise because newspaper notices cost too much; Ermengarde rejects cabaret work not chiefly from moral horror but because the pay is poor; and her final romantic decision is a strategic calculation about gold and inheritance. Violence is treated with the same casual absurdity: a cat is kicked despite being “unquestionably innocent,” and Algernon is pushed from a train window before the heroine settles down to sleep.

The thematic centre is the exposure of sentimental ideals as disguises for material interest. Nearly every relationship is tied to money, property, wages, social status, or the gold beneath the farm. The villain’s greed is obvious, but the supposedly virtuous characters are scarcely less transactional. Even honesty is rewarded through adoption and wealth, while love repeatedly yields to better financial prospects. The ending completes the inversion of melodramatic morality: the heroine does not marry the faithful hero or defeat the villain through purity; she chooses the richest available arrangement and coerces the villain into marriage.

Overall, the style is exuberantly artificial, irreverent, and self-aware. It reproduces melodrama’s heightened language and rigid conventions closely enough to remain recognisable, then sabotages them through literalism, anachronistic slang, numerical precision, narrative commentary, economic realism, and shameless reversals. The resulting tone is gleefully deadpan: the narrator reports absurdities with mock gravity while allowing nearly every lofty emotion to collapse into appetite, vanity, or cash.

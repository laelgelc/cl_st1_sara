The story uses a learned, antiquarian mode of cosmic horror, presenting the supernatural through the sober language of scholarship, testimony, and documentary investigation. Its first-person narrator is educated, rationalistic, and retrospective, recounting events from a position of belated terror. He repeatedly stresses his former scepticism, which makes the eventual implications seem forced upon him by evidence rather than produced by credulity. This creates a confessional tone: the narrator is not simply telling a frightening story but recording knowledge he now considers psychologically dangerous.

The prose is formal, archaic, and highly Latinate. Words such as “aeons,” “monstrosity,” “hieroglyphics,” “antiquarian,” and “incalculable” give the narration an air of erudition and historical depth. Intensifying adjectives accumulate around objects and settings—“Cyclopean,” “nameless,” “abysmal,” “unhallowed,” “hideous,” “malignant”—so that description often conveys emotional dread before it supplies concrete information. The diction persistently emphasizes obscurity and failed classification: things are “unknown,” “unplaceable,” “undecipherable,” “indescribable,” or only vaguely analogous to familiar forms. The repeated inability to name or categorize phenomena is central to the effect.

Sentences are frequently long, syntactically layered, and periodic, with subordinate clauses, semicolons, parenthetical qualifications, and delayed conclusions. This produces a stately, accumulative rhythm resembling an academic report that gradually becomes overwhelmed by its subject. Shorter declarative sentences are used sparingly at moments of revelation or transition, giving them extra force. Ellipses in the epigraph and dashes in the narration suggest interrupted thought, suppressed knowledge, and connections too disturbing to articulate directly.

The narrative is constructed as a dossier. Its evidence includes manuscripts, press cuttings, dated notes, medical observations, archaeological objects, eyewitness accounts, institutional affiliations, addresses, and exact times. Nested narration is prominent: the principal narrator reads his uncle’s records, which contain Wilcox’s testimony and Legrasse’s account, which in turn includes statements from cult members and Professor Webb. This layering creates distance from the central horror while also multiplying apparent corroboration. Precise geographical and historical details lend plausibility to increasingly fantastic claims.

Pacing is slow and investigative rather than action-driven. Suspense develops through the piecing together of apparently unrelated facts: a death, a clay relief, recurring dreams, global disturbances, an ancient idol, and parallel rituals found in remote cultures. Revelations are deferred, and each answer opens a larger mystery. The story alternates between compressed summaries of research and extended set pieces, especially descriptions of artefacts, dream-cities, landscapes, and ritual scenes. Climactic moments depend less on surprise than on recognition—the realization that separate fragments belong to one immense pattern.

Imagery is monumental, geological, marine, and architectural. Vast cities, monoliths, swamps, subterranean spaces, black seas, green ooze, tentacles, wings, and colossal bodies evoke a world older and larger than humanity. Scale is repeatedly distorted: tiny idols represent gigantic entities, while human civilization becomes a transient incident within cosmic time. Darkness, depth, remoteness, and decay dominate the visual field. Sound is equally important, particularly chants, tom-toms, cries, unpronounceable syllables, and “voices” that cannot be translated into ordinary language.

The horror arises from epistemology as much as from physical threat. Knowledge is fragmented, and safety depends on keeping it fragmented; terror begins when facts are correlated. Dreams function as a channel through which ancient nonhuman powers reach sensitive minds, while art involuntarily records what reason cannot comprehend. Science and scholarship are treated ambivalently: they provide the methods that authenticate the mystery, but they also expose humanity to truths it may be unable to survive.

Characterization is subordinate to atmosphere and evidence. Figures tend to be defined by profession, temperament, and relation to knowledge—the sceptical heir, the venerable professor, the hypersensitive artist, the practical police inspector, the explorer-anthropologist. Dialogue is limited and often quoted because it has evidentiary value. When direct speech appears, it is elevated and aphoristic, as in Wilcox’s poetic comparison of dreams with ancient civilizations.

The tone combines intellectual restraint with emotional excess. The narrator uses cautious qualifiers—“perhaps,” “it seemed,” “I suppose,” “as far as he could tell”—yet surrounds them with superlative language and repeated declarations of horror. This tension between methodological caution and mounting hysteria is a defining stylistic feature. The narrator tries to preserve rational form even as the content destroys rational confidence.

The piece also reflects the prejudices of its historical idiom. Its descriptions of race, ethnicity, class, and cultural difference employ dehumanizing and hierarchical language, often associating marginalized peoples with degeneration, primitiveness, and occult threat. These attitudes are embedded in both characterization and horror imagery and are an important, though objectionable, component of the text’s period style.

Overall, the style can be characterized as documentary cosmic horror: ornate and archaizing prose, an unreliable but scholarly first-person witness, nested records, cumulative corroboration, delayed revelation, grotesque hybrid imagery, and a governing sense that humanity occupies an insignificant and precarious place in an ancient, incomprehensible universe.

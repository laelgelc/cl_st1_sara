The story uses a brisk, socially observant realist style enlivened by comedy, sentiment, and satirical irony. Its setting is rendered through sharply contrasted social spaces: the gaudy, performative luxury of the Pink Fountain Room; the hot, disciplined efficiency of the kitchen; the aromatic bustle of the bar; and, finally, the stale dining room after the celebration. Concrete occupational detail—trays, checking stamps, dish covers, wine labels, kitchen stations, tips, and waiters’ tricks—gives the narrative a strong sense of urban working life. The New Year’s festivities initially appear glamorous, but the accumulating images of drunkenness, debris, stale air, and moral exhaustion expose that glamour as tawdry and artificial.

The narrator is omniscient, conversational, and conspicuously present. Direct addresses such as “if you know what I mean,” informal confidences such as “privately, and entre nous,” and self-interruptions such as “there must be a stop to this description” create the impression of a witty storyteller speaking intimately to the reader. This voice shifts easily between affectionate teasing, mock solemnity, social criticism, and genuine tenderness. It often makes an assertion and then humorously corrects or qualifies it, as when apparent aristocrats and actresses are revealed to be ordinary workers in costume-like social roles. Parenthetical remarks, dashes, and asides are central stylistic devices, allowing the narrator to puncture pretension without losing sympathy for the characters.

Irony is the dominant rhetorical mode. The opening treats New Year’s customs as ancient rites being “rigidly observed,” turning drunken hotel behavior into mock anthropology. Gussie’s routine work as a kitchen checker is elevated into “goddessing,” with her desk figured as an altar surrounded by incense and burnt offerings. Heiny’s promotion to waiter is narrated as a Cinderella transformation, while the mechanical manners of service are described as though they were innate aristocratic gifts. These mock-heroic comparisons magnify ordinary people and occupations while simultaneously exposing the absurdities of status, fashion, and conspicuous consumption. The irony is generally warm rather than cruel: it laughs at vanity and affectation, but preserves respect for labor, loyalty, cleanliness, and kindness.

The prose alternates between long, cumulative descriptive sentences and short emphatic statements. Extended sentences pile up clothing, gestures, smells, sounds, and social details, reproducing the crowded abundance of the hotel. Parallel constructions and inversions—such as the repeated uncovering and inspection of dishes—give work routines a ceremonial rhythm. Short sentences and fragments then sharpen comic turns, emotional realizations, or changes of pace. Repetition is used extensively: “Thump!” marks the checking stamp and the relentless tempo of service; recurring references to Gussie’s cleanliness establish her symbolic role; and the repeated movement between dining room and kitchen reinforces the divide between spectacle and labor.

Sensory writing is especially prominent. The story layers sound through clattering dishes, clinking glasses, popping corks, scraping chairs, stamping checks, and swinging doors. Smell distinguishes environments: roasting and frying belong to the kitchen, while mint, citrus peel, cinnamon, clove, and alcohol define the bar. Color imagery is equally dense—pink fountains, white shirtwaists, violet draperies, gold hair, copper kettles, crimson cheeks—and often carries moral force. Artificial colors, cosmetics, satin, and jewels are associated with feverish display; white cloth, cool air, clear skin, coffee, ham on rye, and the gray woolen blanket suggest sanity, domestic comfort, and wholesomeness.

Characterization depends on visual caricature, occupational behavior, and revealing gesture. Minor figures are sketched quickly through one exaggerated physical feature or moral habit: Tony’s long fingers and petty frauds, Miss Sweeney’s thin malicious voice, the fleshy diner who beats time with a rye roll, or Tillie’s sodden clothes and broken teeth. Gussie is developed through recurring images of neatness, daisies, starched fabric, smooth collars, and clear coloring. Her immaculate appearance functions as more than physical description; it represents self-command, integrity, and emotional health. Heiny’s divided identity is encoded in his names. “Henri” signifies professional polish, social ambition, and distance, while “Heiny” signifies intimacy, ordinariness, and his authentic self. His emotional return is therefore dramatized linguistically when “all the Heiny in him” resurfaces.

Dialogue is colloquial, class-specific, and phonetically suggestive without becoming difficult to follow. Contractions, slang, nonstandard grammar, dropped consonants, and expressions such as “believe me,” “ain’t,” “sorehead,” and “ham on rye” establish an early twentieth-century urban American milieu. Speech distinguishes personalities: Gussie is dry, controlled, and quick-witted; Tony is threatening and insinuating; Miss Sweeney is gossipy and needling; Heiny’s language moves from waiterly restraint back to plain emotional directness. Much of the conflict is conducted indirectly through banter, professional exchanges, and insults disguised as jokes, allowing jealousy and hurt to remain socially contained until the closing reconciliation.

Structurally, the story moves from public spectacle to private moral recognition. The opening presents celebration at its most crowded and theatrical. The narrative then narrows to Gussie’s desk and her emotional predicament, builds tension through Heiny’s avoidance and Tony’s provocation, and reaches a darker climax after dawn, when the supposed “lovely doll” collapses and her companions abandon her. This incident strips the festivities of their glamour and enables Heiny to distinguish display from decency. The resolution deliberately returns to humble motifs introduced earlier: Joey’s, coffee, ham on rye, escorting Gussie home, and ordinary human care. The circularity makes reconciliation feel like a restoration of proper values rather than merely a romantic reunion.

The emotional tone combines humor with restrained pathos. Gussie’s heartbreak appears through small signs—a mist in her eyes, two tear marks on the checking sheet, trembling fingers—rather than through melodramatic confession. The narrator’s jokes protect the story from excessive sentimentality, while repeated domestic and tactile images give the ending warmth. Tillie’s final appearance broadens the moral beyond romance. Though physically unbeautiful, she becomes reassuring because of her labor and compassion, like the “gray woolen blanket” after a nightmare. The closing exchange of New Year’s wishes thus quietly relocates grace from the extravagant dining room to the workers who clean, serve, protect, and endure.

Overall, the style is comic-naturalistic and morally contrastive: exuberant in description, intimate in narration, theatrical in social observation, and sentimental in a controlled, self-aware way. Its central stylistic pattern is the deflation of false glamour followed by the elevation of ordinary goodness. Luxury becomes sordid; plainness becomes beautiful; “Henri” recedes into Heiny; and the true renewal promised by New Year’s is found not in champagne or spectacle, but in coffee, companionship, honest work, and humane recognition.

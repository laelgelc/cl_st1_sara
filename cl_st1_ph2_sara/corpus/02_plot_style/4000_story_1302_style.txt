The story uses a framed first-person narrative: an unnamed speaker reports a tale told by an acquaintance, while the acquaintance recounts his younger self’s encounter with Teresa. This double remove gives the piece the quality of an oral anecdote or moral testimony. The opening is deliberately plain and economical, moving quickly into the central recollection, while the final return to the outer narrator—marked by the acquaintance shaking ash from his cigarette—creates a reflective pause before the explicit moral conclusion.

The narrative voice is conversational, candid and strongly subjective. Teresa is initially presented through the student’s disgust and social prejudice, not through neutral description. His language is harsh, comic and dehumanising: she is associated with a “fishwife,” a “mastodon in petticoats,” a chimney-blackened dove and a face “carved out by a hatchet.” These grotesque comparisons expose both her formidable physical presence and the narrator’s youthful cruelty. The later revelation forces a retrospective reassessment of this vocabulary. Once her loneliness becomes clear, the narration shifts from ridicule to shame, tenderness and self-reproach. The story’s emotional force depends on this reversal of perception.

The diction combines colloquial speech with elevated, somewhat archaic phrasing. Expressions such as “d’ ye,” “Deuce take you,” “Wha-at?,” “Phew!” and “Well, well!” create an animated oral manner, while phrases such as “May the Mother of God protect thee,” “from thenceforth” and “universal impeccability” give the prose a period character. The mixture may also suggest translation: everyday exclamations sit beside formal abstractions and unusually elaborate constructions. Social labels—“questionable repute,” “fallen classes,” “path of righteousness”—are used with increasing irony, revealing the euphemism and hypocrisy of respectable society.

Sentence structure varies according to function. Descriptive passages often use long, cumulative sentences packed with physical details, comparisons and qualifying clauses. Moments of confrontation rely on short questions, fragments and rapid exchanges. Ellipses, dashes, exclamation marks and interjections reproduce hesitation, embarrassment and spontaneous speech. Parenthetical thoughts allow the younger narrator’s private mockery to run beside the spoken conversation, creating dramatic irony. Repetition is especially important in the revelation scene: “There’s no Boles,” “no Teresa,” and “he doesn’t exist” circle around the painful truth until the student finally understands it.

Dialogue dominates the central action and carries both characterisation and plot. Teresa’s speech is deferential, repetitive and emotionally direct, frequently addressing the narrator as “Mr. Student” or “sir.” Her uncertain explanations, malapropism-like confusion and physical agitation communicate illiteracy, vulnerability and fear of humiliation. The student’s replies are initially curt, patronising and suspicious. Their changing relationship is therefore registered less through authorial explanation than through altered patterns of speech: interrogation gives way to listening, and mockery gives way to participation in Teresa’s sustaining fiction.

The story is structured as a compact moral reversal. It begins with social distance and apparent grotesque comedy, develops through the small mystery of the letters, reaches its emotional climax when Teresa reveals that Boles is imaginary, and ends with a brief account of their reciprocal arrangement. The invented correspondence functions as the central symbol. Writing does not merely transmit information; it creates an imagined bond that makes loneliness bearable. The exchange of letters and mended clothes also forms a modest economy of mutual care: the student supplies tenderness in words, while Teresa answers through practical acts of service.

Tone is deliberately unstable. The early scenes are sardonic, contemptuous and faintly farcical, particularly in the contrast between Teresa’s sentimental language and her imposing body. During the disclosure, the comedy becomes awkward and painful. The dominant tone then turns compassionate, penitential and finally polemical. Even after the revelation, however, the prose retains rough humour: Teresa “roared” rather than wept, and the student calls himself a “blockhead.” This prevents the pathos from becoming wholly sentimental and preserves the speaker’s self-mocking candour.

Physical imagery is vivid, exaggerated and socially charged. Teresa is first rendered almost entirely as a body—large, dark, coarse, muscular and loud—whereas Boles exists only as language. The contrast between brutal materiality and imagined tenderness is central to the story. Particularly effective is the ironic dove image: Teresa calls herself a “sorrowing little dove,” and the narrator initially treats the phrase as absurd, but the later revelation shows that the emotional identity it expresses is genuine. Outer appearance and inner need are thus placed in sharp opposition.

The ending changes register from narrative to essayistic social criticism. The speaker generalises from Teresa’s case to condemn self-sufficiency, moral superiority and the category of the “fallen classes.” Rhetorical questions, collective pronouns and religious imagery broaden individual guilt into social indictment. “We” replaces “I,” implicating both narrator and reader. The image of respectable people “wrapped round in the rags of our virtues” reverses conventional moral hierarchies: supposed virtue becomes shabby concealment, while the socially condemned woman emerges as fully human. The conclusion is openly didactic, yet its moral authority comes from the preceding concrete episode of misjudgment rather than from abstract preaching alone.

Overall, the style combines realist social observation, grotesque caricature, oral storytelling, psychological revelation and humanitarian moral argument. Its defining movement is from surface judgment to inward recognition, and its principal technique is irony: the narrator believes Teresa ridiculous, only to discover that his own complacency is the more shameful and absurd condition.

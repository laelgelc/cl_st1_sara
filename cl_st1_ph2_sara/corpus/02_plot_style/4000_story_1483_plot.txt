A quiet, middle-aged Londoner tells his wife he is leaving briefly for the country, but secretly rents a room in the next street, initially intending to observe how his weeklong absence affects her. Driven by vanity, selfish curiosity, and indecision, he disguises himself and repeatedly postpones his return. Even when his wife falls gravely ill from distress, he remains hidden; after she recovers, the emotional gulf between them seems too great to cross.

For twenty years he haunts the neighborhood, watching his home and wife while gradually losing his place in her life. After ten years, they accidentally meet in a crowded street, but she does not consciously recognize him, and he retreats in anguish, realizing the madness of his self-imposed exile. Still, he continues telling himself he will return soon.

One cold, rainy evening in the twentieth year, he sees the warmth of his former home and impulsively enters as casually as though he had been absent only a day. He resumes life as a loving husband until his death, but his long disappearance demonstrates how stepping outside one’s place in human relationships, even briefly, can make a person an outcast who nearly loses that place forever.

The story uses a framed, first-person oral narrative: an unnamed woman recounts a supernatural experience to a small, skeptical audience gathered in a dark studio. The opening conversation establishes the performance as a story told aloud and creates an ironic governing principle—her promise to give only the “essentials”—which is repeatedly tested by the vivid physical, psychological, and circumstantial details that follow. Brief returns to the listening group, such as the observation that their pipes have gone out, reinforce the communal storytelling setting and heighten suspense by showing that the audience has become completely absorbed.

The narrative voice is conversational, self-aware, and confidential. The speaker frequently addresses her listeners directly through phrases such as “you know,” “as you may imagine,” and “any one of you men now listening to me.” These intrusions simulate spontaneous speech and allow her to anticipate skepticism, defend her reactions, and comment wryly on gender. Her voice combines independence and candor with emotional vulnerability. She describes herself as a modern, athletic, free-thinking “new woman,” yet openly admits that her confidence collapses under genuine danger. This tension gives the narrator psychological credibility while also placing the story within a social world concerned with women’s autonomy, respectability, and exposure to male judgment.

The diction moves between plain, colloquial language and elevated supernatural or spiritual vocabulary. Everyday references to cigarettes, omnibuses, policemen, hockey, half-crowns, gas jets, and middle-class tea parties anchor the experience in a recognizable London reality. Against this material background stand terms such as “psychical,” “vibrations,” “condition,” “soul,” “ecstasy,” and “glory.” The ghost’s explanation blends spiritualism with quasi-scientific language, treating space, embodiment, emotion, and haunting as metaphysical conditions. This mixture is characteristic of supernatural fiction that seeks to make the uncanny sound speculative rather than purely folkloric.

Sentence structure closely follows emotional intensity. Expository passages use long, layered sentences containing qualifications, parenthetical remarks, and retrospective commentary. During moments of fear, the prose becomes more fragmented and accumulative, reproducing the narrator’s racing thoughts: trivial objects and social anxieties crowd her consciousness without logical sequence. Short declarations—“I chilled,” “I was safe,” “The room was empty”—punctuate these longer passages and deliver sharp changes in tension. Repetition is especially prominent in dialogue, with phrases such as “I am frightened,” “love me,” and “kiss me” gaining force through insistence. Exclamation marks, dashes, pauses, and broken questions preserve the breathless rhythms of spoken testimony.

Dialogue carries much of the plot and characterization. The early exchanges are abrupt and defensive, while the ghost’s later speeches become extended, pleading, and rhetorically elaborate. His repeated self-identification as “the man who was frightened to death” functions as both an uncanny refrain and a literalized paradox. His language gradually shifts his role from threatening stranger to suffering victim. The narrator’s responses change accordingly: suspicion becomes terror, terror becomes pity, and pity becomes active love. This emotional reversal is the central structural movement of the story.

The pacing follows a carefully staged escalation. The mundane arrival at the lodging house gives way to the first shock of mistaken identity, then to pursuit through the dark building, apparent entrapment in another room, metaphysical revelation, and finally intimate contact with the ghost. The chase sequence accelerates through forceful verbs such as “dashed,” “tearing,” “banging,” “rushed,” “slammed,” and “flung.” Once the narrator confronts the apparition, the pace slows so that the ghost can explain his condition and persuade her to act. The climax is not an attack but an embrace and repeated kiss, transforming Gothic menace into spiritual release. A calm aftermath then replaces panic with wonder before the final explanatory coda supplies corroboration.

Atmosphere is built through darkness, emptiness, silence, and sensory uncertainty rather than graphic horror. The unfurnished rooms, dusty stairs, hidden stars, faint street lamps, circling wind, and unused attics create an austere urban Gothic setting. The house is located in central London rather than in an ancient castle or remote ruin, bringing supernatural dread into an ordinary modern environment. Sensation is described intensely and often synesthetically: a voice seems to touch the narrator’s boots or sound inside her; fear becomes a hot fluid; psychic dread travels down the spine like a knife. Contrasts between cold and heat, darkness and radiance, solidity and intangibility culminate in the ghost’s disappearance, experienced simultaneously as cool wind, burning fire, and “flaming sweetness.”

The story’s supernatural effect depends heavily on ambiguity. For much of the encounter, the narrator cannot decide whether the stranger is a ghost, a rogue, or a madman. Even after accepting his account, she struggles to distinguish an external transformation from a change within herself. The ending then validates the apparition without explaining its mechanism. Sir Henry reveals that the false story of a murdered woman was deliberately planted, whereas the true history closely matches the ghost’s confession. This functions as a controlled-evidence twist: because the narrator did not know the real history, imagination alone cannot easily account for what she saw. At the same time, she withholds the full truth from her uncle, preserving the experience as private and resisting complete rationalization.

Irony operates throughout. The narrator condemns irrelevant detail yet repeatedly notices precisely the “unessentials” she promised to omit. Those details are not merely decorative; they reveal how consciousness behaves under terror and make the extraordinary event persuasive. Her attempt to prove modern courage by spending the night in a haunted house initially exposes her vulnerability, but her deepest courage ultimately consists not in suppressing fear but in overcoming it through compassion. The ghost, introduced in the conventional position of the object that terrifies, is himself the being most afflicted by fear. Likewise, the investigators who seek evidence of haunting perpetuate the haunting through their curiosity and alarm.

Thematically, the story treats emotion as an active spiritual force. Fear, hatred, and remorse imprison the ghost, while sympathy, tenderness, and self-forgetting love release him. Haunting is therefore presented less as a punishment imposed from outside than as a psychological state prolonged by repetition and reinforced by others. The narrator succeeds because she abandons the detached curiosity of the psychical researcher and enters into genuine human relation with the apparition. Her kiss has sacramental overtones: physical intimacy becomes an act of redemption, and the ghost’s emerging radiance suggests transfiguration.

Gender is integral to this redemptive pattern. The narrator’s independence is treated with both admiration and period-specific irony, and the male listeners remain a skeptical audience whom she repeatedly challenges. The ghost appeals explicitly to a supposedly abundant but socially unused feminine capacity for love. Although this draws on conventional ideas of female compassion, the narrator is not passive: she investigates, judges, chooses, acts, and controls the later disclosure of her experience. Her bravery is emotional and ethical as well as physical.

Overall, the style combines urban Gothic suspense, fin-de-siècle spiritualism, psychological realism, and philosophical romance. Its chief stylistic signature is the movement from concrete fear to metaphysical compassion, achieved through an intimate speaking voice, accumulating sensory detail, dramatic dialogue, strategic repetition, and a final corroborative twist. The supernatural is made convincing not through exhaustive explanation but through the narrator’s embodied reactions and the emotional coherence of the ghost’s release.

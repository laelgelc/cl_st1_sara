The story uses an elevated, archaic mode of mythic cosmic horror. Its prose resembles a translated legend, sacred chronicle, or oral folktale rather than modern realist narration. Antiquated constructions—“hath,” “whither,” “scarce,” “naught,” “whilst,” “betaken themselves”—combine with ceremonial repetitions and declarative statements to give the narrative an ancient, authoritative tone. Invented place names, forbidden manuscripts, ancestral titles, and half-explained customs create the impression of a much larger body of lore without interrupting the tale to systematise it.

The narrator is omniscient, impersonal, and culturally embedded in the imagined world. Events are often introduced through communal tradition—“it is said,” “the villagers tell,” “the people fear”—so hearsay and legend carry nearly the same authority as witnessed fact. Characters are viewed from a distance and defined by emblematic traits: Barzai is learned, proud, and fearless; Atal is young, cautious, and receptive to awe. Psychological complexity is secondary to their mythic roles. Barzai embodies intellectual arrogance, while Atal functions as the surviving witness whose terror confirms the limits of human knowledge.

Sentences are generally long, cumulative, and rhythmically formal. They often join clauses with semicolons, conjunctions, and parallel phrases, allowing description and suspense to gather gradually. Repetition is a central device: gods are repeatedly associated with peaks, clouds, moonlight, memory, and secrecy, while the ascent is marked by phrases such as “higher and higher” and “up and up.” This recursive phrasing gives the prose an incantatory quality and makes the journey feel both physically relentless and ritually predetermined. Shorter declarations appear near crucial revelations, giving them the force of pronouncements.

The diction is ornate and strongly atmospheric. Adjectives such as “mournful,” “spectral,” “hideous,” “cyclopean,” “loathsome,” and “inconceivable” do more than describe objects; they prescribe an emotional response of dread and sublime awe. Natural features are personified or spiritualised: mists are the memories of gods, mountains resemble temples or statues, clouds become ships, and winds carry divine sighs. The landscape is therefore not a passive setting but a sacred, animate realm whose physical laws can yield to supernatural ones.

The imagery moves from remote beauty to vertiginous terror. Early scenes emphasise pale vapours, white peaks, cold moonlight, and melancholy divine nostalgia. During the climb, the imagery becomes harsher and less stable: black chasms, mute granite, bleak ice pinnacles, failing light, endless slopes, and a sky that seems to exert an upward pull. Spatial inversion is especially important. The final horror is not merely falling from a mountain but “falling into the sky,” a paradox that signals the collapse of ordinary orientation and human reason.

The story’s structure is that of a cautionary legend. It begins with broad cosmology, narrows to local history, introduces a transgressive seeker, and then follows a steadily escalating ascent toward forbidden revelation. The opening supplies the prohibition before the protagonist violates it, making the outcome feel inevitable. Suspense develops slowly through warnings, ominous lore, difficult terrain, gathering clouds, and delayed divine sounds. At the climax, narrative distance contracts as Barzai’s shouted speeches report an unseen revelation in real time. His language progresses from triumph to uncertainty to fragmented panic, dramatising the destruction of his confidence.

Dialogue is sparse and almost entirely reserved for the climax. Barzai’s cries are theatrical, repetitive, and exclamatory, with ellipses marking broken perception. He begins by naming himself as prophet and master of divine secrets, then discovers that the familiar gods are themselves weak beings protected by incomprehensibly greater powers. Because Atal cannot directly see what Barzai sees, the horror remains partly indirect. Sound—shouting, shrieking, laughter, thunder—conveys the revelation more strongly than visual description, preserving mystery while intensifying dread.

The governing tonal movement is from melancholy wonder to cosmic humiliation. The earth’s gods initially seem supreme, secretive, and dangerous, yet they are ultimately revealed as “feeble” before the other gods of the outer abysses. This nested hierarchy is fundamental to the cosmic-horror effect: every apparent summit conceals a greater height, and every body of knowledge is dwarfed by an older, inaccessible order. Human scholarship offers no protection. Indeed, Barzai’s learning feeds the pride that destroys him.

The ending returns to the detached voice of legend and communal memory. Material evidence—a colossal symbol carved into the summit—confirms that something occurred but does not explain it. Barzai disappears, Atal refuses to pray for him, and local fear becomes ritualised across generations. The final image circles back to the gods dancing nostalgically above the mists, restoring the opening atmosphere while altering its meaning. What first seemed like divine sternness now appears to be the caution of lesser beings sheltering beneath powers even they fear.

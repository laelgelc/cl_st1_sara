The story uses an intimate first-person retrospective voice, combining the authority of an adult observer with the tenderness and occasional self-reproach of a father. The narrator is educated, reflective, and gently ironic, frequently revealing his character through digressive commentary. His domestic perspective keeps the narrative personal even when it touches on cultural difference, colonial politics, imprisonment, migration, and poverty. The tone begins as affectionate comedy, gradually acquires melancholy, and culminates in restrained sentiment rather than overt melodrama.

The diction has a formal, early twentieth-century literary quality. Expressions such as “without demur,” “impending disaster,” “highly improbable,” and “the accustomed work in the accustomed place” create an elevated narrative register, while period terms and culturally specific references—“eight-anna bit,” “Puja Holidays,” “Bhairavi,” and “father-in-law’s house”—locate the story in colonial Calcutta. The language sometimes reflects an older ethnographic vocabulary, describing Rahmun through markers such as his turban, clothing, accent, physique, and mountain origins. These outward distinctions are ultimately counterbalanced by the story’s insistence on a shared emotional identity: both men are fathers.

Sentence structure alternates between brisk, simple statements and long, carefully balanced sentences containing subordinate clauses, parenthetical observations, and accumulative detail. Short sentences often mark transitions or emotional realizations: “This was their first meeting”; “Time passed away”; “He also was a father.” Longer sentences are used for recollection, imaginative travel, domestic bustle, and emotional expansion. Exclamation marks, rhetorical questions, dashes, semicolons, and occasional fragments reproduce the narrator’s changing thought process. The dash is especially important, often interrupting a thought at the point where emotion exceeds explanation: “I could see—”; “while I was—. But no.”

Dialogue is frequent, direct, and strongly characterising. Mini’s speech is rapid, inquisitive, repetitive, and associative, moving abruptly from language to weather to family relationships. Her recurring cry of “O Cabuliwallah! Cabuliwallah!” gives the story an oral, almost refrain-like quality. Rahmun’s exchanges with her depend on repeated jokes, especially the “elephant” in his bag and the double meaning of “father-in-law’s house.” These comic formulas later return in altered circumstances, turning humour into pathos. Dialogue therefore functions not merely as realism but as a structural device: repetition measures the passage of time and demonstrates what has been lost.

The narrative is broadly chronological but selective. It opens with a vivid domestic scene, condenses the growth of Mini and Rahmun’s routine visits, pauses for reflective digressions, dramatizes the arrest, then leaps across the prison years to the wedding day. This temporal compression makes childhood appear fleeting. Pacing is lively in the early scenes, especially where Mini’s chatter drives the action, but becomes slower and more contemplative near the conclusion. The final encounter unfolds through hesitation, recognition, silence, gestures, and objects rather than through extensive explanation.

Humour is produced through affectionate exaggeration, incongruity, and narrative understatement. Mini has supposedly “not wasted a minute in silence”; her mother imagines dangers ranging from thieves and tigers to malaria and caterpillars; the narrator worries that Rahmun’s arrival will prevent the completion of his seventeenth chapter. The contrast between the narrator’s melodramatic fictional lovers and the ordinary interruption outside his window lightly mocks literary romance. This comedy also makes the later emotional shift more effective, since the same jokes and relationships return under conditions of age, imprisonment, and separation.

Imagery moves between enclosed domestic spaces and imagined expanses. The study, window, courtyard, lane, and inner apartment define the narrator’s circumscribed life, while Rahmun evokes mountains, defiles, forests, camel caravans, weapons, and distant Afghanistan. Seasonal imagery provides an emotional framework. Autumn first suggests travel and heroic adventure, then returns at Mini’s wedding as a season of beauty, departure, and loss. Sunlight is repeatedly used to soften sordid surroundings and to suggest moral illumination: the wedding morning’s golden radiance is ultimately surpassed by the narrator’s compassionate act.

The story relies on symbolic objects with strong emotional economy. Rahmun’s bag initially represents Mini’s childish fear and later becomes part of their private joke. Nuts, raisins, and grapes shift from merchandise or bribery into tokens of paternal affection. His fettered hands contrast with the inked impression of his daughter’s hand: one signifies punishment and confinement, the other enduring attachment across distance. The handprint is the central emotional emblem because it collapses differences of nationality, class, and circumstance into a universally recognisable bond.

Characterisation is economical and relational. Mini is defined by speech, curiosity, laughter, and finally bridal silence. Rahmun is first perceived as physically imposing and culturally foreign, then humanised through patience, humour, vulnerability, and fatherhood. The narrator develops from amused observer to morally awakened participant. Mini’s mother is drawn more broadly, almost caricaturally, through comic fearfulness, but her suspicions provide social tension and preserve a sense of possible danger around the stranger.

The dominant stylistic pattern is contrast: child and adult, smallness and physical enormity, domestic confinement and distant wilderness, comedy and grief, memory and change, commercial exchange and selfless giving. Repetition with variation is equally central. The wedding-day question about the “father-in-law’s house” repeats an old joke, but Mini’s embarrassment and Rahmun’s shock reveal that time has made the former relationship unrecoverable. The ending avoids restoring the past; instead, it converts recognition of loss into generosity. Its final emotional effect is humane, elegiac, and quietly universalising.

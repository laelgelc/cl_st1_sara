The story is written as a comic first-person oral monologue in a strongly phonetic rural Southern dialect. The narrator addresses an unseen listener as “sir,” periodically answering implied questions and wandering away from his main subject before correcting himself—“But I set out to tell you…” This creates the impression of spontaneous conversation rather than composed narration. Apostrophes, altered spellings and eye-dialect forms such as “thet,” “ez,” “thess,” “hisself,” “’lowed,” “befo’,” and “’Piscopal” reproduce pronunciation and establish social setting, while recurring formulas—“Well, sir,” “Of co’se,” “I reckon,” and “says I”—give the voice a distinctive rhythm. The prose uses long, loosely coordinated sentences filled with repetitions, digressions and afterthoughts, closely imitating the associative movement of speech.

The narrative voice is affectionate, earnest and unintentionally comic. Deuteronomy Jones presents himself as a plain, practical, religiously tolerant father, yet his logic is frequently naïve, superstitious or comically self-contradictory. He treats vaccination and baptism as parallel procedures that may or may not “take,” accepts dubious folk beliefs while claiming not to believe in them, and defends indulgence of Sonny even as he insists that he does not overindulge the child. The humour depends less on explicit jokes than on deadpan delivery: absurd events are narrated with complete seriousness, including the vaccination of the household animals, the parents’ rebaptism, and Sonny’s baptism by rain while perched on a bean arbor. Understatement and solemn phrasing intensify the incongruity.

Characterisation is achieved primarily through speech and anecdote. The narrator’s diction reveals his warmth, credulity, pride and devotion, while Sonny emerges through repeated patterns of action: he resists physical control, negotiates shrewdly, orders others to undergo whatever is intended for him, and retreats to inaccessible places. The adults continually interpret his obstinacy as evidence of preference, principle or sweetness, creating gentle dramatic irony. The rector is initially represented through pompous ecclesiastical language but becomes humane and flexible once confronted with the family’s predicament. Dialogue frequently sets formal church language against the narrator’s vernacular, producing both social contrast and comedy.

Structurally, the story is cumulative and digressive. It begins with the surprising result—the family has become Episcopalian—then works backward through Sonny’s religious preferences, the vaccination episode and the splinter crisis before reaching the climactic outdoor baptism. The vaccination sequence functions as a comic rehearsal for the christening: in both cases Sonny refuses the rite, compels others to receive it first and remains beyond adult authority. This parallel prepares the final punch line, when he asks what will happen if his christening “don’t take.” Episodes escalate through repetition, with each new concession becoming more extravagant, but the casual storytelling voice makes the escalation feel natural.

The imagery is domestic, rural and concrete. The story draws on farms, porches, animals, creeks, weather, bean arbors, apples and children’s games rather than abstract description. Similes are colloquial and vivid: the narrator loses his backbone “like” a feather bolster, Sonny runs like “zig-zag lightnin’,” and the swollen toes are compared through the nursery rhyme of the little pigs. Formal religious vocabulary—“rites,” “sacrament,” “proxies,” “spiritual benefits”—is repeatedly translated into homely terms, misunderstood or adapted to immediate practical needs. Capitalisation and typographic emphasis mark spoken stress and sharpen comic turns, especially in phrases such as “HE COME DOWN” and “S’POS’IN’ MINE DON’T TAKE.”

The tone combines farce, satire and tenderness. Denominational customs and clerical dignity are gently mocked, but the story is not hostile to religion; practical compassion ultimately matters more than ritual correctness. Likewise, the narrator’s ignorance is a source of humour without erasing his emotional intelligence or parental love. Anxiety over illness and death gives the comedy real stakes, while the closing domestic image of the parents drying Sonny’s hair restores warmth after the absurd ceremony. The overall style is therefore best described as vernacular regional humour shaped by oral storytelling, dramatic irony, escalating anecdotal comedy and an affectionate portrayal of family life.

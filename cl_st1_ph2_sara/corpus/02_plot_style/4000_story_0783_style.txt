The story’s style blends late-Victorian adventure romance, wilderness naturalism, supernatural fantasy, and lyrical tragedy. Its narrative begins with the physical brutality of a frontier tale—a failed lynching, an armed escape, and a perilous canoe journey—but gradually passes into an Edenic island romance and finally into a visionary encounter with death. The movement from grim realism to dreamlike myth is central to the story’s effect. Violence, survival, love, fatherhood, guilt, and mortality are treated not merely as plot elements but as stages in a symbolic passage from fear to happiness, then from happiness to grief.

The narrative is divided into numbered sections, each marking a distinct tonal and dramatic phase. The opening section is fast, harsh, and crowded with action; the middle sections slow into elaborate descriptions of sea, forest, wildlife, and domestic intimacy; the final section compresses time and moves toward hallucination and supernatural revelation. Epigraphs from Fitzgerald’s rendering of Omar Khayyam and from Kipling establish the governing themes of irreversible fate, elemental freedom, and heterosexual union. The ending returns to these themes in an apocalyptic lyrical coda, enlarging Kent’s personal fate into a meditation on the transience of nature, love, and death itself.

The narrator uses third-person omniscience, generally staying close to Kent’s perceptions while retaining the authority to supply legends, geographical lore, symbolic commentary, and mythic history. Psychological states are often externalized rather than clinically explained. Kent’s terror becomes “the fear of fear,” something that grips or drags at his throat and must be physically shaken off. Later, grief is imagined as a hidden presence in the forest and death as a watcher across the sea. This personification gives emotions the force of antagonists and turns the landscape into an extension of consciousness.

The prose alternates sharply between muscular action writing and ornate, highly coloured description. In the lynching and escape scenes, sentences are driven by active verbs, abrupt clauses, repetition, and accumulative motion: bodies sprawl, twist, thrash, plunge, and wallow. Parataxis and semicolon-linked actions produce speed and breathlessness. In the island passages, the syntax becomes more expansive and musical, with balanced phrases, flowing participles, and sensuous catalogues. The canoe is “bronzed,” “jewelled,” and trailed by seaweed in a “diamond-flashing wake”; waves resemble opals, the sea pearl and sapphire, and dawn rosy flame. This jewelled palette is characteristic of romantic and decadent prose, transforming natural scenery into an almost enchanted visual surface.

Imagery is exceptionally dense and depends on colour, light, texture, and movement. White, crimson, silver, sapphire, opal, ebony, gold, and rose recur throughout. The natural world is rendered through tactile comparisons—velvet, silk, polished stone, foam, fur, and glass—as well as through painterly changes in light. Similes often link wilderness phenomena to precious materials or ritual objects, while darker comparisons draw on butchery, torture, skulls, shrouds, and graves. The beauty of the island is therefore never wholly innocent: mortality remains embedded within the luminous scenery.

Sound patterning is equally prominent. Alliteration, internal rhyme, repeated consonants, and echoing word sequences give the prose a quasi-poetic cadence. Phrases such as “the polished paddle swung and dipped, and swept and swung and dipped again” imitate repeated physical movement. Other passages employ incantatory repetition—“turning, turning,” “lapping, lapping,” “whispering, listening, and whispering”—to suspend ordinary time. Onomatopoeic and imitative sounds, including “splash,” “pit-a-pat,” bird cries, cracking branches, and the clash of surf, make the environment acoustically vivid. Refrains such as “Ihó! Inâh!” and “The earth alone lasts” increasingly function like ritual responses.

Dialogue in the mainland scenes is terse, colloquial, and heavily marked by eye dialect. Forms such as “git,” “fur,” “kin,” “yer,” and “ain’t” characterize the gum workers as rough, provincial, and socially distinct from the narrator’s elevated voice. Their speech is fragmented, repetitive, and often darkly comic, especially when lynching, burial, superstition, and the division of Kent’s possessions are discussed with practical bluntness. This grim humour intensifies rather than relieves the brutality. By contrast, dialogue on the island is sparse, tender, and linguistically estranged: Kent and the girl initially cannot understand each other, so voice itself becomes “wordless melody.” Communication proceeds through gesture, repetition, shared labour, and an idealized language of endearment.

The story repeatedly contrasts ugliness and beauty. The gum camp is associated with mould, blood, sweat, musty bedding, greasy cards, smoke, and rotten leaves; the island with clear water, blossoms, birds, silk, moonlight, and perfumed nights. Yet the contrast is unstable. Kent brings violence, guilt, and fear into the island paradise, while the island’s beauty proves spectral and impermanent. The ideal refuge becomes a psychic or supernatural space in which he can briefly experience innocence, love, and fatherhood before the unresolved past returns in the person of Tully and the rope.

Nature is intensely animated. Birds, deer, mist, waves, stars, and trees appear to act, speak, remember, or participate in human destiny. Animals are frequently named and individualized, creating the impression of a mythic community in which human beings are only one form of life among many. The child’s ability to communicate with animals deepens this animistic mode and marks him as supernatural. Seasonal and diurnal cycles structure the island episodes, while repeated dawns, twilights, migrations, and tides suggest a cosmic rhythm indifferent to individual lives.

The island material draws on stylized Indigenous vocabulary, names, songs, kinship formulas, and cosmological motifs. These elements are presented through a distinctly period-specific romantic and primitivist lens: the unnamed girl is idealized as innocent, instinctive, sexually unashamed, and spiritually close to nature. Her characterization is more symbolic than psychologically individualized, and her culture is filtered through exoticized language and mythic imagery rather than represented with ethnographic realism. The effect is central to the story’s dreamlike atmosphere but also reflects colonial-era conventions in which Indigenous identity becomes a vehicle for fantasies of purity, timelessness, and escape from modern society.

Several motifs bind the sections together. The rope mark around Kent’s neck anticipates the noose at the end. The mist first appears as a threshold protecting the island, then becomes the medium through which the island, the woman, and the child dissolve. White imagery shifts from gulls, foam, and beaches to the child’s unnatural shadow and finally to deathly pallor. The canoe signifies escape but also the inevitable return imagined by Tully. Fire changes from a desperate survival need into a domestic miracle and then into torchlight revealing the dead man’s face. The “fear of fear” gives way to genuine fear only when Kent acquires something he can lose; love therefore becomes the “key” that opens grief.

The dominant tone is elevated, fatalistic, and elegiac, though it contains intervals of brutality, irony, erotic wonder, and pastoral serenity. Sentiment is expressed without restraint: beauty is luxuriant, love sacred, terror bodily, and grief cosmic. The story favours archetypal roles—the fugitive, the avenger, the innocent maiden, the miraculous child, the returning dead—over subtle social realism. Its final effect comes from the collision of these modes: frontier violence is transformed into legend, paradise into hallucination, and an individual execution into a myth about the impermanence of all things.

The story adopts a richly descriptive, historically inflected prose style that combines sensuous Mediterranean atmosphere, psychological observation, philosophical allegory, and mordant satire. Its diction is formal and literary, with occasional archaic or period-coloured terms such as “galley,” “bier,” “gorgeret,” “forepined,” and “sternsheets.” This elevated vocabulary is offset by the sailors’ coarse, colloquial speech, full of curses, practical concerns, and bawdy humour. The resulting contrast between lofty abstraction and bodily vulgarity is central to the story’s voice.

The narration is third-person and broadly omniscient, moving fluidly between panoramic description, communal gossip, and the private thoughts of individual characters. It first establishes the ship and sea from a detached, almost painterly perspective, then enters the consciousness of the young Spaniard and his mistress, exposing their vanity, cruelty, fear, desire, and self-deception. Free indirect discourse is used extensively: the characters’ assumptions merge with the narrator’s language without always being explicitly introduced. This creates an ironic distance, since their moral judgments are often contradicted by their behaviour. The woman, for example, regards her possessive and casually cruel lover as “a good man,” while the nobleman imagines himself refined even as he treats her like an animal or object.

Sentences tend to be long, rhythmical, and elaborately balanced. Descriptive passages accumulate clauses, repetitions, and parallel structures to slow narrative time and reproduce the oppressive stillness of the heat. Phrases such as “very slowly” recur for hypnotic effect, while semicolons and dashes allow observations to unfold through qualification and afterthought. At moments of action or comic reversal, the syntax becomes shorter and dialogue-driven. The prose therefore alternates between languorous suspension and sudden disturbance: calm sea and clamour, sleep and violence, erotic reverie and mortality.

Imagery is intensely visual, tactile, and corporeal. Light gives the landscape a “hard and merciless definition”; the town appears enamelled and flattened like a painting; stars leave tracks of fire on the water. Heat is rendered through blood, sweat, dust, fire, and melting flesh. Against this harsh physicality stand imagined scenes of cool pastures, streams, winds, cypresses, and chestnut groves. These contrasts are not merely decorative: coolness represents idealized pleasure and escape, while heat exposes the body’s discomfort, decay, and vulnerability.

The dominant figurative system is one of corruption and transformation. Cancer, ulcers, worms, fever, acids, rotting flesh, and foul smells repeatedly undermine ideals of beauty, sanctity, and human dignity. These images are paired with the language of alchemy: sulphur, salt, mercury, lead, brass, iron, and gold. Gold becomes a symbol of spiritual integrity and incorruptible love, while base metals represent the unstable mixture of lust, anger, hatred, and false belief. The imagery expands from one diseased body to a diagnosis of humanity itself, turning physical corruption into a metaphysical and social condition.

The tone is persistently ironic. Religious devotion is treated with seriousness but is surrounded by venality, vanity, lust, superstition, and commerce. The captain prepares a grand bier for a martyr who turns out not to be dead, then worries about which city will value the corpse more. His piety is sincere in its way, yet inseparable from prestige and practical advantage. The climactic philosophical discourse on spiritual purity is likewise framed by the creaking labour of chained slaves and the sounds of blows from below deck. Such juxtapositions prevent the story from settling into simple reverence: noble doctrines coexist with cruelty that no one present seriously questions.

Dialogue strongly differentiates character. The nobleman speaks tersely and languidly, using indifference as a sign of rank. The mistress’s speech is direct, anxious, and submissive. The captain is expansive, digressive, theatrical, and comic; his language shifts between rehearsed pious rhetoric and earthy seafaring idiom. His storytelling is full of qualifications, interruptions, proverbial comparisons, and self-corrections, producing an oral, anecdotal texture. The dying holy man speaks in elevated, patterned, sermon-like periods, rich in extended metaphors and antitheses. These distinct registers create both social hierarchy and tonal variety.

Structurally, the story develops through a sequence of ironic reversals. Apparent calm conceals slavery and aggression; erotic leisure gives way to news of religious violence; a supposedly dead martyr is recovered alive; a prepared funeral bier becomes a deathbed; and spiritual revelation is followed by logistical concern over the profitable or prestigious destination of the relic. The captain’s embedded tale provides the moral and symbolic centre, linking sexual desire, bodily disease, conversion, and alchemy. The later speech retells and interprets that anecdote from the holy man’s own perspective, transforming a grotesque erotic revelation into a lifelong spiritual mission.

The pacing is deliberately unhurried. Much of the plot consists of waiting, sleeping, talking, remembering, and contemplating. Major events occur offstage—the stoning, the rescue, and much of the saint’s life—and reach the reader through reports or retrospective narration. This indirectness places emphasis not on adventure but on interpretation: how violence becomes martyrdom, how disgust becomes conversion, and how sanctity becomes a potential civic possession.

Overall, the style is sensuous yet cerebral, grotesque yet elegant, and comic without being merely frivolous. It uses historical distance to examine perennial tensions between flesh and spirit, beauty and decay, faith and self-interest, idealism and cruelty. Its characteristic effect comes from placing lyrical description, bawdy anecdote, metaphysical speculation, and practical absurdity side by side, allowing each to expose the limitations of the others.

The story adopts a heightened Gothic mode, combining aristocratic decadence, hereditary hatred, supernatural fatalism, and psychological horror. Its atmosphere is consistently oppressive and portentous: ancient castles, faded tapestries, forests, storms, fire, ancestral prophecies, and a demonic horse create a world in which landscape and architecture seem to participate in the characters’ doom. Horror develops less through explicit violence than through ominous correspondences—the horse in the tapestry, the animal emerging from the burning stables, the rider’s shadow matching the ancestral murderer, and the final cloud of smoke taking equine form. Repeated images of flames, redness, gigantic scale, and distorted human expression bind the supernatural elements together.

The narrator is erudite, intrusive, and deliberately equivocal. Rather than simply recounting events, the voice pauses to discuss superstition, incredulity, social behavior, and the unreliability of interpretation. It cites French authorities, invokes metempsychosis, alludes to Herod and Caligula, and comments ironically on the characters’ explanations. This creates an antiquarian and intellectual frame around the sensational plot. The narrator often withholds certainty through phrases such as “it is said,” “seemed,” “perhaps,” and “if it implied anything,” allowing supernatural and psychological readings to coexist even as the story’s final design strongly suggests reincarnation or spectral revenge.

The prose is ornate, formal, and Latinate. Long periodic sentences accumulate subordinate clauses, qualifications, parallel constructions, and parenthetical interruptions before reaching their principal point. Dashes are especially prominent, functioning as pauses, emphatic pivots, and containers for explanatory asides. Semicolons, colons, and carefully balanced clauses give the narration a declamatory rhythm. At moments of crisis, however, the syntax contracts into sharper units—“One instant … another”—so that the climax acquires speed and violence. Repetition and incremental phrasing are used for intensification, as in the horse becoming “more absorbing,” “more impossible,” and increasingly beyond rational control.

The diction is elevated and archaic, favoring words such as “portentous,” “preternatural,” “malignity,” “equivocal,” “phlegmatic,” “estray,” and “curvetted.” Adjectives frequently appear in clusters, enlarging objects into grotesque or sublime spectacles: the horse is enormous, fiery, colossal, unnatural, convulsive, and demon-like. Abstract nouns—“fatality,” “incredulity,” “enmity,” “audacity,” “animosity,” and “fervor”—lend the narrative a philosophical gravity. French quotations, noble titles, elaborate names, and references to feudal hierarchy reinforce its cosmopolitan, pseudo-historical character.

Characterization is stylized rather than intimate. Frederick is presented as a type of the decadent young tyrant: precocious, cruel, proud, isolated, and fascinated by forces that ultimately master him. Count Berlifitzing is likewise defined by a few dominant traits, particularly hatred and devotion to horses. Interior psychology appears mainly through external signs—fixed gazes, pallor, shudders, distorted expressions, compulsive riding—rather than sustained introspection. The horse receives comparable or greater psychological force through its “human-looking” eyes, intelligence, rage, and apparent triumph, blurring the boundary between animal, revenant, and avenging will.

Dialogue is sparse, formal, and theatrically pointed. Exchanges about the horse and the Count’s death use irony, understatement, and strategic pauses. Frederick’s calm “Shocking!” after displaying satisfaction exemplifies the story’s taste for sinister discrepancy between spoken decorum and inward malignity. Stretched typography in “I-n-d-e-e-d-!” and recurring exclamations add melodramatic emphasis, while the servants’ reports provide circumstantial evidence without fully resolving the mystery.

The plot is tightly governed by foreshadowing and symbolic symmetry. The opening doctrine of transmigration anticipates the possible return of Berlifitzing in equine form; the prophecy predicts the reversal of rider and horse; the first stable fire is answered by the destruction of Metzengerstein’s palace; and Frederick’s apparent triumph over his rival becomes his own annihilation. The central irony is that the proud rider who believes he can tame even a devil gradually becomes the creature’s captive. The ending converts this pattern into a single emblematic image, with the horse-shaped smoke functioning as supernatural confirmation, visual signature, and final seal of fatality.

The pacing moves from discursive exposition through concentrated uncanny episodes to a rapid apocalyptic conclusion. Early passages establish genealogy, property, superstition, and rivalry at length; the tapestry scene then serves as the narrative hinge, after which repetition of the Baron’s rides creates a sense of obsession and inevitability. The climactic return compresses action into vivid auditory and visual impressions—clattering hoofs, roaring flames, shrieking winds, a solitary human cry—before ending in sudden calm. Overall, the style is ornate, ironic, symbolically patterned, and intensely atmospheric, with rational commentary continually shadowed by the certainty of supernatural doom.

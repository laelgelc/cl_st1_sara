The story adopts the manner of a mock-medieval chronicle: an ostensibly historical account filtered through a knowing, intrusive narrator who treats folklore, scandal, and sexual farce with the solemnity of official history. Its opening appeal to an “old chronicler” gives the narrative a spurious documentary pedigree, while references to Rouen, Duke Richard, King Louis XI, Plato, biblical figures, legal proceedings, heraldry, and unnamed “writers of the period” create an elaborate façade of authenticity. This antiquarian framing is deliberately unstable, however, because scholarly authority is repeatedly invoked to dignify absurd, indecent, or transparently invented material.

The narrative voice is expansive, amused, and morally unreliable. It habitually calls the protagonist “the good man,” “this wise man,” or “the poor old fellow,” even when recounting idleness, theft, fraud, and sexual violence. This discrepancy between the narrator’s indulgent language and the actual conduct described is the central source of irony. The narrator behaves less like an impartial historian than like a convivial storyteller addressing an audience already disposed to enjoy roguery. He comments freely, anticipates objections, supplies mock explanations, cites authorities, and ends by extracting a knowingly perverse moral. Direct addresses and phrases such as “you may be sure” or “From this you can obtain” strengthen the sense of an oral raconteur performing for listeners.

The diction mixes archaic, courtly, legal, theological, and proverbial registers with earthy colloquialism. Terms such as “provost,” “varlets,” “indulgences,” “canons,” “Paschal feast,” and “letters patent” evoke a historical romance, while idioms such as “ducks and drakes,” “up hill and down dale,” and “made one’s mouth water” keep the prose energetic and popular. Euphemism is especially prominent in the treatment of sex: bodies and acts are rendered through circumlocutions such as “counting the joists,” “two hemispheres,” “twin charms,” “pretty delta,” “implements of happiness,” and the censored family name “Bonne-C------.” These evasions do not suppress obscenity so much as heighten it through comic ingenuity. Classical and biblical allusions perform a similar function, raising bawdy appetite into mock-heroic grandeur.

Sentences are generally long, cumulative, and clause-heavy. They often proceed through semicolons, parenthetical qualifications, lists, and chains of causal explanation, reproducing the leisurely abundance of an old chronicle or translated Renaissance tale. The syntax likes to delay the comic point while adding examples and digressions. Repetition and parallelism give many passages an aphoristic cadence: rich and poor, labour and idleness, bodily health and luxury, hats and bonnets, priest and hangman are arranged in comic oppositions. The prose also favours enumerations—possessions, foods, social ceremonies, authorities, bodily effects—which create a sense of excess appropriate to its Rabelaisian humour.

Characterisation is broad, emblematic, and anecdotal rather than psychologically subtle. Tryballot is established through a sequence of comic biographical episodes that all illustrate the same governing traits: improvidence, idleness, appetite, adaptability, and cheerful shamelessness. His childhood fascination with thieving birds becomes a recurring motif and a mock-philosophical origin story for his later behaviour. Blackbirds, jays, and sparrows function simultaneously as tutors, analogues, and excuses. His father, the peasants, the cowherdess, the duke, the guards, and the noblewoman are sketched mainly through their role in the joke. Social types matter more than individual interiority.

The structure is episodic and digressive. A lengthy pseudo-biography precedes the principal incident, establishing Tryballot’s habits and philosophy before the “celebrated trial of Rouen.” The central action then follows the pattern of a fabliau: transgression, capture, legal examination, an ingenious or absurd defence, a conditional sentence, a public test, reversal, reward, marriage, and an etiological account of a family name. The final paragraph adds a mock sermon whose supposed lesson—that austere poverty preserves virility better than courtly luxury—deliberately distorts the moral implications of the events. Pacing accelerates once the trial begins, with dialogue, public spectacle, and the test at the gallows supplying the climax.

Humour arises from incongruity, exaggeration, inversion, and false logic. Begging is treated as an honourable profession with “statutes and canons”; wastefulness becomes philosophical detachment; sexual appetite becomes an “illustrious virtue”; execution becomes an experiment; and involuntary bodily response becomes legally exculpatory proof. The protagonist’s arguments are comic syllogisms in which biblical precedent, poverty, age, and opportunity are assembled into self-serving justification. Hyperbole is constant: everyone knows the beggar, his popularity is provincial, the whole town attends the execution, a monument is erected, and a noble lineage descends from the affair. The result is a grotesque enlargement of private misconduct into civic legend.

Dialogue is comparatively sparse but strategically placed. Most speech takes the form of compact sayings, legal testimony, or comic self-defence. Tryballot’s voice mirrors the narrator’s: resourceful, aphoristic, shameless, and adept at turning vice into virtue. The cowherdess’s testimony combines genuine injury with bawdy comic phrasing, while the duke’s decree converts justice into theatrical ordeal. This treatment reflects the tale’s historical fabliau mode, though to a modern reader the sustained comic framing of rape and the coerced marriage are starkly disturbing. The style minimizes the victim’s perspective and redirects sympathy toward the offender, revealing a deliberately transgressive but ethically troubling narrative stance.

The dominant tonal blend is bawdy, irreverent, mock-learned, and celebratory. Beneath the farce lies a carnivalesque reversal of normal hierarchies: the beggar lives like a nobleman, poverty becomes wealth, idleness becomes wisdom, the condemned man triumphs over justice, and bodily vitality defeats official authority. Institutions—law, religion, aristocracy, marriage, genealogy, and historiography—are all absorbed into the joke. The overall effect is that of a richly embellished droll tale: verbose, pseudo-historical, proverb-loving, physically comic, morally inverted, and narrated with exuberant complicity in its rogue hero’s worldview.

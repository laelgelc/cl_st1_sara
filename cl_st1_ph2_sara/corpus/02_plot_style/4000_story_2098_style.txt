The story adopts the form of a reflective historical report that gradually becomes a philosophical and political parable. Its first-person narrator presents himself as an educated participant-observer: a former subordinate supervisor in the construction of the wall, a student of history, and a cautious interpreter of institutions. His voice is sober, conscientious, and apparently rational, yet his reasoning repeatedly leads from practical facts into uncertainty, paradox, conjecture, and myth. He qualifies his claims with phrases such as “perhaps,” “in my view,” and “it is possible,” giving the narration an air of intellectual modesty while also emphasizing how little can be known with confidence.

The prose is formal, analytical, and discursive. Long sentences accumulate clauses, explanations, exceptions, and corrections, often reproducing the movement of a mind testing its own conclusions. Assertions are frequently followed by objections, rhetorical questions, partial answers, and further qualifications. This creates a recursive argumentative rhythm: the narrator introduces a problem, offers a plausible explanation, exposes its inadequacy, and then shifts to a deeper but less verifiable level. Repetition reinforces this process, especially in constructions such as “nothing would have been achieved,” “once again,” and “I need to say it again and again.” The syntax can be expansive and heavily subordinated, but short declarations and exclamations—“Unity! Unity!” or “Why?”—occasionally interrupt the flow with emotional or aphoristic force.

The diction combines administrative precision with metaphysical abstraction. Concrete measurements, ranks, building procedures, examinations, tax lists, orders, and chains of supervision lend the account documentary credibility. Against this bureaucratic vocabulary stand elevated terms such as “leadership,” “empire,” “divine worlds,” “human nature,” and “infinite extent.” Institutions are often described without identifiable occupants: the highest authority is merely “the leadership,” whose office, location, and membership are unknown. This impersonal naming makes power feel both omnipresent and inaccessible. Numerical and geographical details similarly create an impression of exactness while underscoring the incomprehensible scale of the empire.

Structurally, the narrative proceeds by association rather than by conventional plot. It begins with the technical puzzle of piecemeal construction, moves through education and worker morale, considers the Tower of Babel, speculates about the intentions of the leadership, asks whom the wall is meant to repel, and finally turns to the remoteness of the emperor and the peculiar political consciousness of the people. Anecdotes from the narrator’s childhood and village life interrupt the exposition, grounding large abstractions in vivid scenes. Each digression initially appears secondary but becomes central, so that the text continually postpones resolution while enlarging the original question. The wall functions less as the endpoint of the inquiry than as an entry into questions of authority, collective purpose, belief, distance, and social cohesion.

The tone is restrained, earnest, and subtly ironic. The narrator treats implausible explanations with grave seriousness, allowing absurdity to emerge without overt satire. Official logic remains internally coherent even when it culminates in the conclusion that the leadership deliberately desired an impractical method. Devotion and skepticism coexist: the narrator reveres collective labour and imagines the leadership in quasi-divine terms, yet repeatedly reveals the emptiness, remoteness, or unknowability of authority. The resulting irony is unstable rather than comic; neither the narrator nor the reader can fully determine whether institutions are wise, irrational, deceptive, or simply too vast to comprehend.

Imagery converts abstract political relations into physical scale and motion. Human nature becomes “whirling dust”; thought becomes a river that either follows its course or floods its banks; national unity becomes blood circulating through the infinite body of China. The empire is imagined as cloud, sediment, maze, and unreachable centre. The recurring imagery of walls, circles, roads, courtyards, stairs, and enclosed spaces suggests both connection and obstruction. Structures intended to unify or protect also divide, delay, and conceal. The comparison with the Tower of Babel deepens this ambiguity by linking monumental construction with collective aspiration, failed communication, and unstable foundations.

The embedded legend of the imperial messenger is the story’s most concentrated parabolic passage. Its second-person address briefly makes the reader the emperor’s distant subject, waiting for a message that can never arrive. Repetition and escalating spatial obstacles slow the messenger’s progress until movement becomes indistinguishable from stasis. The passage condenses the story’s central pattern: authority issues a message, intermediaries attempt to transmit it, immense systems absorb it, and the subject is left with hope rather than knowledge. The closing image of someone sitting at a window and dreaming of the message gives bureaucratic failure an intimate, melancholy quality.

Characterization is subordinate to social and intellectual types. Labourers, supervisors, priests, officials, villagers, nomads, scholars, emperors, and courtiers are defined mainly by their place within institutional systems. Even the narrator is characterized less through personal relationships than through his habits of study, memory, caution, and interpretation. Crowds and populations often act collectively, while rulers remain abstract or historically confused. This suppression of individualized character contributes to the story’s allegorical atmosphere.

The pacing is deliberately slow and cumulative. Technical exposition and philosophical commentary occupy more space than incident, while anecdotes serve as illustrative evidence rather than dramatic turning points. Suspense arises from inquiry rather than action: the reader follows the narrator’s effort to discover why the wall was built as it was and what the empire means to its subjects. Yet each answer opens another uncertainty. The final refusal to pursue the investigation further preserves this incompleteness and suggests that certain truths are psychologically or politically destabilizing.

Overall, the style is that of a bureaucratic-philosophical parable: lucid in local detail, labyrinthine in total design, solemnly logical, and haunted by irresolvable contradictions. Its distinctive effect comes from treating impossibility as an administrative fact and metaphysical mystery as a matter for careful historical inquiry. The governing tensions are between unity and fragmentation, loyalty and ignorance, practical labour and unknowable purpose, central power and peripheral life, and the human desire for explanation and the limits beyond which explanation becomes dangerous.

The story adopts the style of a nineteenth-century didactic or moral-improvement tale, using a simple financial predicament to advocate thrift, self-command, and personal responsibility. Its tone is earnest, admonitory, and confidently moralistic. Pleasure is evaluated according to its practical and ethical consequences, while debt and habitual indulgence are presented not merely as financial errors but as signs of weakened character. The narrative assumes that reason, arithmetic, and disciplined restraint lead naturally to “order, tranquillity of mind, success, and true enjoyment.”

The piece is overwhelmingly dialogue-driven. It begins in medias res with a rapid exchange of short questions and answers, withholding narrative explanation until the central contrast has been established. This gives the opening the briskness of a staged debate. Thereafter, the conversation becomes a prolonged Socratic interrogation: Hamilton asks Hoffman to identify each expense, converts small daily or weekly sums into annual totals, and lets the accumulating figures expose the latter’s self-deception. Repetition of questions such as “What did it cost?”, “How often?”, and “Anything more?” supplies rhythm and makes the lesson feel methodical and unavoidable. The recurring presentation of calculations also creates a ledger-like structure, with each new item functioning as another piece of evidence.

The narrator uses a clear third-person omniscient perspective, but intervenes sparingly. Narrative passages identify the men, explain their opposing relationships to money, disclose Hoffman’s suspicions and embarrassment, and mark his changing emotional state. The narrator’s judgments are explicit rather than neutral: Hamilton “understood the use of money,” whereas Hoffman “practised the abuse” of it. Characterization is therefore schematic and exemplary. Hamilton is the prudent mentor, calm, rational, observant, and self-denying; Hoffman is the incredulous spendthrift who progresses from defensiveness through astonishment and humiliation to repentance. Neither is psychologically complex, because both primarily embody contrasting habits and moral positions.

The diction is formal, plain, and period-specific. Expressions such as “pray,” “pocket-book,” “six-pence,” “shilling,” “counting-room,” “thraldom,” and “poor Richard’s time” place the story in an older mercantile culture. Elevated moral abstractions—“integrity of purpose,” “disquietude,” “humiliations,” “self-controlling,” and “moral purpose”—alternate with the concrete vocabulary of salaries, boarding costs, cigars, billiards, stage hire, oysters, and confections. This combination allows the story to move continually from ordinary purchases to broad ethical conclusions. Exclamations such as “Impossible!”, “Preposterous,” and “True as I live!” heighten Hoffman’s surprise and lend the exchanges a mildly theatrical quality.

Sentence structure varies according to function. Much of the dialogue consists of clipped, direct sentences that keep the accounting brisk, while Hamilton’s moral explanations expand into balanced, cumulative sentences and parallel constructions. His final speech is especially oratorical, building through lists and repeated clauses: “wise, prudent, just, and self-denying” and “self-poised, self-controlling, self-possessing men.” Aphorisms are prominent, including “By spending less than he receives,” “A leak is a leak,” and “Figures are inexorable.” These concise maxims give Hamilton’s advice the authority of proverbial wisdom. Antithesis also shapes the argument: saving is opposed to wasting, reason to appetite, freedom to thraldom, and lasting benefit to momentary pleasure.

The principal imagery is economic and domestic rather than lyrical. The metaphor of the leaking “flagon” or “butt” represents the cumulative force of tiny expenditures, while money “puffed away” in cigar smoke converts waste into a vivid physical disappearance. Hoffman’s inability to leave the city is framed as imprisonment, and habitual appetite becomes a master whose “thraldom” must be broken. Such metaphors reinforce the story’s central claim that apparently trivial indulgences gradually erode both material independence and moral agency.

Pacing is initially quick, then deliberately repetitive and cumulative. Each expense—cigars, billiards, refreshments, entertainment, jewelry, transport, sweets, drives, and excursions—is isolated, calculated, and added to the total. This slows the narrative action but increases argumentative pressure. Emotional stage directions such as Hoffman dropping his eyes, coloring, starting to his feet, or resting his head in his hands punctuate the arithmetic and register its effect. The climax is not an external event but the revelation that his annual spending approaches eight hundred dollars against an income of six hundred.

The ending is explicitly reformative and leaves little room for ambiguity. Hoffman restates Hamilton’s lesson in moral language, acknowledges the danger of debt and sensual indulgence, and vows immediate change. The final reference to being taught “anew the multiplication table” neatly condenses the story’s method: elementary arithmetic becomes an instrument of ethical awakening. Overall, the style is lucid, argumentative, proverb-rich, and pedagogical, with dialogue serving less to reproduce natural conversation than to dramatize a practical lesson and guide the reader toward a predetermined moral conclusion.

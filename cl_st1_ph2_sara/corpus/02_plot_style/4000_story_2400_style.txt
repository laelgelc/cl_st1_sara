The story is written in a nineteenth-century antiquarian Gothic mode, combining regional realism, oral folklore and the structure of a cautionary fairy tale. Its atmosphere depends on the close interweaving of an accurately mapped Irish landscape with supernatural belief. Roads, bogs, mountains, ruined castles, cabins and historical place names are described with topographical precision before Lisnavoura is established as a liminal, haunted presence. This slow geographical opening gives the account the air of local history or collected testimony, making the later marvels seem rooted in an actual community rather than in an abstract fantasy world.

The narrative voice is authoritative, retrospective and occasionally intrusive. It shifts between the impersonal manner of a chronicler and the personal presence of a storyteller, using phrases such as “of which I have spoken” and “which I had better relate connectedly.” The narrator explains local customs, expressions and superstitions for an audience presumed not to know them, often inserting definitions or parenthetical qualifications. This documentary manner lends credibility to the supernatural narrative while preserving the flavour of an orally transmitted legend. The narrator does not openly dispute the villagers’ interpretation of events; instead, fairy belief is presented from within the emotional and cultural logic of the community.

The prose is elaborate, visual and rhythmically expansive. Long, cumulative sentences frequently move through chains of subordinate clauses, appositions and coordinated details. Landscape descriptions unfold panoramically, while supernatural objects are rendered through dense catalogues of colour, costume, movement and ornament. Repetition and parallel construction reinforce both abundance and dread: the carriage is successively gilded, emblazoned and adorned, while the child’s later appearances follow a repeated pattern of smiling, beckoning and vanishing. Shorter sentences are strategically introduced at moments of shock—“Twilight was deepening” or “Molly Ryan never more saw her darling”—giving decisive emotional force to the story’s turning points.

The diction alternates between elevated literary language and strongly regional speech. In the narration, words such as “prophylactic,” “machinations,” “ineffable,” “beleaguering” and “irretrievably” create a formal, learned register. This is contrasted with the Munster-inflected dialogue, represented through phonetic spellings, altered grammar and local vocabulary: “craythurs,” “childhers,” “doore,” “wisha,” “astora” and “mavourneen.” The dialogue conveys social setting, emotional immediacy and oral authenticity. Characters often speak through exclamations, repetitions, blessings and invocations of God, especially in moments of fear. Their Catholic devotional language coexists naturally with pre-Christian fairy lore, revealing a world in which holy water, prayers, rowan trees, horseshoes and house-leek all belong to the same practical system of supernatural defence.

Imagery is dominated by contrasts between beauty and menace, light and darkness, attraction and repulsion. Autumn sunset, purple heath, thinning ash leaves and birdsong create a melancholy pastoral setting, but the lengthening shadow of Lisnavoura turns that beauty ominous. The fairy procession is similarly double-sided. The princess has a sweet, bell-like voice, a captivating smile and jewelled arms, while her servants are diminutive, sharp-featured, fanged and malicious. The enormous white horses and sumptuous gilded carriage evoke aristocratic splendour, yet their exaggerated proportions and unnatural movement make them uncanny. The black attendant, described through grotesque and racialised imagery typical of the period, intensifies this fusion of spectacle and horror. Similes are vivid and concrete: the bog is “level as a lake,” rock formations resemble fortifications, the horses’ manes stream “like so much smoke,” and Billy rises “as lightly as a feather.”

The pacing follows a gradual escalation. The opening lingers over history, geography, poverty and domestic safeguards, establishing both realism and foreboding. The children’s disappearance is then disclosed incrementally: first all three seem missing, then two return, and only afterward is Billy’s loss confirmed. The children’s account temporarily rewinds the chronology and retells the central event in a more continuous, highly visual set piece. After the abduction, the story becomes episodic, recording Billy’s diminishing apparitions over months and years. This deceleration transforms immediate terror into prolonged grief, and the final retrospective passage broadens a single disappearance into a lifelong absence.

Suspense is created largely through foreshadowing and dramatic irony. The profusion of charms around the cabin signals danger before any supernatural event occurs. Sunset, falling leaves, the early barring of doors and the hill’s advancing shadow all mark the approach of a vulnerable threshold between day and night. The adults and readers recognise the danger associated with Lisnavoura before the children do. In the abduction scene, temptation operates through successive distractions: the princess’s beauty masks the hostility around her, and the rolling apples draw the children farther from home without their noticing the distance. Dust then functions as both physical concealment and magical transition, allowing the fairy company to vanish at the exact moment daylight disappears.

Characterisation is economical and archetypal but emotionally effective. Molly Ryan is defined by poverty, maternal vigilance, religious-folkloric fear and helpless grief. Nell initially provides practical reassurance, but her confidence quickly gives way to inherited terror. The younger children are characterised through innocence and sensory response rather than psychological complexity. Billy’s golden hair, blue eyes and simplicity mark him as exceptionally beautiful and therefore especially vulnerable to fairy selection. The supernatural figures are not rounded personalities but embodiments of seduction, caprice and concealed cruelty.

The dominant tone is mournful, ominous and compassionate, with occasional touches of grotesque animation. The story avoids explicit violence; its horror comes from removal, uncertainty and the impossibility of burial. Billy is neither securely alive nor acknowledged as dead, and his fleeting returns deepen rather than relieve the family’s loss. The final image of Lisnavoura seen from afar turns the landscape itself into a memorial. Thus the conclusion shifts from folkloric terror to elegy: the true emotional centre is not the spectacle of fairy abduction, but the enduring pain of a family denied knowledge, recovery and a grave over which to mourn.

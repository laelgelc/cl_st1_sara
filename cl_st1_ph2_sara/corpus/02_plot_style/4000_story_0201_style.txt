The story is a first-person retrospective comic anecdote, narrated by an educated American woman whose self-awareness, social anxiety, and dry wit shape the account. The voice is conversational and confiding, frequently addressing experience through rhetorical questions, ironic asides, and hindsight. Although the narrator presents the nocturnal journey as genuinely alarming, she continually converts danger into entertainment by exaggerating her fears and exposing the absurdity of her own conduct. This creates a tone that moves between apprehension, mock melodrama, social satire, and affectionate self-ridicule.

The prose has a distinctly nineteenth-century character. Its vocabulary combines polished, literary phrasing—“foreboding fear,” “perversity of my fallen nature,” “calmness of despair”—with colloquial expressions such as “bundled in,” “croak,” and “pok[e] up the little door.” Long, carefully coordinated sentences establish circumstances and accumulate complications, while short sentences and fragments sharpen comic turns: “Not a doubt of it”; “Blest if I do!” Punctuation is expressive and conspicuous, with semicolons, dashes, exclamation marks, and interpolated asides reproducing the narrator’s animated delivery. Period spellings and constructions, including “any thing,” “curb-stone,” and formal social terms such as “brougham,” reinforce the historical setting.

Dialogue is central to both pacing and characterization. Exchanges between the anxious narrator and her more carefree sister are brisk, teasing, and rhythmically balanced. The narrator predicts disaster; her sister minimizes it, laughs, sleeps, or offers practical suggestions. Their contrasting temperaments generate much of the comedy. The cabmen’s dialect is represented through dropped consonants, nonstandard grammar, and occupational speech—“mum,” “ain’t,” “a-goin’,” “cabbies”—while the repeated “All right, mum” becomes an ironic refrain because almost nothing is right. British voices and customs are filtered through an American perspective, allowing the story to play gently with national stereotypes, propriety, class assumptions, and the figure of the “strong-minded American” woman.

The plot follows an escalating episodic structure. An ordinary social visit leads to a mildly improper choice of transport, then to fog, an inexperienced or drunken driver, failed inquiries, a temporary rescue, renewed uncertainty, and finally an accidental arrival home. Repetition sustains the comic ordeal: the cab travels “on and on,” drivers repeatedly admit uncertainty, and apparent solutions produce further wandering. Time markers—half-past ten, eleven o’clock, approaching midnight—raise the pressure, while the dwindling lights and passers-by make London seem progressively emptier. The conclusion rapidly releases the tension, then closes with a breakfast-table retelling that turns private embarrassment into communal amusement and supplies a lightly moralizing punch line.

Description is vivid but selective, emphasizing weather, movement, visibility, and physical dishevelment. London is rendered through muddy streets, dim lamps, indistinct terraces, wet squares, and enveloping fog rather than through precise geography. The fog functions both literally and structurally: it obscures landmarks, weakens voices, destabilizes social identity, and makes respectable women appear suspicious. The story’s recurring imagery transforms the cab journey into a maritime voyage or supernatural pursuit. Reins become a “useless rudder,” the women are “shipwrecked,” safety is a “spar of salvation,” and their destination is a “haven.” At the same time, the white horse, unseen driver, and ceaseless motion create a mock-Gothic “phantom cab” motif. These grand metaphors humorously magnify a mundane transportation mishap.

Character portraits are economical and caricatural. The sister is visualized as a “dilapidated Ophelia,” with limp curls, awry clothing, clasped gloves, and a mud-spattered face; the literary allusion elevates her comic misery into theatrical tragedy. The first driver becomes a “burly, breezy cherub,” then a sleeping, beery figure presiding over a ghostly vehicle. The helpful gray-haired gentleman is cast as “John Bull,” less an individualized person than an embodiment of dependable English decency. Such figures are drawn through memorable physical details, emblematic labels, and the narrator’s shifting emotional judgments.

Humour arises chiefly from incongruity. The women strive to preserve dignity while muddy, lost, frightened, and dependent on strangers. The narrator invokes eminent acquaintances and distinguished ancestors to authenticate her respectability, but the effort only highlights how dubious she imagines herself to look. Her threats, vows, and visions of annihilation are comically disproportionate to the situation. Bathos repeatedly punctures elevated language: heroic rescue is followed by worries about cab fare, Gothic terror by sneezing and snoring, and national independence by a resolution never again to use a hansom. The style therefore blends travel writing, urban adventure, social comedy, and mock-Gothic storytelling, using lively dialogue and theatrical exaggeration to turn embarrassment into a polished, humorous reminiscence.

The story adopts the style of a Victorian historical exemplum: it retells documented episodes from the past in order to celebrate courage, loyalty, and self-sacrifice. Its mode lies between short fiction, popular history, and moral instruction. Historical dates, named figures, political background, and explanatory asides lend the narrative an air of authority, while reconstructed dialogue, ominous warnings, dramatic entrances, and scenes of physical danger give it the immediacy of romance.

The narrator is omniscient, authoritative, and openly evaluative. Rather than remaining neutral, the voice judges people and events through emphatic moral labels such as “brave,” “noble-hearted,” “foul,” “cruel,” “lawless,” and “bloodthirsty.” It frequently interprets history for the reader, excuses or censures royal conduct, and explicitly states the lesson to be drawn. Direct addresses and inclusive phrases such as “Our tale” create the impression of a storyteller guiding an audience. The narration also shows deliberate restraint at moments of extreme violence: the murder itself is passed over because the declared subject is “golden, not brazen deeds.” This keeps attention on heroic conduct rather than gruesome spectacle.

The structure is strongly expository at first and increasingly dramatic thereafter. The opening establishes the monastic setting, the Scottish political situation, and James I’s character at considerable length. This contextual material delays the central action but frames the assassination as the tragic consequence of national disorder and aristocratic rebellion. Once the conspirators approach, the pace accelerates through short exclamations, rapidly sequenced actions, and repeated obstacles: missing bolts, barred windows, the broken arm, the sealed escape route, and the assassins’ return. The story then shifts across centuries to Marie Antoinette at Versailles, creating a parallel historical episode in which attendants again guard a threatened sovereign. The closing paragraph unites both narratives under a single moral image of stars made visible by a darkening sky.

The prose is elevated, formal, and archaically colored. Its vocabulary draws on chivalry, monarchy, religion, and moral discourse: “sovereign,” “minstrelsy,” “traitors,” “extremity of peril,” “saintly,” and “fated chambers of royalty.” Period terms such as “tire-women,” “catherans,” “putting the stane,” and “cognizance” strengthen the historical atmosphere. Dialogue is similarly dignified and often shaped as memorable declaration or prophecy. Exclamation marks, apostrophic phrases, and interjections such as “Alas!” and “Behold” heighten the melodramatic and oral quality.

Sentence construction is predominantly long and cumulative, with multiple subordinate clauses, semicolons, parenthetical qualifications, and balanced contrasts. These expansive sentences are suited to historical explanation and moral reflection. During moments of danger, however, they are interrupted by terse statements and exclamations—“Alas! the bolts and bars were gone!” and “Yes, there was.”—which sharpen suspense. Parallel syntax and enumeration are common, especially in descriptions of James’s accomplishments, Scotland’s violence, and the sequence of defensive actions.

Characterization is idealizing and emblematic rather than psychologically intricate. James is presented as accomplished, civilized, and fundamentally upright, though the narrator concedes his craft, tyranny, and injustice. This qualified praise gives the account a limited appearance of historical balance while preserving him as a tragic reforming monarch. Joan Beaufort and Marie Antoinette are figures of threatened nobility. Catherine Douglas, Madame Auguier, and the guardsmen are defined chiefly through decisive acts of loyalty. Their physical vulnerability intensifies their moral strength: Catherine’s arm is “true as steel” even though it is too frail to withstand the attackers.

The dominant techniques are contrast, foreshadowing, parallelism, and symbolic imagery. Courtly festivity is contrasted with monastic austerity; civilization with savagery; fragile bodies with steadfast spirits; and royal helplessness with the courage of attendants. Repeated warnings—the Highland woman, the prophecy of a king’s death, and the conspirator’s misgivings—create tragic inevitability. The two historical episodes mirror one another through doors, interrupted sleep, attacking mobs, endangered queens, and loyal guardians. Images of darkness, blood, steel, stars, and thresholds convert historical incidents into moral symbols.

The emotional register is reverential, patriotic, and elegiac, with strong melodramatic accents. Suspense and pathos are employed not merely to entertain but to inspire admiration. The conclusion states the governing idea directly: violence and treason provide the darkness against which loyalty shines more brightly. The final verse functions as a commemorative inscription, elevating Catherine Douglas from a historical participant into a national moral icon.

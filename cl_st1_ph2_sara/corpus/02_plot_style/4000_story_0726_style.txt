The story adopts a polished, early twentieth-century literary style that blends psychological realism with supernatural mysticism. Its atmosphere develops gradually from ordinary urban comfort into unease, revelation, and spiritual wonder. The supernatural is not presented through visible apparitions or overt horror, but through changes in perception: a forgotten obligation, an inexplicable sense of presence, heightened sensitivity to beauty, and the eventual recovery of a buried memory. This restraint gives the story the character of a contemplative ghost tale rather than a conventional tale of terror.

The narration is third-person and closely aligned with the protagonist’s consciousness. Although the narrator retains an external perspective, the prose repeatedly enters his uncertain thought processes through free indirect discourse, rhetorical questions, fragments, and self-corrections: “But where, what, with whom?” and “Growth? He dismissed the word.” This creates psychological intimacy while preserving narrative flexibility. The narrator also occasionally interprets the protagonist from a slightly superior vantage, identifying truths that the practical-minded man “neither acknowledged nor recognized.” The resulting voice is sympathetic but gently ironic, especially when contrasting his supposed stolidity with the profound transformation taking place within him.

The protagonist is characterized principally through opposition. He is practical, robust, commercially minded, emotionally restricted, and suspicious of imagination; beauty, art, and artists have previously seemed useless to him. His orderly flat and well-regulated habits embody this temperament. Against that solidity, the story introduces instability, receptiveness, memory, and wonder. The repeated word “growth” marks the central psychological process. At first he experiences change as threatening and pathological, but gradually recognizes it as an enlargement of consciousness. Character development is therefore inward and revelatory rather than social or action-driven.

The diction moves between plain, concrete observation and elevated, lyrical description. Everyday language dominates the opening: the theater, taxicab, evening papers, pipe, drink, overcoats, and golf sticks establish a recognizable modern world. As the protagonist’s perceptions deepen, the vocabulary becomes increasingly poetic and ceremonial, using words such as “hierophantic,” “sacerdotal,” “immemorial,” and “transfiguring.” This stylistic elevation mirrors his awakening. Beauty is personified as a feminine power that “smote him between the eyes,” while Night becomes “mysterious, myriad-eyed,” almost a divine being. The shift in register is thus structurally meaningful: the prose itself undergoes the transformation experienced by the character.

Sentence structure is highly rhythmic and often accumulative. Long sentences reproduce the gradual swelling of sensation through clauses, repetitions, and lists: uneasiness grows “upwards, outwards,” while beauty arrives with “stars and clouds and perfumes.” Short sentences and fragments interrupt these flowing passages at moments of alarm or recognition: “There was some one in the flat”; “He was shivering”; “He saw. He understood.” Dashes are especially prominent, marking hesitation, qualification, sudden association, and the pressure of thoughts that resist orderly expression. Semicolons and repeated conjunctions help create a measured, incantatory cadence. Parenthetical remarks and phrases such as “as it were,” “perhaps,” and “it seemed to him” preserve ambiguity by presenting supernatural interpretations as subjective impressions.

Repetition is a major organizing device. “It was curious” establishes the tone of puzzled retrospection, while “forgotten,” “overlooked,” “left undone,” and “remember” form a lexical pattern around suppressed memory. The recurring language of entrance—doors, thresholds, curtains, opening, widening, and letting in—turns the mind into a resistant interior space. Memory is personified as a woman who first peeps around a curtain and finally reveals her face. The repeated sequence of dismissal and return creates the sense of an influence patiently pressing against consciousness. Parallel phrases such as “He saw the procession of the clouds; he saw the stars; he saw the moonlight” add rhetorical intensity and suggest a newly awakened, almost visionary attention.

Imagery is built around several connected contrasts: enclosure and openness, artificial light and moonlight, city and nature, solidity and fluidity, fear and beauty. The theater and flat initially represent enclosed, artificial environments, whereas the open window becomes the threshold of revelation. Electric lights are switched on during fear and turned off when the protagonist accepts the night. London is transformed through marine and celestial imagery: traffic roars “like a sea,” roofs become a “sea of buildings,” and moonlight falls in “a shower of silver spears.” The urban setting is not rejected but transfigured, allowing wild fragrance, stars, clouds, and ancient mystery to penetrate modern metropolitan life.

The pacing is deliberately slow and cumulative. The opening begins in medias res with an unexplained disturbance, then traces its recurrence in increasingly intense stages. Little external action occurs: the protagonist leaves the theater, returns home, searches the flat, stands at a window, and goes to bed. Suspense instead arises from minute fluctuations in feeling. Delayed explanation is crucial. The name Straughan appears only after the psychological and aesthetic transformation is well advanced, the forgotten promise is recalled near the end, and the confirming letter arrives in the final lines. This structure converts what first appears to be anxiety into supernatural communication and retrospectively gives every earlier sensation a coherent meaning.

Dialogue is sparse and mostly internal or remembered. Brief quoted thoughts dramatize the protagonist’s efforts to reason with himself, while the imagined appeal—“Let me in, let me in”—condenses the story’s central metaphor of spiritual entry. Because interpersonal conversation is almost absent, the narrative has a solitary, introspective quality. Even Straughan is characterized indirectly, through memory and contrast: he is poor, artistic, sensitive to “color and sound,” and devoted to the beauty his friend once dismissed. His posthumous return expresses his personality more fully than any physical description could.

The emotional trajectory runs from vague discomfort through panic, resistance, seduction, intoxication, and peace. Horror vocabulary—“horrid,” “panic,” “chills,” “shivering,” “haunted”—is gradually absorbed into language of blessing and uplift. This produces a tonal reversal: the ghostly visitation is ultimately benevolent, and fear results not from malevolence but from the protagonist’s resistance to an unfamiliar enlargement of being. The final revelation is concise and understated. The plain statement that Straughan died that evening anchors the preceding lyricism in narrative fact, while the last sentence—“he had come back—in the Beauty that he loved”—fuses apparition, memory, friendship, and aesthetic revelation into a single closing image.

Overall, the style is atmospheric, introspective, symbolically patterned, and increasingly rhapsodic. It combines the suspense mechanisms of supernatural fiction with the inward focus of a conversion narrative. Its most distinctive feature is the correspondence between form and subject: as the protagonist becomes receptive to beauty, the prose expands from practical description into sensuous, personified, quasi-religious lyricism.

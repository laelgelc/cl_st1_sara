The story adopts the form of an Australian bush yarn: an anecdotal, orally inflected tale built around deception, drink, rough fellowship and an ironic reversal. Its two-part structure retells the same incident from different viewpoints. Dave Regan’s first-person account explains how he and Jim trick the publican, while the second drover’s account reveals the delayed consequences. This overlapping narration creates dramatic irony, broadens the joke and gives the episode the feel of a story passed from speaker to speaker around a campfire.

The narrative voice is informal, companionable and digressive. Both narrators speak as working bushmen addressing an implied audience, frequently using formulas such as “I can tell you a yarn” and pausing to supply local history, gossip or practical explanation. Their voices are observant but understated: dubious conduct is related with little moral anxiety, as though cunning, drinking and minor swindling are ordinary parts of life on the track. The narration generally proceeds chronologically, but compact descriptive asides—especially the explanation of Poisonous Jimmy’s nickname and habits—interrupt the action in the manner of spontaneous oral storytelling.

The diction is strongly vernacular and regionally marked. Australian terms such as “swags,” “mulga,” “mob,” “tucker,” “shanty,” “shout,” “chequeman,” “cut-out” and “on the boose” establish the social and geographical world without formal exposition. Nonstandard grammar and phonetic contractions—“me and my mate,” “ain’t,” “sez,” “knowed,” “borrered,” “comin’,” “waitin’”—differentiate spoken language from the somewhat more standard narrative prose, although even the narration retains colloquial rhythms. Euphemistic printed oaths such as “blanky,” “flamin’,” “crimson” and interrupted dashes evoke vigorous profanity while turning it into part of the comedy.

Syntax is predominantly direct and additive, with clauses linked by “and,” “but,” “so” and “then.” This produces a steady, conversational momentum and mimics speech rather than polished literary narration. Longer sentences often accumulate actions or descriptive details in loose sequence, while dialogue is delivered in short exchanges and repeated formulas: “‘It’s hot.’ ‘It’s hot.’” Such repetition heightens deadpan humour and later becomes comic when the same social ritual is replayed by the drovers. The prose balances brisk scene-setting with extended comic portraiture, especially in the description of Jimmy’s mismatched eyes, broken nose and beard “like a bush fence round a stubble-field.”

Dialogue carries much of the characterization and action. Dave and Jim’s fraud emerges through insinuation, winks and strategically vague answers rather than explicit planning. Poisonous Jimmy’s calculated hospitality is exposed through his offhand questions, free drinks and conspicuous attempts to appear innocent. In the second half, his mounting fury is conveyed through fractured speech, extravagant abuse and repeated demands for his money. Individual voices are socially specific, but the dialogue also works theatrically, with compact cues, reversals and recurring phrases that resemble the beats of a comic performance.

Characterization relies on caricature, physical detail and behaviour rather than psychological analysis. Dave and Jim are opportunistic, quick-witted and laconic. The drovers are rough but fair-minded, willing both to drink Jimmy’s beer and to pay for it when challenged. Jimmy is the central comic figure: predatory, suspicious and physically formidable, yet defeated by his own greed. His “sharp cunning little eye” and misleading “cold stony eye” become a running motif, first suggesting menace and later complicating the fight. Minor figures—the flirtatious barmaid, the quiet boss and the mounted police—are sketched economically through a gesture, manner or role.

The humour is dry, cumulative and ironic. Much of it comes from incongruous similes, restrained commentary and the gap between what Jimmy thinks is happening and what the reader knows. His smile is so false it would make “anybody home-sick”; his beard resembles a rough fence; his grin is “like a parson with a broken nose.” The deception succeeds because Jimmy believes he is manipulating the supposed drovers, only to discover that his own bait—free beer and credit—has been used against him. The anticlimactic final sentence, “They fined him ten bob,” compresses Jimmy’s rage, pursuit and claims of justice into a flat bureaucratic punchline.

The setting is rendered through practical details of distance, heat, dust, money, horses, cattle and roadside drinking places. The landscape is not treated lyrically; it is a working environment that determines thirst, travel, employment and vulnerability. Social life revolves around droving camps, stores, shanties and towns, while money arrives irregularly in cheques and is quickly threatened by alcohol. The story’s world is masculine, itinerant and governed less by formal institutions than by reputation, physical competence and bush codes of reciprocity.

Tonally, the piece is affectionate, sardonic and unsentimental. It does not condemn the swindle directly, but frames it as a contest between habitual tricksters in which the sharper men win. Violence remains comic rather than tragic, and even Jimmy’s threats are absorbed into the exuberance of the yarn. Overall, the style combines oral realism, regional idiom, economical plotting, grotesque comic description and a circular structure in which repeated details acquire fresh meaning from a second teller’s perspective.

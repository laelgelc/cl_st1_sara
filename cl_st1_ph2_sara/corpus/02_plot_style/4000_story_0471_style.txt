The story uses a lively first-person retrospective voice, presented by an unnamed Harvard student who speaks as both participant and amused observer. His narration is informal, companionable, and knowingly unreliable in minor matters, full of conversational qualifications such as “I guess,” “I forget which,” and “that’s all I’ve got to say.” He addresses the reader directly and tells the episode as an anecdote shared among friends rather than as a polished literary performance. The retrospective stance permits affectionate mockery: the narrator remains caught up in the excitement of the occasion while also recognising the absurdity of the students’ behaviour.

The diction combines early twentieth-century American college slang with mock-elevated rhetoric. Colloquialisms such as “daffy,” “moseyed,” “scooted,” “licked,” “for fair,” and “like Sam Hill” create a youthful, masculine campus idiom. Football terminology, Harvard songs, cheers, buildings, newspapers, and institutional customs give the narrative a strongly local collegiate identity. Against this everyday vocabulary, the story repeatedly sets grandiose phrases about “Duty and Patriotism,” “the scene of the conflict,” and “flaming pinions.” This inflated language parodies ceremonial speeches, athletic hero worship, and institutional self-importance. McTurkle’s hesitant, academic manner—marked by repeated “ah,” formal phrasing, and cautious abstraction—provides a further comic contrast with the crowd’s energetic slang.

Humour is the governing mode. Much of it arises through incongruity: a solitary, socially obscure scholar becomes “the band”; a French horn plays unsuitable airs at the head of a football procession; and McTurkle delivers a solemn speech without even knowing which sport is to be played. The narrator also uses comic definitions and analogies, most notably the description of the French horn as an instrument whose notes become “balled up and confused,” followed by the comparison with French verbs. Hyperbole is frequent but delivered with straight-faced conviction: McTurkle supposedly achieves fourteen octaves, enthusiasm becomes “a drug on the market,” and the horn gives Annie Laurie her “final death blow.” Understatement works alongside exaggeration, especially when disasters, injuries, and mob-like disruptions are treated as ordinary features of college life.

The syntax is generally direct and fast-moving, with many coordinated clauses linked by “and,” reproducing the breathless accumulation of events. Repetition gives passages momentum: injuries follow injuries, speeches prompt increasingly wild applause, the procession absorbs more students, and demands from the crowd spread down the line. Short sentences and fragments sharpen comic beats—“McTurkle was transformed”; “We cheered”; “Then we went home.” Parenthetical remarks, dashes, self-corrections, and afterthoughts preserve the feel of spontaneous oral storytelling. The prose often moves from a long, elaborate buildup to a brief deflationary payoff.

Dialogue occupies a large part of the narrative and is used theatrically. Bud’s mock-heroic persuasion of McTurkle unfolds like a comic set piece, with escalating appeals and carefully timed responses. Crowd speech is represented through chants, interruptions, ellipses, stretched vowels, and overlapping cries, creating the sound and disorder of a mass gathering. Repeated formulas—“We want,” “A-a-aye,” “Speech! speech!”—turn the students into a comic chorus. Individual characterisation is economical and speech-based: Bud is inventive and theatrically manipulative, McTurkle is earnest, literal-minded, and dignified, and the narrator is amused, sociable, and irreverent.

Character description relies on caricature and a few recurring visual details. McTurkle is thin, gaunt, near-sighted, scholarly, and perpetually blinking; his glasses, long legs, book-covered table, plaid lounging coat, and French horn make him immediately recognisable. Such details are exaggerated without becoming cruel. Although the students exploit his innocence, the narrative treats him with genuine affection. His momentary transformation from ignored “grind” to celebrated leader gives the comedy a warm emotional centre.

Structurally, the story follows a clear anecdotal escalation. It begins with Harvard’s sporting misfortunes, moves through officially organised enthusiasm, then shifts into an improvised nighttime procession. The recruitment of McTurkle supplies the central comic premise, while the march, concert, and speech develop it through increasingly public absurdity. Near the end, the narrator explicitly jokes that the story lacks a climax, drawing attention to the conventions of storytelling and pretending technical incompetence. The following scene supplies an anticlimactic coda: McTurkle declines to attend the game, Harvard loses, and the narrator closes with a rhetorical appeal that absolves him. This self-conscious ending preserves the loose, spoken quality of the tale while still giving it a neat comic resolution.

The pacing is brisk and cumulative. Exposition is compressed into lists, while major comic scenes slow down for dialogue, gestures, reactions, and crowd noise. Transitions are simple and chronological—“In the morning,” “After a bit,” “Back in the Square,” “I got halfway”—which keeps the narrative easy to follow despite the size and movement of the crowd. Suspense is light and comic rather than dramatic: the reader waits to see whether McTurkle will agree, what he will play, what he will say, and whether his contribution will somehow affect the game.

The dominant tone is exuberant, nostalgic, satirical, and good-natured. The story gently mocks Harvard boosterism, football fervour, formulaic speeches, mob enthusiasm, and the social gulf between athletic campus culture and academic isolation. Yet it does not reject communal enthusiasm; it revels in its energy and remembers the night as “glorious.” The satire is therefore inclusive rather than hostile. The narrator laughs at the institution, the crowd, McTurkle, and himself in roughly equal measure.

Overall, the style is that of a period American campus yarn: oral in manner, episodic in construction, rich in slang and local colour, and driven by hyperbole, incongruity, rhythmic repetition, and comic deflation. Its most distinctive feature is the sustained collision between heroic athletic rhetoric and mundane reality, embodied by an earnest scholar leading thousands of football enthusiasts with a French horn while playing “Annie Laurie.”

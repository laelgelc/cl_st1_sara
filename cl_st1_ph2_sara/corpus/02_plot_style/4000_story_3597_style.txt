The story uses a first-person animal narrator whose innocent, limited understanding creates sustained dramatic irony. The dog speaks with warmth, sincerity, and moral seriousness, yet repeatedly misinterprets human vocabulary, motives, and events. Her confident errors—such as defining “heroism” as agriculture or imagining that her buried puppy has been planted and will grow—are comic on the surface but increasingly devastating. The reader understands what the narrator cannot, so the prose makes naïveté both its chief humorous device and its principal source of pathos.

The narrative voice blends childlike simplicity with an elaborate, conversational storytelling manner. The narrator often addresses the reader directly through questions, explanations, and confidential asides: “you know,” “as you see,” and similar phrases create the impression of an intimate oral reminiscence. Her language moves between plain emotional statements and long, loosely unfolding sentences filled with qualifications, repetitions, semicolons, dashes, and parenthetical observations. This gives the prose an expansive, anecdotal rhythm while preserving the speaker’s guileless personality. Exclamations, abrupt fragments, and repeated words intensify moments of affection, fear, or grief.

The diction is deliberately mixed. Domestic, concrete language about children, dogs, gardens, fire, pain, and food sits beside grand abstract terms such as “unintellectual,” “supererogation,” “synonymous,” “instinct,” and “reason.” Polysyllabic vocabulary is treated as social performance rather than reliable knowledge. The mother dog collects impressive words and improvises their meanings, producing verbal comedy and satirizing pretension, second-hand learning, and the tendency to confuse confident speech with wisdom. Nautical metaphors, theatrical expressions, colloquialisms, and comic exaggerations make these early passages especially lively and playful.

Tonally, the story undergoes a controlled movement from farce to tragedy. Its opening is buoyant, irreverent, and digressive, built around jokes about religion, education, status, and canine society. The middle becomes tender and idyllic as it describes the narrator’s new home, maternal happiness, and devotion to the family. The nursery fire introduces suspense, bodily danger, and painful misunderstanding, though the eventual recognition of the dog’s courage briefly restores emotional security. The final laboratory episode then turns the earlier comedy of misunderstanding into horror. The narrator’s trusting interpretation remains unchanged, but the reader’s knowledge transforms her cheerful gratitude into unbearable irony.

The characterization depends on contrast rather than psychological exposition. The dog’s mother is vain and intellectually fraudulent but brave, compassionate, and ethically profound; this opposition separates genuine virtue from mere education. The narrator likewise lacks human knowledge but possesses loyalty, courage, empathy, and reason. The scientist, by contrast, is verbally sophisticated and socially honored, yet capable of cruelty and moral blindness. His polished intellectuality is repeatedly undercut by his conduct. The story therefore uses reversals—cultivated versus ignorant, rational versus instinctive, human versus animal—to expose the inadequacy of conventional hierarchies.

The structure is linear and episodic, divided into numbered sections that mark stages in the narrator’s life: maternal instruction, separation, domestic happiness, heroic action, public praise, and final loss. Early comic material is not incidental; it establishes the narrator’s habit of misunderstanding words and prepares the tragic misreadings at the end. Motifs also recur with altered force. The mother’s teaching about self-sacrifice leads directly to the rescue of the baby, while the gardening imagery that once represented shared happiness becomes the basis for the narrator’s belief that the dead puppy will emerge from the earth. This careful foreshadowing gives the apparently casual narration strong formal unity.

Satire and sentiment are closely interwoven. The story mocks religious labels, social vanity, fashionable learning, scientific self-importance, and the moral complacency of people who regard animals as expendable. Yet it does not maintain satirical distance from the narrator; instead, it invites deep sympathy for her. Repetition, diminutives, tactile descriptions, and affectionate epithets—especially in passages concerning the baby and puppy—heighten tenderness. Violence is described plainly rather than sensationally, allowing the narrator’s incomprehension and the humans’ detachment to carry the emotional weight.

Overall, the style is deceptively simple: a comic animal monologue gradually becomes an indictment of human cruelty. Its signature qualities are an ingenuous first-person voice, elaborate conversational syntax, malapropism and false definition, dramatic irony, tonal reversal, sentimental detail, and moral satire. The prose repeatedly shows that verbal intelligence can coexist with ethical stupidity, while a supposedly “dumb beast” may embody courage, love, and humanity more fully than the humans who judge and exploit her.

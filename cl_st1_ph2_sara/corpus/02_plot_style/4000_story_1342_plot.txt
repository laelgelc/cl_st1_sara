Elijah Martin, a cowardly, lazy, and disregarded resident of Redwood Camp, is swept away during a catastrophic flood and carried into the wilderness. Hungry and terrified, he finds an Indigenous sweat-house, eats the fish stored there, and disguises himself in the ceremonial clothing of a dead chief. When the local Minyo tribe discovers him, they mistake his arrival for the fulfillment of a prophecy that their new chief would come from the sea wearing his predecessor’s regalia.

Martin’s passivity, helplessness, and lack of weapons appear supernatural to the Minyos, who install him as their sacred chief. Initially afraid of being exposed, he accepts the role because it provides safety and respect. When two white captives are brought before him, his horrified reactions are interpreted as commands to release them. Hearing the freed men praise him as a powerful, natural ruler transforms his self-image and gives him genuine confidence.

Under Martin’s leadership, the peaceful Minyos become more organized and prosperous. He negotiates a favorable treaty with the government, protects their land, rejects exploitative trade goods, encourages agriculture and commerce, and helps shield them from white violence. After two successful years, however, he grows restless and homesick despite his wealth and authority.

The arrival of a new Indian agent and his wife disrupts his isolation. The curious Mrs. Dall secretly approaches Martin and remarks on his handsome appearance, not realizing he understands English. Overcome by attraction and longing for his former world, Martin impulsively embraces and kisses her before fleeing. Mrs. Dall immediately realizes that the supposedly Indigenous chief is actually a white man, while Martin’s young Minyo wife, Wachita, silently witnesses the encounter.

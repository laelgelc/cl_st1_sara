The story uses a first-person retrospective voice that closely preserves the consciousness and speech patterns of a nearly sixteen-year-old Kentucky boy. The narrator speaks with youthful certainty, emotional openness, and limited moral understanding, even though he is recounting events that have already begun to alter him. His voice is colloquial and regionally marked, using nonstandard grammar and idioms such as “we was,” “wouldn’t of,” “I seen,” “cut out,” “laid low,” “jawed,” and “give me the fantods.” Recurrent conversational phrases—“Well,” “I can’t help it,” “I knew,” “I can’t make it out,” and “That’s what I’m writing about”—create the impression of spontaneous oral storytelling. The narration repeatedly wanders into memories and explanations before returning to the central incident, making the reader feel that the boy is struggling aloud to understand his experience.

The diction is plain, concrete, and emotionally direct. Rather than offering sophisticated psychological analysis, the narrator relies on simple judgments such as “lovely,” “clean,” “rotten,” “square,” “mean-looking,” and “all right.” These words acquire thematic force through repetition. Horses represent beauty, honesty, discipline, and instinctive purity, while the brothel is described through dirt, ugliness, decay, and falseness. The narrator’s vocabulary often collapses aesthetic, ethical, and physical qualities into one another: what is beautiful is assumed to be good and clean, while what is ugly or foul-smelling is assumed to be morally corrupt. This reflects both his adolescence and his idealistic, still-incomplete moral framework.

The language also contains frequent racial slurs and broad racial stereotypes. These are embedded in the narrator’s period-specific vernacular and reveal the normalized racism and paternalism of his social environment. At the same time, the narrator often trusts and admires Black stable workers more than white adults, treating them as loyal, humorous, knowledgeable, and naturally connected to horses. This does not cancel the racism of the language or characterization; rather, it produces a historically revealing contradiction. The boy can recognize individual warmth and integrity while still thinking through the prejudiced categories his culture has given him.

Sentence structure alternates between blunt declarations and long, accumulating sentences joined by “and,” “but,” “then,” and “when.” The extended polysyndetic passages reproduce breathless speech and convey the narrator’s excitement, especially when he describes racing culture, early mornings at the training tracks, or Sunstreak’s preparation for the race. Repetition and loose coordination often replace formal subordination, allowing impressions to arrive in a rush. Short sentences then provide emphasis: “It was my scheme.” “It was a joke.” “He lied.” “I can’t make it out.” Grammatical roughness is therefore central to the characterization rather than incidental; the prose sounds inexperienced, but it is carefully shaped to communicate obsession and moral bewilderment.

The narrative is digressive and delayed. It announces early that the narrator witnessed something he cannot understand, but postpones the revelation through background about Beckersville, runaway travel, gamblers, stable workers, family life, and his lifelong passion for thoroughbreds. This delay builds curiosity while establishing the emotional system through which the climactic event will be judged. The plot moves from adventure story to racing idyll and finally to disillusionment. Its true climax is not Sunstreak’s record-breaking victory but the narrator’s discovery of Jerry Tillford in the brothel. The story then ends without resolution, returning insistently to the question “What did he do it for?” The unresolved ending preserves the authenticity of adolescent confusion and makes the story a study of damaged idealism rather than a neatly completed moral lesson.

Characterization is filtered almost entirely through the narrator’s enthusiasms and aversions. Human beings are frequently assessed according to their relation to horses. Bildad is admirable because he understands racing life and helps the boys; the narrator’s father is valued for his tolerance; Jerry Tillford initially becomes almost sacred because he appears to share the narrator’s intuitive communion with Sunstreak. The horses themselves are more intensely individualized than many of the people. Middlestride is slow-starting, awkward, and powerful late; Sunstreak is controlled, inwardly fiery, beautiful, and modest. Anthropomorphism is prominent, but it works in two directions: horses are given moral and emotional qualities, while people are compared to horses. The crucial comparison between the red-haired woman and Middlestride deliberately fails because she resembles the horse physically but lacks his “clean” nature.

The story’s imagery is strongly sensory. The idealized racetrack world is built from dew-covered grass, frying bacon, coffee, manure, pipe smoke, laughter, shining coats, silk racing clothes, and the physical ache of watching a great horse run. Smell is particularly important, functioning as a measure of emotional and moral harmony. Before the narrator’s disillusionment, even the mixture of manure, food, horses, smoke, and human bodies smells “lovely.” The brothel, by contrast, “smelled rotten,” and after the revelation the racetrack air itself no longer tastes or smells as good. The external world has not changed; his perception has. Sensory contamination becomes the clearest sign that experience has spoiled his former innocence.

Figurative language is simple but vivid and drawn from the narrator’s familiar world. Sunstreak is compared to a girl glimpsed only in imagination, to the water immediately before Niagara Falls, and to a bird dog running flat out. Emotional conviction is bodily: the narrator’s throat aches when he recognizes a great horse, his heart thumps, he cries, and his fingernails cut his palms. Such somatic imagery makes his intuitions feel immediate and involuntary. He does not reason his way toward knowledge; he experiences it as pressure, pain, smell, excitement, or revulsion.

A major stylistic device is repetition with altered meaning. The “shine” in Jerry’s eyes first signifies shared knowledge, devotion, and almost paternal tenderness in the paddock. When the same shine appears as he looks at the woman, the repeated image becomes disturbing. The narrator cannot accept that one expression might belong to both exalted and sexual feeling. Similarly, his repeated insistence that he “knew” Sunstreak would win contrasts with his inability to know why Jerry behaves as he does. He possesses intuitive certainty about horses but lacks an adequate understanding of adult complexity.

The tone combines exuberance, comedy, nostalgia, reverence, disgust, and grief. Early episodes, such as eating half a cigar in the hope of becoming small enough to be a jockey, have the quality of affectionate rural anecdote. The racing passages become lyrical and devotional, with the horse serving as an image of concentrated beauty and purpose. The brothel episode abruptly introduces grotesque realism. Yet the tone remains morally naïve rather than conventionally prudish: what horrifies the narrator is not simply sex, but the apparent equivalence between Jerry’s look of communion with Sunstreak and his desire for the woman. His outrage comes from a perceived betrayal of the sacred.

Overall, the style is an example of vernacular psychological realism shaped as a coming-of-age confession. Its surface simplicity conceals a carefully organized network of contrasts: child and adult, horse and human, instinct and appetite, cleanliness and corruption, certainty and mystery, idealization and experience. The story derives its power from refusing to translate the boy’s confusion into mature explanation. Instead, the repetitive, digressive, sensuous voice traps the reader inside the moment when a cherished vision of beauty is first complicated by the contradictory nature of human desire.

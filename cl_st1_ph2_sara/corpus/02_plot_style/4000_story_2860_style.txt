The story uses a brisk, realist comic style built around close observation of bourgeois domestic life. Its prose is clear, chronological, and largely unornamented, with an omniscient third-person narrator who moves freely between external action, concise character description, and the private anxieties of Mme. Oreille. The narrator establishes character through a dominant trait—her extreme thrift—and then repeatedly tests and magnifies that trait until it becomes both the engine of the plot and the source of the comedy. Descriptions are economical but sharply selective: physical details such as her small stature, neatness, wrinkles, haste, and short temper reinforce the impression of compressed, nervous energy.

The prevailing tone is dryly ironic and gently satirical. Mme. Oreille’s horror at losing eighteen francs is presented in language more appropriate to catastrophe, injury, or warfare, creating comic disproportion. Domestic quarrels become storms, the home becomes more frightening than a battlefield, and a damaged umbrella assumes the emotional importance of financial ruin. The narrator rarely comments at length, instead allowing understatement, exaggeration, and the contrast between trivial causes and violent reactions to expose the characters’ absurdities. The satire is directed particularly at miserliness, marital friction, petty respectability, office vanity, and the opportunistic use of bureaucracy.

The plot has the compact structure of an escalating anecdote. An initial social embarrassment leads to the purchase of the umbrella; one burn becomes many; the domestic conflict produces an insurance scheme; and the insurance claim culminates in Mme. Oreille ordering the finest possible silk at someone else’s expense. Each development follows logically from character while increasing the absurdity. The ending functions as a concise ironic reversal: the woman who resents every expenditure becomes lavish as soon as the insurance company must pay. It closes on action and dialogue rather than explanation, letting the final contradiction deliver the punch line.

Dialogue occupies a large part of the narrative and is used to dramatize power struggles. Mme. Oreille’s speech is emotional, repetitive, accusatory, and full of exclamations, broken phrases, and practical calculations. Her husband’s speech is defensive and comparatively subdued, emphasizing his domestic helplessness. At the insurance office, dialogue becomes more ceremonious and bureaucratic, creating a contrast between Mme. Oreille’s petty claim and the officials’ formal world of large financial disputes. The manager’s restrained politeness, mild jokes, and eventual resignation form a comic counterpoint to her growing confidence. Social hierarchy is conveyed through modes of address, elaborate courtesy, embarrassment, and the contrast between private aggression and public timidity.

Sentence structure varies with mood. Expository passages use long, balanced sentences containing several clauses, while moments of anger or panic break into short statements, stammering, dashes, repetitions, and rapid exchanges. The diction is accessible and concrete, repeatedly returning to money, prices, bills, damage, repairs, and material objects. Exact sums—eight francs and a half, eighteen francs, twenty-six francs, five hundred francs—give the story a precise financial vocabulary and reveal Mme. Oreille’s habit of reducing experience to arithmetic. Hyperbole intensifies her emotions, while similes drawn from injury, hunting, storms, and combat lend mock-heroic grandeur to the minor incident.

Characterization is chiefly behavioral rather than psychological. Mme. Oreille is shown counting, inspecting, hesitating, blushing, rehearsing, lying, bargaining, and finally exploiting the settlement. Her transition from fear to determination is carefully staged through physical movement: she slows as she approaches the office, passes the door repeatedly, struggles up the stairs, stammers before the officials, and then grows steadily more assertive once she senses that her claim may succeed. This attention to gesture and social discomfort gives the comedy a vivid, almost theatrical quality.

Overall, the style combines realism, farce, and moral irony. A commonplace object becomes the centre of a tightly controlled social comedy in which vanity, greed, fear, and self-justification reveal themselves through escalating practical complications. The narrative remains outwardly detached, but its arrangement makes the judgment unmistakable: thrift pushed into obsession produces waste, dishonesty, and comic extravagance.

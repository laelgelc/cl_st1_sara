The story adopts the manner of a literary folktale or historical legend, combining oral storytelling, moral fable and Gothic decline. Its central device is a personified wind that acts as narrator, witness, commentator and participant. The opening directly addresses the reader with questions and invitations to listen, establishing the intimacy of a tale told aloud by the fireside. Thereafter, the wind speaks in the first person, claiming access to places and events across generations. Its recurring cry—“Whew!—whew!—fare away!”—functions as a refrain, a transition between episodes and an audible emblem of time’s relentless movement.

The narrative is episodic and broadly chronological. It begins with Borreby Hall at the height of its splendour, then marks successive stages of loss: the mother’s death, the felling of the forest, the failure to sell the warship, Waldemar Daa’s obsession with alchemy, the exhaustion of the estate, eviction, dispersal and death. Large spans of time are compressed through seasonal and natural cycles, especially in formulations that align human mortality with falling snow, blossom and leaves. The later return to Anna Dorothea after half a century gives the story the shape of a rise-and-fall chronicle, while the final reference to steam and new roads widens the perspective from one family’s ruin to the disappearance of an entire social order.

The prose alternates between expansive scenic description and short, emphatic judgments. Sentences often accumulate details through coordination, catalogues and repeated clauses, producing a flowing, breath-driven rhythm suited to the wind’s voice. At moments of crisis, the syntax becomes sharper and more fragmentary: “Fewer servants; more mice,” or “Nothing to bite, and nothing to burn!” Exclamations, rhetorical questions, direct addresses and sound effects give the narration a strongly performative quality. Repetition is prominent, particularly paired words and parallel structures such as “smoke and ashes,” “cracks and crannies,” and “away, away.” The language has a translated nineteenth-century flavour, with formal titles, archaic social vocabulary and expressions such as “kirtle,” “flagons,” “Herr,” “highborn,” “fared away” and “man-of-war.”

Imagery is vivid, symbolic and often drawn from nature. The daughters are repeatedly identified as flowers—rose, lily and pale hyacinth—so that appearance also suggests temperament: Ida is tender and romantic, Johanna proud and rigid, and Anna Dorothea delicate, solemn and compassionate. Social hierarchy is expressed through bird imagery, most pointedly in the comparison of the humble shipbuilder to a sparrow attempting to enter a crane’s nest. Trees, nests and birds provide a moral counterpoint to aristocratic ambition. Waldemar destroys the oak forest and its habitats to build a ship that never sails, whereas Anna Dorothea’s childhood mercy toward a stork’s nest is echoed when a stork’s nest later preserves the roof of her final dwelling. This symmetry gives the story a providential, fairy-tale logic without making reward or punishment conventionally comforting.

The settings are rendered through strongly contrasting material details. Early wealth appears in polished floors, gold brocade, tapestries, carved furniture, silver plate, German ale, black horses and gilded coaches. Decline is registered through empty barns, broken panes, loose doors, mice, spider webs, cold rooms, threadbare clothes and ash. These concrete inventories make poverty feel gradual and physical. Fire and gold form another important symbolic cluster. The warm domestic fire of the opening promises companionship and storytelling, but Waldemar’s furnace consumes food, fuel and property in pursuit of artificial gold. The apparent success in the alchemist’s glass ends with its shattering, a melodramatic but effective externalisation of his final ruined hope.

The wind’s tone is mobile and mixed. It can be playful, boastful and mischievous, but it is also sardonic, elegiac and intermittently compassionate. It mocks vanity and stubbornness, comments on events with proverbial bluntness and sometimes interferes in them, as when it moves branches, scatters straw or blows Johanna overboard. Its moral stance is therefore not wholly benign; it resembles an impersonal force of fate whose pity coexists with cruelty. The refrain can sound exuberant during scenes of festivity, ominous during decline and mournful at death. This tonal flexibility prevents the narration from becoming uniformly solemn.

Characterisation is economical and emblematic rather than psychologically intricate. Waldemar is defined by pride, learning, self-deception and an inability to relinquish an impossible project. His repeated physical posture—head held high, neck stiffened—turns arrogance into a visible motif. The daughters are distinguished through a few recurring traits and images: Ida’s romantic softness, Johanna’s inflexible pride and masculine boldness, and Anna Dorothea’s gentleness, piety and sympathy for vulnerable creatures. Their fates reverse the assumptions of their aristocratic upbringing, reinforcing the instability of rank and fortune. Dialogue is sparse and generally reserved for decisive statements, prayers or self-revealing declarations, so it carries proverbial or dramatic weight.

The dominant themes are pride, transience, failed ambition, social reversal, mortality and the indifference of history. Wealth and lineage offer no protection against folly or time. Human beings attempt to preserve estates, portraits, names and distinctions, while the wind repeatedly disperses them. Christian moral language appears in the contrast between worldly lords and “the Lord,” in Anna Dorothea’s final prayer, and in the suggestion that compassion matters more than status. Yet the ending remains melancholy rather than triumphantly redemptive: graves are forgotten, roads change course and modern traffic passes over the dead.

Overall, the style is richly narrated, orally inflected and symbolically patterned. It joins fairy-tale simplicity to historical specificity, using recurring refrains, natural personification, visual contrasts and moral irony to turn a family chronicle into an elegy for vanished grandeur. Its most distinctive effect comes from allowing the wind to unite sound, movement, memory and time: the same voice that frolics over fields also enters ruined rooms, sings over unknown graves and finally blows the story itself away.

The story is written in a psychologically observant, realist mode, with the theatrical setting supplying an undercurrent of melodrama and tragedy. Its central stylistic device is dramatic irony: Kostromsky performs Hamlet while undergoing his own crisis of belated self-recognition. Shakespearean motifs—madness, mortality, displaced succession, wounded pride and thoughts of self-destruction—are woven into the actor’s collapse, so that the play within the story becomes both a mirror of his condition and the instrument that exposes it.

The narration is third-person omniscient but concentrates closely on Kostromsky’s consciousness. It moves readily from external description into authoritative psychological explanation, often stating motives and emotional conditions directly: his vanity, exhaustion, envy and fear are interpreted rather than merely implied. At important moments the perspective adopts his own rhythms and values through exclamations, rhetorical questions and fragments of inward speech. Yureva’s feelings are also briefly entered, but she remains comparatively idealised and serves chiefly as a foil to him: youth against decline, sincerity against mannerism, and instinctive talent against fame sustained by habit.

The diction is formal, literary and recognisably period-bound, with occasional archaic or translated constructions. Phrases such as “billet-doux,” “erstwhile beautiful face,” “illimitable pride,” and “empoisoned by the venom of grief” create an elevated register, while theatrical and social details give the prose concrete historical texture. Russian names, patronymics, roubles, provincial touring companies and hierarchical forms of address establish the milieu without prolonged exposition. Some idioms and syntactic inversions have the slightly stiff quality of an older English translation, and the spelling “Shakspeare” reinforces that period character.

Sentence structure alternates between long, cumulative passages and short emphatic statements. Extended sentences are used for biography, public reputation and psychological analysis, often accumulating clauses to convey the scale of Kostromsky’s former success or the pressure of his thoughts. Brief sentences—“There was no applause,” “It was a moment of awful enlightenment,” “Yes, it was all past!”—mark reversals and deliver emotional blows. Ellipses, dashes, repetitions and exclamation marks reproduce hesitation, intoxication and agitation, particularly in dialogue and interior monologue. The prose frequently intensifies through lists, as in the catalogue of wreaths, gifts, applause, money and female admiration that reduces Kostromsky’s glorious past to fading images.

Dialogue is strongly characterising and socially stratified. Kostromsky’s speech shifts between grandiose self-mythologising, imperious abuse, drunken vulgarity and, at the end, unexpectedly lucid tenderness. The barber’s stammering deference and the manager’s frantic colloquialism reveal the theatre’s hierarchy and backstage disorder. Yureva speaks little and softly, which enhances her modesty and leaves her acting to carry her emotional force. Yakovlef’s conversation introduces the experienced professional’s judgment and foreshadows Yureva’s triumph, making the later reversal feel inevitable rather than accidental.

The descriptive method relies on vivid visual and auditory contrasts. Backstage reality is rough, shabby and mechanical: cardboard scenery, hammering carpenters, greasepaint, gas burners and discarded stage properties. In front of the curtain, the theatre becomes a charged collective organism, murmuring like a distant sea, sighing “from a thousand breasts,” or erupting in applause. Recurrent imagery of crowds, chains, currents and enchantment represents the mysterious bond between performer and audience. Make-up and mirrors form another major motif. At first cosmetics appear to restore Kostromsky’s nobility and vitality; at the end, paint mingles with tears as the constructed public self dissolves. Light likewise gives way to darkness, moving from blazing footlights to the dim, dismantled stage after the failed performance.

The structure is linear and tightly theatrical, divided into eight numbered sections that function almost like scenes or acts. The opening establishes Kostromsky’s fame and hints at its decay; the emergency casting of Yureva initiates the conflict; successive acts of *Hamlet* chart the transfer of the audience’s attention; and the empty theatre provides the aftermath. Pacing slows at moments of recognition, especially when Kostromsky receives no applause and suddenly understands his decline. Repetition across the performance—entrances, applause, returns to the wings and demands for vodka—measures his deterioration with almost mechanical precision.

The tone combines irony, compassion and moral severity. Kostromsky is exposed as vain, selfish, cruel and alcoholic, yet the narration does not reduce him to a caricature. His final recognition grants him tragic dignity, though not absolution. Yureva’s talent is presented in lyrical and sentimental terms, while Kostromsky’s envy becomes nearly grotesque in its violent fantasies. The conclusion softens this harshness through confession and pity: he recognises both her gift and his injustice, then warns her against repeating his fate. His repeated declaration, “I have finished,” closes the story with the finality of a curtain falling.

Overall, the style blends backstage realism, explicit psychological narration and tragic theatrical symbolism. It is emotionally expansive rather than understated, using elevated language, foreshadowing and climactic reversals to portray artistic decline as a form of death. The dominant effect is that of a moral-psychological tragedy in miniature, in which public applause is both intoxicating and merciless, and the artist’s deepest catastrophe is not failure itself but the sudden recognition that the power which once justified his life has already departed.

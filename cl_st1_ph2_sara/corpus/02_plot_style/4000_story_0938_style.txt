The story uses a brisk, ironic naturalist style that combines detached social observation with comic exaggeration. Its third-person narrator stands outside the action but repeatedly supplies dry judgments about the characters, especially Patsy, whose immense courage is contrasted with his limited intelligence. The opening comparison—he is “not as wise as seven owls,” yet casts a heroic shadow as long as a cathedral steeple—establishes the governing method: mock-heroic language elevates a foolish street quarrel while quietly exposing its absurdity. The narrator’s composure remains largely intact even as the scene grows chaotic, producing humour through the disparity between ceremonious description and irrational conduct.

The prose alternates between lucid scene-setting and sudden, energetic movement. Physical details are selected with a visual, almost theatrical precision: lamp-glare, polished wood, furbished brass, the bartender’s towel, the Cuban’s tiny hands and feet, and the green flash of his emerald ring. Repeated gestures function like stage business. Patsy swaggers and thrusts out his jaw; the Cuban bends his little finger delicately and later assumes a fencer’s posture; the bartender polishes compulsively until he appears frozen in bronze. These recurring details make the characters instantly legible and heighten the contrast between outward performance and actual self-command.

Figurative language is vivid, forceful, and frequently hyperbolic. Rage flashes “like sparks,” an insult crackles “like breaking glass,” fists become “knots of bone and hard flesh,” and intervening men collide with Patsy “like football players.” Such images translate emotion into bodily motion, sound, light, and impact. The narration often attributes monumental significance to trivial acts, using words such as “oration,” “portentous dignity,” “consummate display,” and “battleful” to parody masculine self-dramatization. At the same time, the imagery can turn abruptly animalistic: men snarl, cringe under laughter like lashes, threaten to stick one another like pigs, and display murderous, instinctive rage.

Dialogue dominates the story and carries its social texture. Patsy’s speech is rendered in dense eye dialect—“yeh,” “dat,” “wid,” “t’,” “ain’t”—with slang, repetition, threats, and compressed grammar reproducing a working-class New York street voice. The Cuban’s accented English is represented through errors and phonetic spellings such as “You have insult me,” “wis,” “keel,” and “s-s-satisfac-shone.” His rhetoric is ceremonious, melodramatic, and rooted in an aristocratic code of honour, whereas Patsy understands fighting as immediate bodily “scrap.” Much of the comedy arises from this collision of incompatible vocabularies and customs: sword-duelling and gentlemanly satisfaction confront fists, swagger, and saloon bravado. Neither man truly understands the other, but both recognize insult and masculine challenge.

Repetition drives both rhythm and characterization. Patsy continually insists that the Cuban “wants t’ scrap,” treating this single fact as sufficient explanation for everything. The Cuban repeatedly promises to kill or dissect him with a sword. These verbal loops show each man trapped inside a rigid code of honour. Sentence rhythms accelerate during confrontation through exclamations, interruptions, dashes, short paragraphs, and rapid exchanges, while longer descriptive sentences slow the action to frame gestures and reactions. The result is a controlled alternation between tableau and eruption.

The tone mixes amusement, satire, suspense, and a degree of admiration. Patsy is plainly ridiculous, but his willingness to accept a duel he cannot possibly survive gives his stupidity an oddly heroic integrity. The Cuban is elegant and self-possessed yet equally vain, volatile, and absurd. Even the supposedly sensible peacemakers become entertained, angry, and nearly drawn into the fight. No social group is granted complete superiority. The narrator instead exposes courage, honour, prejudice, and violence as collective performances sustained by spectators, insults, and fear of humiliation.

Structurally, the story is circular. It opens and closes with the same aphoristic description of Patsy, turning the saloon episode into proof of the paradox announced at the start. The escalation is carefully incremental: a careless remark leads to ceremonial outrage, then threats, attempted blows, agreement to duel, near-general combat, and police intervention. The anticlimactic arrest prevents the promised duel, allowing Patsy to preserve his heroic self-image without testing it. Overall, the style is urban, kinetic, visually concrete, dialogue-heavy, and sharply ironic, using caricature and mock-epic elevation to portray the dangerous comedy of masculine pride.

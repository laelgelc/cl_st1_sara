The story uses a brisk, conversational mode associated with early twentieth-century American magazine fiction. Its first-person narrator is not merely an observer but a self-conscious “copy-hunter” who openly searches the city for material. She addresses the reader directly, anticipates objections, comments on storytelling conventions, and turns the act of constructing a story into part of the story itself. This creates an intimate, performative voice: worldly, amused, intrusive, and knowingly theatrical.

The tone combines urban comedy, sentiment, and affectionate cynicism. Potentially melodramatic subjects—homesickness, exile, disgrace, and return—are repeatedly undercut by wisecracks, mundane details, and parodies of stock fiction. The narrator invokes misty eyes, prodigal children, villains, heroines, and dramatic reunions only to expose the gap between literary convention and ordinary behavior. Even sincere emotion is presented with a comic edge. The prevailing attitude is humane but unsentimental: people’s longings are treated seriously, while their romantic illusions are punctured.

The setting is rendered through dense, panoramic description. South Clark Street functions almost as a character: cosmopolitan, shabby, energetic, indiscriminate, and full of social contrasts. The prose builds the street through catalogues of newspapers, foods, businesses, ethnic signs, clothes, smells, buildings, and human types. Juxtaposition is a central technique. Tenements stand against marble hotels, enlistment officers against lodging-house vagrants, spaghetti against chop suey, and loan signs against lunch advertisements. This accumulation produces both journalistic vividness and comic abundance.

Description is highly figurative and often anthropomorphic. The street is compared to a slatternly woman dressed richly on the surface but ragged underneath; the city stamps and molds Mercedes in a “metallic cast”; money “nestles” beside a rib; newspapers from distant places sit beside one another like unlikely neighbors. Physical features are exaggerated into identifying emblems, especially the woman’s willow plume and the man’s Adam’s apple. Such visual shorthand gives the characters a caricatural immediacy while allowing those comic labels to acquire emotional significance.

The diction moves freely between polished narration, journalistic idiom, theatrical terminology, period slang, and colloquial speech. Expressions such as “good copy,” “girlie,” “gink,” “nix,” and “I’m on” anchor the story in an urban vernacular. Dialogue reproduces nonstandard grammar, clipped phrasing, regional rhythms, and social background. Mercedes speaks in a fast, slangy American idiom, while Guy’s more formal English repeatedly gives way to western directness. Tony’s hard-boiled patter is compressed and commercially distracted, often broken by exchanges with customers. Some ethnic and social labels are markedly period-specific and would now read as stereotyped or offensive.

The syntax is varied for comic and dramatic effect. Long cumulative sentences dominate descriptive passages, often using parallelism, repetition, and lists. Short declarative sentences then provide punch lines or narrative pivots: “And then this happened”; “They brought my story with them”; “Well, there you are.” Parenthetical remarks and mock asides interrupt the flow, reinforcing the narrator’s presence. Dashes, exclamations, rhetorical questions, direct addresses, and abrupt fragments give the prose the rhythm of spoken performance.

Characterization depends more on surfaces, gestures, possessions, and speech than on sustained psychological exposition. Mercedes is initially assembled from excessive urban signs—dyed hair, bright cosmetics, elaborate furs, high heels, jewelry, and the plume—while Guy is defined by ranch-bred movement and conspicuous physical features. These appearances invite easy moral conclusions, but the story complicates them. Mercedes momentarily assumes the predatory manner suggested by her costume, yet remains emotionally perceptive and capable of change. Guy’s aristocratic English nostalgia is similarly overturned by his attachment to ranch life. Their inner lives emerge through what they remember: bread dough, newspaper columns, silver tea services, bunkhouses, family clothing, horses, city lights, and familiar noises.

The plot is symmetrical and built around reversal. Two strangers approach the newsstand from opposite directions, each asks for a newspaper associated with an abandoned home, and each resolves to return. After a conspicuously announced one-year leap, they approach again from opposite directions and reveal that the journey home cured their homesickness by proving that they no longer belonged there. The final exchange repeats the opening situation with altered newspaper choices. The Kewaskum paper and the London paper are rejected in favor of Chicago and San Antonio papers, turning consumer choice into a concise statement of identity.

Dialogue carries most of the central action and thematic development. The initial conversation between the two exiles grows from simultaneous confession into mutual encouragement, while the later conversation mirrors and revises it. Their speeches are expansive, anecdotal, and crowded with domestic particulars. Emotion is strongest when expressed indirectly through concrete memories rather than abstract declarations. Homesickness appears in recollections of porch vines, bread pudding, roses, ranch skies, bad cooking, city trains, and everyday clothing.

The governing theme is that home is made by habit, usefulness, and present belonging rather than by birthplace or idealized memory. Distance enlarges the remembered past, while return makes it “shrink.” Both characters discover that they have been transformed by the places they once considered temporary or alien. The story therefore treats identity as adaptive and environmental: the city has fashioned Mercedes, and the American West has remade Guy. Its conclusion is not tragic exile but comic recognition. They belong where their routines, work, desires, and attachments have taken root.

Overall, the style is colorful, ironic, fast-moving, and audience-conscious. It blends urban reportage with comic caricature, sentimental plotting with anti-sentimental correction, and social observation with a neat magazine-story structure. The ending delivers the characteristic final snap: two newspaper titles silently resolve the characters’ emotional journeys and convert the newsstand’s cosmopolitan abundance into a metaphor for chosen home.

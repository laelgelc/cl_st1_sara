The story uses a retrospective first-person narrator whose confident social judgments are repeatedly corrected by experience. Her voice is observant, cultivated, witty, and self-aware, but also capable of impatience, condescension, and imaginative overreach. Much of the narrative energy comes from the gap between what she initially assumes and what she eventually understands. Her changing descriptions of Cavanelle—from “humbug” to “fool,” and finally to “angel”—create a gently ironic pattern while revealing the moral limitations of practical judgment.

The prose combines social realism with affectionate comedy. Concrete details of clothing, food, furnishings, transport, domestic economy, and neighborhood geography establish a vivid late-nineteenth-century New Orleans setting. Streetcars, cobblestones, the French market, a yellow one-story house, modest refreshments, worn coats, and half-bottles of wine give the story a strong material texture. These details often function economically as well as visually: Cavanelle’s shabby clothes and carefully rationed household pleasures measure the sacrifices he makes for others.

The diction is cosmopolitan and regionally specific, blending English with French expressions and Creole references. Phrases such as “voyez vous,” “des petits soins,” “petit ménage,” and “demi-verre” evoke the city’s Francophone culture without interrupting the narrative to explain it. Cavanelle’s dialogue is rendered phonetically through dropped consonants, altered verb forms, and French-inflected syntax—“I remark’,” “sen’ her,” “robust’”—which gives him an audible identity. His speech is expansive, ceremonious, flattering, and hyperbolic, reflecting both his occupation as a salesman and his emotional generosity. Mathilde’s languor and reserve are conveyed more through description than dialogue, making her a still point beside her brother’s restless animation.

Sentence structure varies between long, elaborately balanced reflections and short exclamatory judgments. Parenthetical qualifications, rhetorical questions, dashes, semicolons, and accumulative lists imitate the narrator’s shifting thoughts. Passages of comic speculation often swell into miniature fantasies, as when she imagines Mathilde being carried away by the wind or Cavanelle transformed by freedom into a healthier, better-dressed man with a waxed moustache. These flights of imagination are then undercut by reality, reinforcing the story’s irony. Repetition is used for emotional emphasis, especially in “Poor Cavanelle!” and the recurring phrase “des petits soins,” which becomes a motif for his instinctive devotion.

The characterization relies heavily on contrast. Cavanelle is energetic, mobile, talkative, and self-denying; Mathilde is frail, languid, quiet, and physically insubstantial. His grandiose claims about her voice clash sharply with the narrator’s experience of its thinness and inadequacy. This discrepancy produces situational and dramatic irony: the reader recognizes that Cavanelle’s praise is not fraud but love-shaped perception. The narrator’s practical assessment of Mathilde as talentless is probably accurate, yet the story gradually makes that accuracy seem morally incomplete.

The tone moves fluidly among amusement, satire, irritation, pity, and reverence. Comic descriptions can be pointed and even harsh, particularly in the narrator’s treatment of Mathilde’s singing and Cavanelle’s “fatuity,” but the comedy is ultimately softened by compassion. Death is presented through a deliberately clinical metaphor—the “skillful though merciless surgeon”—which exposes the narrator’s mistaken belief that Mathilde’s removal will liberate Cavanelle. The later revelation that he has simply transferred his care to Aunt Félicie overturns this expectation. Sacrifice is not merely an unfortunate burden imposed on him; it is his essential mode of being.

Structurally, the story is compact and circular. It begins with the narrator regarding Cavanelle as an “angel” because of his devotion to Mathilde, passes through disillusionment and the temptation to classify him as a fool, and ends with the same judgment of angelhood on broader grounds. The two meetings across the shop counter frame the intervening visit, death, absence, and return. This circular design shows that Cavanelle does not develop in the conventional sense; instead, the narrator’s understanding develops. The closing sentence delivers a concise moral reversal, converting apparent foolishness into secular sainthood.

Overall, the style is locally textured, psychologically alert, gently satirical, and morally ambivalent. It resists blunt sentimentality by filtering tenderness through irony and allowing the narrator’s prejudices to remain visible. Its central stylistic achievement is to make the same behavior appear ridiculous and sublime at once: Cavanelle’s blindness is comic, but his inexhaustible impulse to care for vulnerable people gives that blindness the character of grace.

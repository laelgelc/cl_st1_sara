The story uses an expansive, late nineteenth- or early twentieth-century literary style that blends sentimental fable, comic adventure, social satire, and melodrama. Its narration is omniscient and strongly authorial: the narrator not only enters the limited understanding of Romulus and Moses but also interprets events, passes moral judgment, addresses the reader, asks rhetorical questions, and occasionally withholds detail for effect. Phrases such as “I will not tell particularly” and direct appeals such as “ah, my friends” make the storyteller’s presence conspicuous. The perspective often rests close to Romulus, allowing ordinary landscapes and creatures to appear strange through the eyes of a captive ape, but it repeatedly widens into social and philosophical commentary.

The prose is formal, ornate, and periodically archaic. Its vocabulary favors elevated abstractions—“desolation,” “poignancy,” “chivalry,” “unencompassed,” “imperious magnificence”—alongside precise or unusual physical terms such as “vermicular,” “bole,” and “alacrity.” Long, cumulative sentences are common, with clauses linked by semicolons, dashes, repetitions, and parallel constructions. These are balanced by occasional short declarative sentences that mark transitions or sharpen irony: “Then the whole world lay before him”; “These two, then, were at large.” Lists and triadic rhythms give the narrative an oratorical quality, while recurring words and sentence patterns create thematic cohesion. “Freedom,” “world,” “grief,” “Christian,” and “little” function almost as verbal motifs.

Description is sensuous and strongly visual. Dust, heat, blue sky, foliage, sunset colors, mountain silhouettes, food aromas, and morning light establish a vivid Californian setting. The changing appearance of the Mount Hamilton observatory—from silver to copper, gold, and finally ebony—acts as a time marker and a visual refrain. Light and color frequently carry emotional meaning: the oppressive glare surrounding captivity gives way to the enlarged blue heavens of freedom, while the final yellow sunshine embodies the mother’s restored happiness. Nature is animated through personification, as when heaven has “windows,” the horizon expands, or the world seems to invite and overwhelm the newly liberated ape.

The tone moves fluidly between whimsical comedy and grave pathos. Romulus’s failed attempt to fly, the dog’s panic, Moses’s guffaws, and the pair’s chaotic acts of liberation are rendered with affectionate humor. At the same time, captivity is described in morally charged language as torture and servitude, and the burial of Wang Tai introduces grief, prejudice, death, and cultural displacement. Much of the humor is ironic rather than merely playful. Characters dismissed by society as an animal and an “idiot” become accidental rescuers, while the supposedly rational and civilized world is associated with cages, institutions, legal absurdity, cruelty, and exclusion. The repeated phrase “Christian law” is especially satirical, reducing religious and civic authority to the literal three feet of soil covering the child.

The plot has the shape of a moral fable or providential tale. It begins with one prisoner’s escape, expands into the freeing of a second prisoner, and culminates in the liberation of a third figure from death. This threefold pattern gives the action symbolic clarity. The early episodes, in which Romulus explores the world, appear episodic and comic, but they establish his curiosity, dexterity, fear of humans, and attraction to fellow captives; these traits later make the rescue plausible. Suspense depends on dramatic irony: readers understand the cemetery, burial customs, grave offerings, and apparent exhumation more fully than Romulus and Moses do. The climactic revelation—that Wang Tai was buried alive—is delayed until the closing paragraph, retrospectively transforming apparent desecration into salvation.

Characterization is largely external and emblematic. Romulus is alert, curious, resourceful, cautious, and instinctively compassionate. Moses is physically powerful, innocent, receptive, and childlike. Their bond is established without dialogue, through looks, gestures, touch, mimicry, and shared action. The observation that neither can speak “and hence neither could lie to the other” turns their wordlessness into moral purity. The mother of Wang Tai is portrayed less as an individualized personality than as an embodiment of universal maternal grief. Her emotional role anchors the sentimental ending and allows the narrative to move from ethnically marked description toward the assertion that “every mother has a mother’s heart.”

The story’s dominant themes are freedom, captivity, instinctive fellowship, the hypocrisy of civilization, and the possibility that innocence may achieve what institutional wisdom cannot. Physical confinement appears in several forms: the circus cage, the asylum fence, the grave, and the social marginalization of immigrant communities. Romulus and Moses are treated as inferior by conventional society, yet their sympathy and mischief produce an act of grace. Freedom is presented as intoxicating, expansive, and even dangerous, repeatedly associated with widened heavens, extended horizons, appetite, play, and disorder. The ending is deliberately bittersweet: the child and mother are reunited, but Romulus and Moses are whipped and returned to confinement, so private redemption occurs without broader social justice.

The narrative relies heavily on contrast and paradox. Animal instinct is set against human cruelty; supposed idiocy against practical salvation; Christian civilization against exclusion and shallow burial; mourning pomp against genuine grief; and criminal disorder against life-restoring action. Rhetorical questions invite the reader to participate in these reversals while also allowing the narrator to suggest motives that cannot be known directly. Exclamation marks, apostrophes, and moral asides intensify the theatrical, declamatory quality.

The treatment of ethnicity and disability reflects dated conventions. Terms such as “idiot,” repeated color-based descriptions, exoticized accounts of Chinese appearance and customs, and broad cultural generalizations would now be regarded as derogatory or reductive. At the same time, the story uses some of these conventions to criticize white Christian prejudice and to insist upon the humanity of socially despised figures. This produces a historically characteristic tension: the narrative’s humanitarian and anti-cruelty sympathies are expressed through language and stereotypes that remain paternalistic.

Overall, the style is that of a richly narrated humane satire: picturesque in setting, ceremonious in syntax, comic in incident, sentimental in emotional design, and sharply ironic in its treatment of civilized authority. Its signature effect comes from placing naïve, nonverbal consciousness inside an intrusive moral narrative, then using accidental comedy to expose social cruelty and deliver a providential final reversal.

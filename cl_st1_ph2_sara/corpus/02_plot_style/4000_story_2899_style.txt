The story uses a highly theatrical first-person voice that combines urbane self-possession, philosophical confidence, sardonic humour, and deliberate provocation. The narrator recounts events from within the scene, allowing his private thoughts, spoken remarks, and retrospective judgments to flow together. He remains poised and ironic while the visitor grows increasingly fearful, creating a comic contrast between calm scepticism and frantic alarm. The narration is strongly subjective: the storm is not merely described but interpreted as majestic, almost sublime, while the lightning-rod seller is filtered through the narrator’s amused suspicion and gradually recast as a grotesque impostor.

The diction is elevated, archaic, and consciously literary. Formal constructions such as “ere,” “hither,” “yon,” “know you not,” and “be so good as” coexist with technical vocabulary about conductivity, metals, electric currents, and lightning. Classical and biblical allusions—Jupiter Tonans, Olympus, Gibraltar, Tetzel, divine ordination, the numbered hairs of the head, and the rainbow as a sign of providence—give the domestic encounter mythic and theological dimensions. Elaborate titles and circumlocutions are used mock-heroically, especially when the salesman is called an “envoy extraordinary and minister plenipotentiary” of Jupiter. The prose often turns ordinary objects into symbolic emblems: the copper rod becomes a wand, sceptre, weapon, and counterfeit guarantee against fate.

Sentence structure alternates between long, cumulative descriptive periods and abrupt exclamations or commands. The opening sentence is expansive and rhythmically crowded, reproducing the rolling and splintering movement of thunder through repeated clauses, participles, and strong sound verbs. In the dialogue, this amplitude contracts into fragments—“Hark!”, “Awful!”, “The rug, the rug!”—which repeatedly interrupt rational discussion. Dashes, semicolons, colons, exclamation marks, rhetorical questions, and sudden interjections create an agitated oral texture. The salesman’s speech is particularly broken and breathless, whereas the narrator’s tends to remain syntactically controlled, ceremonious, and argumentative. This contrast in verbal rhythm functions as characterization.

The imagery is dense, physical, and hyperbolic. Thunder “boomed,” “crashed,” and becomes “Himalayas of concussions”; rain strikes the roof like spear-points; mountains seem to close around and tumble into the cottage. Visual motifs of blackness, indigo, copper, green glass, lightning, and rainbow light bind the human figures to the weather. The visitor’s eyes contain an “innocuous sort of lightning,” and his darkening face mirrors the storm, making him appear both supernatural and faintly demonic. Similes and metaphors are often extravagant, drawing on warfare, geology, religion, commerce, and classical mythology. Personification turns the storm into an active dramatic presence whose peals punctuate and comment upon the exchange.

The story is structured almost entirely as a verbal duel in a confined setting. Minimal action is interspersed with long stretches of dialogue, and the plot advances through competing attempts to control where the narrator stands, what he touches, and what he believes. Repetition supplies both cohesion and comedy: the salesman continually cries “Hark,” invokes danger, advertises the rod’s price, and renews his demand for a purchase. His technical explanations initially sound authoritative but accumulate into absurdity, since almost every feature of ordinary life is declared hazardous. The narrator counters with questions, irony, strategic disobedience, and increasingly direct challenges. The storm intensifies alongside the sales pitch, then clears just as the narrator delivers his climactic denunciation.

The tone blends the sublime, comic, satirical, Gothic, and sermonic. The storm supplies genuine grandeur and menace, but the salesman’s terror and opportunism repeatedly undercut it. Humour arises from inflated politeness, mock reverence, literal-minded questioning, escalating prohibitions, and the mismatch between the visitor’s promise of safety and his own conspicuous cowardice. Gothic details—the drenched stranger, hollow knocking, sunken eyes, blackened face, forked rod, and violent final lunge—make him uncanny, yet the dominant purpose is satirical rather than horrific.

Characterization is emblematic rather than psychologically subtle. The lightning-rod man represents fear-based commerce, manipulative expertise, and the attempt to monetize human anxiety by promising control over uncontrollable forces. His patter combines warning, technical jargon, anecdotal evidence, attacks on competitors, appeals to authority, repetition of price, and lurid images of death. The narrator embodies independence, hospitality hardened into resistance, religious trust, and suspicion of professional alarmism. Their conflict is therefore simultaneously commercial, intellectual, moral, and theological.

The ending shifts from dramatic confrontation to explicit allegorical generalization. After the rod is seized, broken, trampled, and thrown out, the narrator observes that the salesman still “dwells in the land” and prospers through human fear. This closing move broadens one peculiar encounter into a durable social critique. Overall, the style is rhetorically ornate, symbolically charged, aggressively dialogic, and satirically didactic, using a storm-bound domestic episode to dramatize the struggle between fear and confidence, technical promises and providence, coercive salesmanship and individual judgment.

The story adopts the polished, leisurely manner of a late nineteenth-century comic romance, combining a country-house courtship plot with an absurdly bureaucratic treatment of the supernatural. Its first-person narrator is educated, decorous, self-conscious, and timid. He recounts events retrospectively, presenting his romantic indecision in elevated language that makes his weakness both sympathetic and ridiculous. Madeline is habitually idealised through possessive and sentimental phrasing, while the narrator’s fear of rejection and of her irascible uncle is analysed at disproportionate length. This sustained self-scrutiny creates gentle irony: he considers himself prudent and sensitive, but the reader sees that his evasiveness repeatedly produces the very disasters he hopes to avoid.

The prose is formal and syntactically expansive. Long, carefully balanced sentences accumulate qualifications, subordinate clauses, and parallel phrases, often delaying the main point in a way that reflects the narrator’s reluctance to act. Descriptions favour cultivated period diction such as “abode,” “bosky shades,” “apparition,” “avowal,” “intercourse,” and “predicament.” The opening landscape is presented through a flowing catalogue of pastoral attractions—lawns, trees, flowers, games, rides, and fishing—before all are subordinated to the attraction of Madeline. Such descriptive passages establish an idyllic romantic setting, but they also carry a faintly parodic excess. Euphemistic or over-elaborate expressions, including “ante-interrogatory period of love,” “future collateral existence,” and “general views on the subject,” comically disguise straightforward emotions and intentions. The narrator’s inability to say plainly that he wishes to propose is therefore embedded in the language itself.

The central stylistic device is deadpan incongruity. The ghost is not frightening in the conventional Gothic sense; instead, he discusses “positions,” “responsibilities,” “applications,” “vacancies,” and “transfers” as though ghosthood were an oversubscribed profession administered by an unseen institution. Supernatural absurdities are accepted with conversational seriousness, and neither narrator nor ghost dwells on their metaphysical implications. The premise is inverted for comic effect: rather than a living man being haunted by a ghost, the premature ghost complains that the still-living Mr. Hinckman “haunts” him. This reversal is typical of the story’s humour, which arises less from overt jokes than from logical, practical reasoning applied to an impossible situation.

Dialogue occupies much of the narrative and supplies its pace, exposition, and comic timing. The ghost speaks volubly, with anxious repetitions, rhetorical questions, exclamations, and increasingly elaborate explanations. His garrulous confidence contrasts with the narrator’s hesitation and with Madeline’s quieter, more pointed remarks. The dialogue also exploits asymmetrical perception: the narrator can hear the ghost while Madeline cannot, so his replies are repeatedly misdirected and misinterpreted. These scenes resemble drawing-room farce, with misunderstandings produced by overheard fragments, concealed interlocutors, ambiguous pronouns, and badly timed entrances. The ghost functions simultaneously as supernatural confidant, unwanted chaperone, comic adviser, and obstacle to the romance.

The tone remains genial, whimsical, and urbane even when the narrator invokes horror, despair, or lifelong misery. Emotional language is deliberately hyperbolic—an awkward exchange becomes “the end of the world,” and romantic embarrassment “blackened” the narrator’s whole life—yet the narrative voice remains controlled enough to expose the exaggeration. Gothic conventions such as moonlight, sleeplessness, an indistinct figure, and a haunted chamber are introduced only to be domesticated. The ghost sits in chairs, lounges on porch railings, yawns, seeks conversation, and worries about being scolded. Suspense is consequently comic rather than terrifying: the danger lies in social humiliation, failed courtship, and the return of an angry uncle.

Structurally, the story is linear and economical beneath its leisurely surface. The pastoral introduction establishes the romance and the uncle as its obstacle; the nocturnal visitation introduces the supernatural complication; two subsequent encounters entangle the ghost more tightly with the courtship; and the final scene resolves both plots at once. Repetition creates escalation: each time the narrator approaches a declaration, the ghost appears and interferes more disastrously. The ghost’s desire for a legitimate “ghostship” runs parallel to the narrator’s desire for Madeline, so both characters seek release from suspended, improper states. This parallel enables the compact double resolution in the closing exchange.

The ending depends on verbal ambiguity and precise comic timing. When the ghost announces that he has finally become “the real ghost of somebody,” the narrator cries, “I would to heaven you were mine!” Grammatically addressed to the ghost but emotionally interpretable as a declaration to Madeline, the sentence allows her to answer, “I am yours.” The conclusion converts the story’s recurring problem—speech addressed to one listener but understood by another—into the mechanism of romantic success. Its abruptness is deliberate: after pages of circumlocution, the desired proposal occurs accidentally in one ambiguous sentence, and Madeline’s concise reply supplies the emotional directness the narrator has lacked throughout.

The story uses a polished comic-realistic style built around an urbane, lightly intrusive third-person narrator. The point of view stays chiefly close to Katie, presenting events through her shyness, modest expectations and emotional loyalties, while retaining enough narrative distance for irony. The narrator moves easily between sympathetic psychological observation and dry social commentary, as in the generalisations about ferry-boat etiquette, thwarted lovers and the behaviour expected of kings. This voice treats even absurd developments with formal composure, allowing the humour to emerge from the contrast between dignified narration and improbable circumstances.

The prose combines literary elegance with energetic American vernacular. Descriptive and expository passages use balanced, often elaborately shaped sentences, precise comic comparisons and mock-heroic phrasing. Katie imagines herself as a minor figure beside a “hero” and “heroine”; Ted is likened to a fairy prince, a messenger boy and a glover’s assistant; ordinary romantic difficulties are framed in terms of fate, royal alliances and legendary elopements. Such comparisons repeatedly elevate mundane life only to puncture the elevation with practical detail. Hyperbole is frequent but controlled: Genevieve is “a destroyer of masculine peace of mind,” New York becomes an oven, and Ted is treated as an athletic sovereign. Parenthetical qualifications and sudden understated corrections sharpen the wit.

Dialogue is central to characterization and social setting. Ted speaks in terse, repetitive slang—“I’m on the level,” “Nothing doing,” “See?”—with colloquial grammar, direct questions and emphatic assertions. His speech establishes him as earnest, uncomplicated and action-oriented rather than verbally sophisticated. Katie’s language is more restrained, correct and conciliatory, reflecting her self-effacement and habit of caring for others. Genevieve, Mr Murdoch and the Tennessee Bear-Cat each receive a distinct sociolect, from affected pseudo-English pronunciation to sporting slang. The grandfather’s regal declarations combine elevated assumptions with domestic helplessness, creating comedy without stripping him of vulnerability. Speech therefore carries class, temperament and emotional intention more strongly than explicit authorial explanation does.

Sentence rhythm varies according to function. Dialogue scenes proceed in short exchanges, often with clipped tags and strategic pauses, giving courtship and conflict a brisk theatrical quality. Narrative transitions expand into longer, syntactically balanced sentences that summarise weeks or months, explain motives or offer amused commentary. Repetition is used both emotionally and comically: Ted reiterates his honesty, the grandfather repeats that the marriage “wouldn’t do,” and royal phrases echo between characters. Lists and accumulating alternatives also create momentum, especially in descriptions of Coney Island, Ted’s credentials and the succession of the grandfather’s delusions.

The story is carefully paced around a sequence of comic reversals. It begins with the surprising selection of unassuming Katie over glamorous Genevieve, develops rapidly through courtship and engagement, then introduces the grandfather’s delusion as the central obstacle. After several failed solutions, the narrative slows into separation and seasonal passage before Ted’s return produces the final reversal. Earlier details—his athletic celebrity, the Mardi Gras election, Katie’s ignorance of Coney Island and the grandfather’s uncertain grasp of geography—are gathered into the resolution. The ending functions as both a romantic payoff and an extended comic explanation, with Ted rationalising his deception until it almost sounds constitutionally valid.

Characterization depends on vivid shorthand, recurring verbal habits and incongruous details. Genevieve is established through beauty, vanity and pronunciation; the Bear-Cat through his broken nose, nickname and elaborate fighting scheme; Ted through his grave face, chewing gum, athletic reputation and solemn directness. Katie is drawn more inwardly. Her habitual repression, loyalty and sense of insignificance make the romance emotionally credible, while her tactful management of her grandfather reveals resilience beneath her passivity. The narration is affectionate toward these characters, even when exposing their vanity, ignorance or eccentricity.

Humour arises chiefly from incongruity, understatement and literal-minded problem solving. A deluded King of England objects to a commoner; Ted answers not by curing the delusion but by finding a technically compatible kingship. Romantic convention is repeatedly translated into the language of working-class New York, sport and commerce. The elevated idea of a “royal alliance” rests on a popularity contest at Coney Island, while Ted’s conscience is eased by comparing amusement-park kingship with European monarchy. The comedy remains warm rather than cruel: the grandfather’s condition creates real hardship, Katie’s sacrifice carries genuine pathos, and Ted’s fraud is presented as an ingenious act of devotion.

The setting is rendered through selective, socially evocative detail rather than dense description. Ferry-boats, amusement parks, Sixth Avenue, Washington Square, athletic clubs, saloons, barrel organs and summer heat create an early-twentieth-century New York of shopkeepers, models, sportsmen and crowded public entertainments. Place is not merely decorative: Coney Island supplies both the story’s popular energy and its decisive pun on kingship. Overall, the style blends sentimental romance, urban comedy, social observation and farcical plotting, maintaining a tone that is brisk, humane, ironic and consistently alert to the comic grandeur of ordinary lives.

Mr. Tymperley, once a comfortably situated gentleman, loses nearly all his money through a disastrous investment recommended by his wealthy friend Mr. Charman. After Charman dies, Tymperley conceals the truth from the widow to avoid blaming her husband and retreats into extreme poverty in Islington, where he begins learning bookbinding to support himself.

When Mrs. Charman unexpectedly finds him, his shame leads him to claim that he lives among the poor by choice as a charitable social worker. His old friends admire his supposed self-sacrifice, while he struggles to maintain the deception and survives on almost nothing.

Mrs. Charman’s daughter, Mrs. Weare, sends him a five-pound cheque to distribute among the needy. Desperately needing clothes and boots, Tymperley begins rationalizing that he might use the money himself, especially since her father caused his ruin. After buying boots with his own scarce funds, however, he recognizes how close poverty has brought him to dishonesty. He entrusts the donation to a clergyman, then writes to Mrs. Weare confessing his financial ruin, his false story, and his near moral failure. He asks her forgiveness and says he hopes his new trade will allow him to live more securely.

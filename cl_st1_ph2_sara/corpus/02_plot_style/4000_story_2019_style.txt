The story adopts the form of an antiquarian ghost tale presented as an edited documentary record. Its narrator is not the principal sufferer but a scholarly intermediary who has acquired, arranged and abridged another man’s papers. This framing voice is restrained, rational and mildly sceptical, repeatedly distinguishing evidence, deduction and conjecture. References to manuscripts, calendars, bibliographies, historical commissions, architecture and local customs create an appearance of factual exactness. The eventual explanation of how the papers were found closes the narrative frame and gives the story the shape of a recovered case history.

The prose is formal, cultivated and distinctly period-conscious. Its vocabulary combines antiquarian, architectural and bibliographical terms with the practical language of travel writing: “sarcophagi,” “coronae,” “effigies,” “demesne,” “account-books,” routes, accommodation and distances. Swedish and Latin words are introduced, translated or qualified as a conscientious researcher might handle them. Invented names and altered place names are treated with scrupulous transparency, strengthening the illusion that the narrator is protecting real people and locations. Parenthetical qualifications—“as I judge,” “it seems,” “I gather,” “probably”—make the narration sound cautious rather than sensational.

Sentence structure is generally long, balanced and syntactically layered. Descriptions accumulate through coordinated clauses, embedded qualifications and lists of concrete details. The narrator frequently corrects, narrows or comments upon his own statements, producing the cadence of a lecturer or editor thinking responsibly in public. Short sentences are reserved for emphasis, especially when a threat becomes immediate: “He had seen them before.” This contrast allows moments of horror to strike sharply against the measured background.

The narrative moves through several levels of voice. The outer narrator summarizes and interprets Wraxall’s papers; Wraxall’s journals supply first-person immediacy; conversations with the innkeeper and deacon introduce local testimony; and the final village recollection shifts into colloquial dialect. Each register has a distinct function. The editorial voice confers authority, the journal exposes a mind gradually losing control, oral folklore supplies warnings, and dialect makes the final atrocity feel embedded in communal memory. Dialogue is used sparingly and tends to be asymmetrical: Wraxall presses for knowledge while local people hesitate, evade or offer incomplete warnings.

Characterization is indirect and economical. Wraxall is reconstructed from documentary traces as intelligent, solitary, cultivated and dangerously inquisitive. His chief fault is established early, then dramatized through repeated acts of trespass: questioning reluctant witnesses, searching private texts, taking the mausoleum key and contemplating opening the coffin. His habit of speaking aloud gives his curiosity a ritual quality, since casual remarks to the dead begin to resemble invitations. Count Magnus is characterized through fragments rather than direct appearance: a portrait, estate records, legends of cruelty, an occult note, engraved scenes and the physical security of his coffin. The supernatural companion is even less fully shown, being reduced to a hood, a cloak and an inhuman appendage. Withholding complete description preserves its force.

The setting is rendered topographically before it becomes threatening. The route between manor, lake, wood, churchyard and inn is carefully mapped, so later movements occur in a space the reader already understands. Architectural description initially resembles leisurely travel literature, but ominous details are planted within it: the Last Judgement, smiling demons, the sealed mausoleum, the black copper roof and the isolated northern entrance. Beauty and historical interest gradually become contaminated by dread. Twilight, woods, churchyards and enclosed interiors are used conventionally, yet the exact geographical and material detail prevents them from feeling abstractly Gothic.

The story’s horror develops by slow accretion rather than overt spectacle. Early warnings appear as minor irregularities: evasive answers, disturbing historical references, ambiguous inscriptions and grotesque ornament. The innkeeper’s embedded tale supplies a concentrated preview of the danger, particularly through the contrast between a victim who screams and an inhuman pursuer who laughs. Thereafter the loosening padlocks create a simple numerical countdown. Wraxall’s repeated unconscious return to the mausoleum suggests supernatural influence without explicitly confirming it. When the coffin opens, the decisive event is reported through sensory fragments and failures of memory rather than a clear revelation.

Repetition is a major structural device. Wraxall twice loses awareness of his surroundings, repeatedly addresses Count Magnus and repeatedly counts his fellow-passengers. The two pursuers recur as stable visual motifs: a tall man in an old-fashioned broad hat and a short hooded figure. The mismatch between twenty-eight figures seen and twenty-six passengers attending meals turns bureaucratic enumeration into a source of terror. Counting, cataloguing and note-taking—normally instruments of rational control—become evidence that reality can no longer be contained by rational systems.

Pacing is deliberately gradual. A substantial opening establishes provenance, biography and historical background before the supernatural plot fully emerges. Exposition is disguised as editorial necessity or scholarly curiosity. The middle alternates archival discoveries, folklore and visits to the tomb, each stage intensifying the previous one. After the final padlock falls, time compresses: the return journey is reduced to anxious notebook entries, the pursuit crosses national boundaries, and the death is conveyed retrospectively. This acceleration mirrors Wraxall’s collapse from composed researcher into a frightened, fragmentary witness.

The tonal method depends on understatement and irony. The narrator often describes ominous matters in dry, courteous or faintly humorous language, calling terrible material “picturesque,” noting Wraxall’s wasted scholarly space, or apologetically assessing his curiosity. The gap between calm expression and dreadful implication intensifies unease. Even the climactic death is not directly represented. Instead, the reader receives an inquest, fainting jurors, silence and the evasive verdict “visitation of God.” The most disturbing facts remain just outside the documentary record.

The central themes are forbidden knowledge, historical guilt, curiosity as transgression and the persistence of evil across time. The past is not safely confined to archives, portraits or tombs; scholarship can reactivate what it studies. Wraxall’s error is less disbelief than aesthetic detachment: he treats cruelty, magic and legend as material for an attractive book until the material answers him. The story also undermines positivist confidence. Manuscripts, eyewitness accounts and numerical records provide abundant evidence, but none can deliver a complete explanation or effective defence.

Overall, the style combines scholarly realism, travelogue, local legend and Gothic horror. Its signature effect comes from placing extraordinary events inside an apparatus of ordinary documentation, then allowing small discrepancies to erode that apparatus. A successful imitation would require a reserved editorial narrator, precise historical and architectural detail, nested documents and testimonies, an inquisitive but credible protagonist, repeated motifs that gather menace, restrained supernatural description, and a conclusion that confirms the horror indirectly while preserving a residue of uncertainty.

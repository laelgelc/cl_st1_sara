Norman Hastings is abruptly transported from a sunny afternoon in 1954 to a strange room in 2004, apparently young, naked, and with no memory of the intervening fifty years. A letter reveals that the room contains a time machine invented by Norman himself at age seventy-five. Rather than moving objects through time, the machine reverses their physical state: Norman has restored his body and mind to age twenty-five, erasing all memories acquired afterward.

The letter tells him that his parents are dead, that his fiancée Barbara became his wife but died after three years, and that he has a now-middle-aged son. It also asks him to decide the invention’s fate. The machine could rejuvenate the elderly and cure diseases, effectively granting immortality, but widespread use would cause catastrophic overpopulation before humanity can colonize other worlds or responsibly limit births.

Norman concludes that the machine is too valuable to destroy and too dangerous to reveal. He must keep it secret until humanity is ready. If the world remains unprepared in another fifty years, he will rejuvenate himself again, lose his memories again, and leave another letter guiding his younger self to the same decision. As the room unlocks, he contemplates an apparently endless cycle of renewed youth, forgotten lives, repeated grief, and guardianship of the secret.

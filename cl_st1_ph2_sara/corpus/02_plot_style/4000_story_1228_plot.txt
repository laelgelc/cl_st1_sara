During the American Revolution, Abraham Duke leaves to join the colonial army after secretly hiding a large inheritance of gold. Refusing to tell his wife Catherine its location, he leaves instructions that she may receive a sealed letter from the parson only if he dies. Just before his departure, his friend Joseph Evarts, a former goldsmith, is found murdered with a sword wound. Because Abraham was the last person seen visiting Evarts, suspicion quietly falls on him.

While Abraham is away, Catherine obsessively searches the house and farm for the gold, driven less by greed than by resentment that her husband did not trust her. Months later, after noticing signs of intruders near Evarts’s deserted house, she is tortured and murdered by robbers seeking the treasure, though they apparently fail to find it.

Abraham returns from the war missing an arm and discovers his wife dead and his farm ruined. As the years pass, the community increasingly suspects him of Evarts’s murder and shuns him. Impoverished, crippled, and isolated, he refuses to use or reveal the hidden wealth and eventually freezes and starves to death beside his empty hearth.

Harry Evarts, Joseph’s grown son, finds Abraham’s body holding a letter addressed to him. The letter reveals that the gold had never been hidden in an ordinary place: Joseph had fashioned it to resemble brass fittings throughout the house—the andirons, fire tools, furniture handles, clock ornaments and pendulum, and other fixtures. Abraham had lived and died surrounded by his fortune, unwilling to expose its secret even to save himself.

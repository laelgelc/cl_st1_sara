During a winter vacation, college senior and aspiring author Eustace Bright visits an older writer at his country cottage. After touring the snowy property, they stop at a ruined rustic summer house, whose dreamlike atmosphere reminds Eustace of telling classical myths to children. He reveals that he has written six new adaptations and asks his host to serve again as his editor.

Back in the cottage, the narrator reviews the manuscripts and agrees to the proposal. He worries that the violence, sorrow, and moral darkness of ancient myths make them unsuitable for young readers, but Eustace argues that such elements are later corruptions. By imaginatively addressing innocent children, he believes, a storyteller can restore the legends to the pure, wondrous forms they possessed in humanity’s childhood. The narrator remains skeptical of Eustace’s confidence and extensive alterations, yet concedes that he has overcome the stories’ moral difficulties.

Eustace recounts how enthusiastically the Tanglewood children received the tales in settings ranging from woods and lakesides to a palace made of snow. He also reports on the children’s growth and health, and describes his own plans to graduate and perhaps continue exploring classical myths as children’s stories. After accepting the role of editor, the narrator closes by expressing affection for the children and regret that he may never chronicle them again.

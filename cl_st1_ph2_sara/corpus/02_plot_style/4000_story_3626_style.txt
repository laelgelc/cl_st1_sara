The story uses a first-person frame narrative that presents an apparently casual railway encounter before yielding to an extended story within the story. The outer narrator is observant, courteous, and initially trusting; the stranger’s embedded narrative dominates the text, while the conductor’s explanation at the end abruptly reinterprets everything that has preceded it. This layered construction creates a classic deadpan comic pattern: an improbable account is delivered with accumulating circumstantial detail, accepted in earnest by the listener, and finally punctured by a rationalizing twist.

The tone combines mock solemnity, macabre humor, and sustained irony. The snowbound journey is initially rendered as genuine disaster fiction, with ominous weather, deepening hunger, and emotionally heightened descriptions of despair. Once cannibalism is proposed, however, the narrative treats it not as savage chaos but as orderly legislative business. Parliamentary nominations, committees, amendments, ballots, caucuses, points of procedure, and formal objections are applied to the selection of human meals. The humor rests on this radical mismatch between horrific subject matter and respectable civic procedure. Euphemistic expressions such as “elected,” “choice,” “breakfast,” and “supper” further suppress the violence and make the episode more grotesquely comic.

The diction alternates between elevated, melodramatic description and bureaucratic or political jargon. The storm passages employ expansive, picturesque language—prairies become a “vast white desert,” drifts resemble “colossal graves,” and snow moves like spray from a stormy sea. These images evoke nineteenth-century adventure writing and create an atmosphere of authentic peril. By contrast, the debate scenes rely on formulas such as “I move,” “the report was accepted,” “the previous question,” and “under the rules.” Capitalized names, state affiliations, procedural summaries, and transcript-like speaker labels imitate official congressional records. This documentary precision lends absurd events a false air of authority.

Sentence structure is similarly variable. The descriptive sections favor long, elaborately coordinated sentences, accumulative lists, parenthetical qualifications, and emphatic punctuation. Dashes frequently mark pauses, intensifications, corrections, or abrupt turns of thought, while exclamation marks heighten both danger and comic theatricality. Short fragments—“Another dawning,” “and hunger,” “the fifth!”—compress the passage of time and dramatize physical decline. In the political debate, sentences become rhetorically patterned and oratorical, using repetition, parallelism, escalating questions, and triadic phrasing. The speech opposing the Oregon candidate, for example, expands into a torrent of insults whose grandiloquence is wildly disproportionate to the practical question of whether the man would make a satisfactory meal.

Dialogue is central to characterization and pacing. The stranger speaks with gentleness, sincerity, and formal courtesy even when describing cannibalism, never signaling awareness that his subject is appalling. His calm evaluations of the victims—judging them for tenderness, bulk, flavor, or “delicacy of fiber”—produce comic horror through understatement. The outer narrator’s attempted interruptions provide brief reality checks, but the stranger repeatedly suppresses them with polite firmness. His final declaration of affection is especially effective because “liking” someone has acquired a lethal double meaning. The conductor’s colloquial explanation then contrasts sharply with the stranger’s polished rhetoric, grounding the conclusion in plain speech.

Characterization is achieved less through psychological analysis than through verbal manner. The stranger’s mild appearance, soft voice, earnestness, and political fluency make his mania initially credible. The outer narrator functions as a comic straight man: his good manners prevent him from challenging the tale, and his growing alarm guides the reader’s response. The many stranded passengers are deliberately thinly individualized, appearing chiefly as names, regional labels, political speakers, and prospective dishes. This reduction of people to entries in a legislative record is itself part of the satire.

The story’s pacing depends on escalation and repetition. A relatively leisurely opening establishes plausibility; the storm narrative then tightens through a chronological sequence of nights and days. The first cannibalistic election is narrated at great length, allowing procedural complications to multiply. Subsequent victims are dispatched more rapidly in catalogues, producing an accelerating rhythm and suggesting the compulsive, rehearsed nature of the stranger’s monologue. The final revelation is delayed until after his departure, preserving tension while also allowing the narrator’s fear of becoming the next “choice” to peak.

The dominant comic methods are incongruity, parody, bathos, understatement, hyperbole, and delayed revelation. The text parodies legislative debate, official minutes, survival narratives, sentimental reminiscence, and gastronomic criticism at once. Lofty appeals to honor, fairness, and constitutional procedure repeatedly collapse into questions of fleshiness and toughness. Sentimental remarks about writing to a victim’s wife or remembering a man with affection become horrifying because they are paired with culinary appraisal. The ending adds a final recursive joke: in the stranger’s complete version, he is unanimously elected as the last meal but procedurally resigns, explaining his survival through the same parliamentary logic that governs the fantasy.

Overall, the style is ornate yet controlled, outwardly earnest, and structurally engineered around deadpan misdirection. Its distinctive effect comes from refusing to alter register when events become monstrous. Political decorum, rhetorical grandeur, and social politeness remain intact throughout, transforming cannibalism into an administrative matter and turning institutional procedure itself into the principal object of satire.

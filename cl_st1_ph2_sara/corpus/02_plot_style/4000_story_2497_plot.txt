In a winter North Atlantic gale, the poorly disciplined crew of the lumber ship Francis Spaight loses control of the vessel. It capsizes onto its side, killing the mate and two sailors, but Mahoney and fifteen-year-old O’Brien cut away the masts and enable the waterlogged ship to right itself.

Thirteen survivors endure days without food and with little fresh water. As hunger, exposure, and painful sores weaken them, the men become increasingly cruel, especially toward the spirited O’Brien. After sixteen days, the captain proposes killing one person for food. Despite Mahoney’s objections, the men restrict the drawing of lots to the four boys. The process is manipulated so that O’Brien is declared the loser.

The terrified cook, Gorman, is forced to kill him. Attempts to draw blood from O’Brien’s arms fail because his veins are nearly empty from dehydration, but the starving men finally overpower the boy and compel Gorman to cut his throat. Immediately afterward, Mahoney spots a ship approaching to rescue them. The men throw the collected blood overboard and conceal O’Brien’s body beneath a tarpaulin. As the rescue boat arrives, Gorman collapses into escalating, maniacal laughter.

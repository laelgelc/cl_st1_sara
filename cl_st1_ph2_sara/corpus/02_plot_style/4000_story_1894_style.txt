The story is a comic maritime yarn told in the first person by an elderly night watchman recalling his years at sea. Its dominant stylistic feature is the narrator’s strongly marked working-class seafaring dialect, represented through dropped initial consonants, phonetic spellings and colloquial grammar: “’ave,” “’im,” “wot,” “arter,” “ag’in,” “six ’undered,” and “we was.” This eye dialect establishes voice, period and social setting immediately. It also makes the tale sound spoken rather than written, as though it were being delivered informally to a listener. Recurrent formulas such as “I says,” “says Bill,” and “then I see” reinforce the rhythm of oral storytelling. The narrator’s grammar is nonstandard, but his account is clear, controlled and shrewd; the contrast prevents the dialect from implying intellectual simplicity.

The narrative voice is genial, candid and morally compromised. The watchman presents greed as a universal weakness while speaking as if he were merely an amused observer, even though he quickly joins Bill’s scheme and demands half the supposed fortune. His opening claim that sailors are seldom miserly prepares an ironic story in which nearly everyone becomes obsessed with money. He habitually softens wrongdoing with comic euphemism and false sentiment. Bill’s attempt to steal the money becomes a “little game,” while threats and searches are framed as concern for “the pore boy.” This gap between what the men say and what they mean is central to the humour. The narrator rarely condemns anyone directly; instead, character is exposed through behaviour, dialogue and self-serving rationalisation.

The prose is plain, economical and event-driven. Descriptive passages are sparse, with only enough nautical detail to make the setting tangible: the barque, fo’c’sle, companion-way, wheel, watches, bunks, dirty weather and East India Docks. Technical language is introduced naturally through the sailors’ experience rather than explained for an outsider. Figurative language is similarly concrete and comic. Getting down the companion-way unseen is compared to removing a man’s false teeth without his noticing, while the unseen hand that supposedly tickles the cook is “the size of a leg o’ mutton.” Such comparisons are domestic, bodily and exaggerated, matching the narrator’s practical imagination.

Dialogue carries most of the characterization and much of the plot. Exchanges are brief, quick and adversarial, with characters constantly bargaining, threatening, teasing or trying to outwit one another. Bill is blustering, greedy and transparently manipulative; Jimmy answers him with literal-minded cheek and increasing confidence; the second mate’s vanity is revealed by how readily he accepts Bill’s absurd flattery. Repetition is used for comic escalation, especially in disputes over whether the money was thrown overboard, where it is hidden, and who can trust whom. Speech often contains a surface politeness that barely conceals hostility. Bill calls Jimmy “my lad” while intimidating him, and Jimmy negotiates shares while insulting Bill’s appearance and intelligence.

The humour is predominantly ironic, situational and character-based. It arises from greed making supposedly experienced adults foolish, while the apparently vulnerable boy remains several moves ahead of them. There is also sustained dramatic irony: Bill and the narrator believe they are manipulating Jimmy, but their increasingly frantic conduct suggests that Jimmy is manipulating them. The story repeatedly converts morally dubious behaviour into farce. A deathbed scene leads not to solemn reflection but to calculations about banknotes; a nocturnal search becomes a misunderstanding about tickling the cook; and Bill’s attempt to obtain the mattress requires an extravagantly sentimental speech about wanting to remember the second mate every night. The understated narration heightens these moments because absurd events are reported with relative seriousness.

Structurally, the tale follows a compact chain of reversals. Thomas’s determination to take his savings with him creates the initial mystery. Jimmy’s claim to have thrown the packet overboard appears to end the matter, but Bill suspects him; Jimmy then admits that he kept it, only to claim that he hid it in the second mate’s mattress. Bill’s ingenious purchase of the mattress seems to promise success, but the final search reveals nothing, and Jimmy disappears. Each apparent solution creates a fresh complication, allowing suspense and comedy to rise together. The repeated searches, threats and failed schemes form a pattern of mounting desperation, while the ship’s approach to London supplies a natural deadline.

Pacing is leisurely at the outset, where the narrator establishes Thomas’s miserliness and illness, then accelerates after the deathbed revelation. From that point, scenes are linked by practical questions—whether Jimmy has the money, where he has hidden it, how the men can reach the mattress and how they can exclude him from the division. Short dialogue exchanges quicken the movement, while summary passages compress the week-long searches and the voyage toward London. The final dockside sequence slows slightly to prolong expectation before delivering the reversal.

Characterization is economical and caricatural without being flat. Thomas is defined by lifelong miserliness, Bill by greedy ingenuity, Jimmy by alertness and nerve, and the second mate by vanity mixed with slyness. Minor figures receive memorable traits in a phrase or two: Walter Jones believes himself medically knowledgeable because his family is always ill, and the cook is “a terrible ticklish man.” These concise tags make the shipboard community vivid and provide material for later jokes. No character possesses clear moral authority. Thomas wastes his life hoarding, Bill and the narrator plot theft, Jimmy is probably a trickster, and the second mate may understand more than he reveals.

The ending is deliberately suggestive rather than explanatory. Jimmy’s disappearance strongly implies that he has escaped with the money, but the second mate’s “kind smile” leaves open the possibility that he discovered it or participated in the deception. The narrator does not resolve the mystery; he closes on Bill trying unsuccessfully to smile back at the mate. This visual image supplies both humiliation and uncertainty. It also preserves the oral-yarn quality, since the pleasure lies less in establishing legal truth than in showing greed punished by bafflement.

Overall, the style combines vernacular narration, compact plotting, nautical realism and dry comic irony. Its moral method is indirect: rather than preach against greed, it lets greed generate suspicion, humiliation and self-defeat. The tone remains amused and unsentimental even around death and attempted theft, producing a characteristic mixture of geniality, cynicism and farce.

The story uses a realist, humane, late nineteenth-century mode, combining careful social observation with a sentimental moral argument. Its prose is formal and slightly old-fashioned, with period vocabulary, Russian names and institutions, precise monetary details, and translated idioms that preserve the atmosphere of Imperial Russia. Concrete references to uniforms, streets, ranks, examinations, food prices, and bureaucratic offices create documentary credibility. The repeated use of terms now regarded as derogatory reflects the historical language and assumptions of the setting; crucially, the narrative ultimately challenges the dehumanising judgment expressed through that vocabulary.

The narrative is framed as a conversation between an unnamed first-person observer and his companion, Zimina. An incident in a sunlit park initiates a philosophical disagreement about whether a severely disabled person possesses distinctly human qualities. Zimina answers not through abstract debate but through an extended autobiographical tale, producing a story-within-a-story. This embedded narrative occupies most of the text and moves chronologically from his youthful arrival in Petersburg, through failure and destitution, to Stepan’s decisive act of generosity. The return to the park frame is brief and supplies an explicit interpretation of the anecdote. The structure therefore resembles a moral exemplum: an initial proposition is tested and overturned by lived experience.

The point of view is intimate but layered. The outer narrator is observant, somewhat skeptical, and initially emotionally limited; Zimina’s inner first-person account is more confessional and self-critical. He openly admits fear, aversion, vanity, humiliation, hunger, and suicidal despair, which makes his later compassion feel earned rather than idealised. Retrospective narration allows him to alternate between the naïveté of his younger self and the wiser understanding of the man telling the story. Occasional direct addresses—“You see,” “I tell you frankly,” and similar phrases—retain the cadence of oral storytelling and keep the listener present within the embedded narrative.

Description is rich, visual, and socially specific. The opening park is rendered through heat, colour, foliage, children’s games, clothing, and patterns of movement. Petersburg is characterised by grey rain, gleaming bronze, marble staircases, waxed floors, and dazzling military uniforms, while Alexandra Ivanovna’s poverty is conveyed through dull green glass, paraffin fumes, burning fat, darkness, and cramped domestic space. Such contrasts carry thematic weight: the ornamental grandeur of the Academy exposes Zimina’s insecurity, whereas the poor room becomes the site of genuine moral revelation. Similes are frequent and accessible, often drawn from familiar objects or childhood experience: chestnut flowers resemble “gigantic imperial candelabra,” parquet gleams “like a mirror,” and Stepan’s cries are “bird-like.”

The diction shifts according to subject. Descriptive passages are expansive and decorative, while scenes of poverty rely on plain, exact nouns and figures—copecks, bread, textbooks, tickets, opening hours. Military and academic terminology establishes Zimina’s social world and youthful ambition. Emotional language becomes increasingly direct as his circumstances deteriorate, with recurring words associated with shame, hunger, loneliness, pity, tenderness, love, and compassion. Stepan’s tiny idiolect—“Papan,” “mnya,” “teki,” and “oorli”—is repeated as a central motif. At first these sounds seem to confirm the other characters’ belief that he lacks reason; by the climax, “teki” becomes fully meaningful through action, demonstrating communication and empathy beyond conventional speech.

The syntax is generally long, balanced, and cumulative. Sentences often gather physical details or successive clauses before arriving at an emotional conclusion. Semicolons, dashes, ellipses, and parenthetical qualifications reproduce recollection, hesitation, and spoken emphasis. Paragraphs of panoramic description alternate with brisk dialogue, rhetorical questions, exclamations, and short emphatic statements such as “Three copecks!” This variation keeps the long anecdote animated. Lists are especially important: lists of children’s activities, military uniforms, bureaucratic offices, textbooks, and varieties of bread establish both abundance and frustration. Repetition intensifies key states, particularly Stepan’s cries, Zimina’s hunger, and the futile movement from one office to another.

Dialogue is naturalistic but purposeful. It differentiates the characters through tone: the outer narrator speaks in abstract, categorical terms; Zimina responds courteously but firmly; Alexandra Ivanovna is apologetic, timid, and practical; Stepan communicates through sounds, gestures, and physical attachment. Much of the story’s irony comes from the contrast between speech and conduct. Educated officers quote Shakespeare and discuss mathematics but refuse help, officials communicate fluently yet behave without compassion, and Stepan—who cannot form ordinary sentences—perceives suffering and gives away the money he fiercely guards. The climactic gesture refutes the narrator’s opening thesis more powerfully than argument could.

The pacing begins leisurely with a broad social tableau, quickens when the bath-chair appears, and then settles into Zimina’s detailed reminiscence. His examination period is narrated through selected scenes rather than continuous summary: arrival, visits to Stepan, academic failure, bureaucratic delays, hunger, the bread shop, the failed request for a loan, and the gift of coins. Comic or self-mocking touches—his imagined moustache, his solemn choice between cigarettes and bread, his exaggeratedly indifferent manner in the bakery—temporarily relieve the accumulating misery. The climax is quiet and compact: Stepan touches Zimina, offers the coins, and retreats. Its restraint makes the ensuing tears and moral release more effective.

The dominant tone moves from observational calm through discomfort, irony, humiliation, and despair to tenderness and affirmation. Sentiment is openly embraced rather than suppressed: characters weep, compassion “warms” the heart, and the lesson is directly stated. Nevertheless, the emotion is grounded in material detail and in Zimina’s unflattering honesty about himself. The story’s principal themes are human dignity, the limitations of intellect as a measure of humanity, compassion expressed through action, and salvation through unexpected fellowship. Secondary concerns include class inequality, urban isolation, institutional indifference, wounded masculine pride, and the moral education produced by failure. The governing paradox is that the character judged least rational performs the most recognisably humane act. Overall, the style is descriptive, anecdotal, ethically didactic, and emotionally direct, using realist detail and a carefully delayed act of kindness to overturn prejudice and affirm compassion as the essential mark of humanity.

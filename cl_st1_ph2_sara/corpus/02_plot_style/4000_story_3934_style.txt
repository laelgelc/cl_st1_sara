The story adopts the manner of a literary fairy tale or animal fable, combining a simple, accessible surface with sophisticated social satire. Its narrative voice is polished, confident, and gently intrusive. The narrator reports events in a seemingly innocent storybook register but occasionally steps forward to address the audience or deliver a dry judgment, most notably in the closing sentence. This creates a double perspective: the tale can be followed as a moral story about friendship, while its ironies are directed toward an adult reader capable of recognizing hypocrisy, vanity, and self-deception.

The structure is framed and recursive. An opening conversation among personified animals introduces the subject of devoted friendship, after which the Linnet tells an embedded tale about Hans and the Miller. Interruptions by the Water-rat comment on storytelling conventions and reveal his affinity with the selfish Miller before the parallel is made explicit. The narrative then returns to the pond for a comic discussion of the moral. This story-within-a-story design allows the outer characters to interpret—or conspicuously fail to interpret—the inner narrative, making reception itself part of the satire.

Characterization is largely achieved through dialogue, repeated phrases, and behavioural contrast. The Miller continually praises friendship, generosity, honesty, and selflessness while acting in ways that contradict every principle he announces. His language is fluent, sententious, and coercive: he converts abstractions into instruments of exploitation, presenting each demand as proof of his virtue and each hesitation from Hans as evidence of moral failure. Hans is characterized by diminutives, modest responses, physical labour, and habitual compliance. The repeated epithet “little Hans,” set against “big Hugh the Miller,” emphasizes the imbalance of power. In the frame, the Water-rat similarly exposes himself through his own speech, especially his belief that a devoted friend should be devoted solely to him.

Irony is the dominant technique. Much of it is dramatic irony, since the reader immediately sees the selfishness that Hans cannot recognize. There is also sustained verbal irony in the Miller’s “beautiful” speeches and in his wife’s admiring responses. Their praise is so exaggerated that it becomes comic condemnation. The narrator rarely labels the Miller cruel; instead, the gulf between his rhetoric and conduct supplies the judgment. The conclusion intensifies this method when the Miller interprets Hans’s death as a personal inconvenience and resolves never to be generous again.

The diction mixes plain narrative language with decorative, sensuous description. The opening uses vivid similes and sharply observed colour—beady eyes, grey whiskers, a black rubber-like tail, yellow ducklings, and the white duck’s red legs. Hans’s garden is rendered through an abundant catalogue of flowers, colours, seasons, and scents. Such passages create a lush fairy-tale atmosphere and associate Hans with beauty, cultivation, and natural generosity. By contrast, the Miller’s world is defined by possessions and stores: flour, cattle, sheep, wine, fire, and property. Concrete objects such as the wheelbarrow, plank, basket, lantern, and sack of flour become symbolic markers of unequal exchange.

The syntax is generally clear and cumulative, often linking clauses with “and” in a manner reminiscent of oral storytelling. Repetition gives the prose a ritual, fable-like rhythm: the Miller repeatedly says that he is “going to give” Hans the wheelbarrow, while Hans repeatedly accepts new burdens in the name of friendship. Dialogue frequently contains balanced claims, aphorisms, and mock-logical deductions. The Miller’s assertion that flour and friendship must not be confused because the words are spelled differently exemplifies the story’s comic use of specious reasoning. Exclamations, rhetorical questions, formal courtesies, and phrases such as “Well, really” heighten the theatrical quality of the exchanges.

The pacing alternates between scenic dialogue and compressed narration. The garden description and the principal conversations unfold leisurely, allowing hypocrisy to accumulate in detail. Hans’s exploitation then develops through a repeated sequence of request, moral pressure, reluctant consent, and exhausting labour. This pattern steadily darkens, moving from flowers and a plank to errands, roof repairs, sheep-driving, and finally the fatal journey through the storm. The climax is comparatively swift and stark, with the worsening weather and repeated darkness producing a tragic counterpoint to the earlier comedy.

The tone is therefore both whimsical and severe. Personified animals, picturesque settings, comic interruptions, and playful comments on critics and fashionable narrative methods create lightness. Beneath that charm lies a bleak account of economic power and moral manipulation. Sentiment is controlled rather than indulgent: Hans’s death is moving, but the narrative quickly turns to the Miller’s grotesquely self-centred mourning. This tonal pivot prevents the story from becoming merely pathetic and preserves its satirical sharpness.

The style relies heavily on antithesis between words and deeds, theory and practice, wealth and poverty, generosity and possession, innocence and calculation. Friendship is presented not as an abstract ideal but as a relationship whose truth must be measured by reciprocity and action. The story also satirizes eloquence detached from ethics: the Miller explicitly ranks talking above acting, while his wife compares his speeches to a sermon. Moral language, social respectability, and intellectual pretension are repeatedly shown concealing selfishness.

Overall, the prose is ornate but lucid, humorous but morally pointed, and conventionally fable-like while also mocking the conventions of fables. Its signature effect comes from allowing vain and selfish characters to condemn themselves through polished speech. The moral is unmistakable to the reader but rejected by the character who most needs it, giving the ending its final note of amused, self-aware irony.

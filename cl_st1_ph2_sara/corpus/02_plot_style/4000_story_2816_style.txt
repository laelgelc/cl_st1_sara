The story is written in a realist, psychologically observant mode, with strong naturalist and ironic elements. Its prose is lucid, formal, and largely unornamented, but it becomes richly descriptive when rendering landscape, weather, bodily sensation, and domestic surroundings. The narrative depends on a sharp contrast between the warm, luminous Mediterranean and the cold, oppressive Norman countryside. Cannes is associated with blue water, sunlight, perfume, orange blossom, openness, and release; Normandy is defined by mud, rain, snow, darkness, draughts, crows, ancient stone, and enclosure. Setting therefore functions as an external expression of the protagonist’s inner life rather than as mere background.

The narration is third-person omniscient, closely aligned with the young woman’s consciousness. It moves freely between objective description, compressed biography, interior thought, and free indirect discourse. Repetition gives her thoughts emotional force: “She will be no more” and “Nothing” reduce her future to a stark, obsessive certainty. Rhetorical questions likewise dramatize her despair without requiring direct confession. Although the story is framed in the present tense at Cannes, most of it unfolds as an extended retrospective account of her marriage and illness. The return to the promenade near the end closes the frame and transforms the opening serenity into tragic fulfilment. Occasional tense shifts and slightly stiff formulations suggest translated nineteenth-century prose.

The syntax alternates between expansive descriptive sentences and short declarative units. Long sentences accumulate geographical or sensory details, often through parallel clauses and lists, while brief sentences mark irreversible facts: “She will be no more”; “She was incurable”; “She must cough.” Anaphora is frequent, especially in phrases such as “all along,” “she looks,” and “she did not understand,” creating a measured, insistent rhythm. Seasonal transitions—“It was summer,” “The autumn came,” “The winter came”—compress time and lend the middle section the inevitability of a chronicle. Parenthetical qualifications and repeated explanatory clauses give the narrator a cool, analytic authority.

The diction is concrete and sensory. Colours recur symbolically: Mediterranean blue and gold suggest vitality and freedom, while Norman black, gray, and white suggest desolation, sterility, and death. Cold is personified as an active persecutor through “crafty currents of air,” “perfidious and frozen hatred,” and draughts that seem to inhabit the rooms. The château itself appears almost animate, chilled by centuries and hostile to the woman’s body. Animal imagery intensifies the mood: the crows form an “army” and a “black disturbance,” whereas the husband’s dogs receive the tenderness that cannot find an equivalent outlet in the marriage. Similes are vivid but accessible, as when villas resemble snow against dark greenery, the roof rises “like a knife blade,” and the snow feels painful “as a wound.”

Characterisation is economical and founded on contrast. The woman is sensitive, urban, timid, pleasure-loving, and physically fragile; her husband is robust, provincial, unimaginative, and complacent. He is not portrayed as deliberately cruel. His cruelty arises from emotional obtuseness, habit, and an inability to imagine needs unlike his own. Repeated phrases such as “he did not understand” make this limitation the central fact of his character. His blunt exclamations, laughter, rural idiom, and preoccupation with hunting and property contrast with her hesitant, broken speech. Her dialogue contains pauses, repetitions, and self-corrections, showing both fear and lack of agency. His speech is confident, dismissive, and often unintentionally comic.

Dialogue is sparse and functional, used mainly to expose the failure of communication within the marriage. The husband interprets material endurance as health and contentment, while the wife cannot articulate her emotional suffocation except through the language of physical cold. The furnace consequently becomes the story’s governing symbol. Literally, it represents warmth; psychologically, it represents recognition, care, and the right to alter an inherited domestic order. Because she can obtain it only by making herself ill, the object acquires bitterly ironic significance. Her self-exposure to the snow is narrated in deliberate, step-by-step detail, combining childish calculation, desperation, and self-destructive resolve.

The tone is compassionate toward the protagonist but controlled rather than sentimental. Pathos emerges from understatement, structural irony, and the mismatch between what characters intend and what their actions produce. The woman deliberately contracts an illness to secure warmth, is sent south because of that illness, and ultimately comes to prefer death in warmth to recovery and a return to cold. Her happiness at Cannes is thus sincere but paradoxical: death represents escape, while health threatens renewed imprisonment. The final satisfaction that she “had her furnace put in” condenses the story’s tragic irony into a mundane domestic victory purchased with her life.

Pacing is initially slow and panoramic, then becomes compressed through summary as the marriage passes from summer into winter. It slows again around key scenes—the furnace arguments, the night in the snow, and the final coughing fit—so that physical gestures acquire symbolic weight. The ending circles back to the opening imagery of the sea and sunset, but the beauty is shadowed by mortality. The unfinished or omitted contents of the husband’s letter shift attention away from information and toward her reaction, while the falling hand and obstinate cough provide a restrained, visual closing image. Overall, the style combines scenic beauty, psychological precision, social critique, and cruel situational irony in a polished nineteenth-century realist manner.

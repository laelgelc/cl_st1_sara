The story is a fast-moving comic bush yarn shaped by broad satire, escalating farce and deadpan exaggeration. Its narrator is omniscient, confident and conversational, recounting absurd events with the composure of a factual report. This contrast between sober delivery and grotesque incident is central to the humour: canvassers are tarred, feathered, harrowed and threatened with abandoned mine shafts, while the narration treats such violence as an ordinary commercial difficulty. Occasional direct addresses to “the reader” strengthen the manner of an oral storyteller who assumes a knowing, socially familiar audience.

The tone is irreverent, exuberant and mock-serious. Hyperbole appears almost continuously: smoke can cause paralysis, mosquitoes fall asleep before biting, genealogies stretch back to Noah’s Ark, and punches propel victims figuratively into the following week. Violent action is rendered as slapstick rather than tragedy, with bodies knocked down, dragged away or drowned in sequences governed by comic machinery. Institutions and respectable roles receive the same treatment as individuals. Publishers are predatory, inventors unreliable, police cowardly or incompetent, politicians absurdly rewarded, and religious respectability becomes part of the final joke. The satire is therefore genial in manner but broadly cynical in outlook.

The diction combines formal narrative phrasing with Australian colonial slang, commercial jargon, sporting language and colloquial idiom. Elevated expressions such as “divers strange bush ways,” “durance vile” and “superlative Dutch courage” sit beside “big bug,” “yabber,” “bloke,” “clout” and “gone in the horrors.” This mixing of registers creates comic incongruity and gives the prose a distinctly late-colonial Australian character. Period references to boxing, political agitation, temperance, book canvassing and remote settlements establish the social world without lengthy exposition.

Dialogue is highly characterising and often dialectal. Dodge’s brisk American manner, the inventor’s enthusiastic patter and O’Grady’s phonetic Irish speech distinguish speakers immediately. The canvasser’s prerecorded sales pitch parodies inflated advertising language: its impossible premiums, extravagant claims and relentless verbosity transform commercial persuasion into an assault. Repetition is especially effective when this sales spiel continues unchanged through fights and arrests, making the automaton’s verbal persistence as threatening as its mechanical strength. The phonetic rendering of accents is broad and caricatural, reflecting the comic conventions and ethnic stereotypes of the period.

Sentences vary between expansive descriptive passages and short action beats. Long cumulative sentences pile up details, examples and increasingly implausible qualifications, while abrupt declarations—“It was a fatal mistake”—signal a new stage in the disaster. Parenthetical comments, dashes, lists and semicolons help preserve the rhythm of spoken anecdote. Similes are vivid, physical and deliberately excessive: smoke behaves like a locomotive, a struck man drops “like a fowl,” and a mechanical blow arrives with the force of a horse-kick. Descriptions favour memorable grotesque particulars over realism, as in Macpherson’s enormous freckles and horn-like moustache.

Characterisation is economical and caricatural. Each person is built around a few enlarged traits: Sloper is bald and sanctimonious, Dodge small and smoky, Macpherson gigantic and combative, O’Grady persuasive but conflict-averse, and Dooley immensely strong yet sleepy and cowardly when defeated. Names and nicknames—Sloper, Dodge, the Genius, the Wombat—have a comic emblematic quality. Psychological depth is secondary to function within the farce; characters exist largely to trigger, misunderstand or suffer from the automaton’s mechanisms.

The structure follows a clear pattern of setup, demonstration, field test and runaway catastrophe. The opening establishes the hostility toward canvassers and the publishers’ financial crisis; the invention then appears as an apparently perfect solution. Ninemile provides a contained comic arena in which each encounter raises the stakes: hostile land agent, dog, sergeant, constable and finally the river. Much of the plotting depends on mechanical cause and effect, with every touch activating another spring and every attempted intervention worsening the situation. The ending compresses the aftermath into a mock-chronicle of deaths, promotions and political success, then reduces the extraordinary adventure to a garbled local legend. This deflation supplies the final ironic stroke.

The setting is presented through comic regionalism. The bush town’s heat, lethargy, whisky culture, local rivalries and indifference to outside authority are exaggerated into a self-contained social mythology. Ninemile is so apathetic that even a spectacular death fails to reach the newspapers, while a campaign to secure the Federal Capital instantly displaces all concern. The result is a style that blends tall tale, colonial sketch, technological fantasy and institutional satire. Its signature qualities are narrative briskness, vernacular energy, grotesque hyperbole, mechanical escalation, mock-documentary authority and a cheerful willingness to turn commercial modernity and civic respectability into anarchic farce.

The story adopts the style of a traditional literary fairy tale, opening with the formulaic “There was once” and moving rapidly from exaggerated prosperity to ruin, magical adventure, courtship, and an ironic reversal. Its world is governed by folktale logic rather than realism: wealth can be squandered almost instantly, a trunk can fly when its lock is pressed, a princess readily accepts a stranger as an angel, and storytelling itself can secure a royal marriage. The narrative combines a simple, linear quest with an extended story-within-a-story, so that the merchant’s son becomes both the protagonist and a surrogate storyteller.

The narrator is omniscient, conversational, and gently intrusive. Events are related with apparent simplicity, but frequent asides guide the reader’s response or add comic emphasis, as in explanations of why the king uses “thou” or why the young man naturally wants to hear what people think of his fireworks. Exclamations such as “dear me” and phrases like “to be sure” create the impression of an oral storyteller addressing an audience. The narrator treats marvels in a matter-of-fact way while quietly exposing vanity, credulity, and self-deception. The ending retains this light manner even as it becomes melancholy: the princess may still be waiting, while the failed suitor wanders the world telling stories.

The tone is whimsical, playful, and satirical, with a persistent undertone of moral caution. Comic exaggeration dominates the opening portrait of the merchant’s son, who makes kites from banknotes and throws gold into the sea. Similar hyperbole shapes the Turks’ response to the fireworks and the townspeople’s increasingly fanciful descriptions of the supposed angel. The humour often depends on bathos: grand claims of noble descent are transferred to kitchen matches, political opinions are attributed to pots and baskets, and a magnificent supernatural identity rests on nothing more than a dressing-gown, slippers, and a flying trunk. The final destruction of the trunk introduces poetic justice, but the punishment is presented with irony rather than solemn condemnation.

The diction is accessible and concrete, characteristic of a tale intended to be understood by both children and adults. Familiar domestic objects, ordinary clothing, food, money, and household routines anchor the fantasy. At the same time, the language occasionally becomes ornate in courtship scenes, especially when the merchant’s son compares the princess’s eyes to dark lakes containing mermaids and her forehead to a snowy mountain filled with splendid halls. These deliberately extravagant images reveal his talent for charming invention and suggest that verbal performance, rather than virtue or achievement, is his chief power. The prose also has an older, translated quality, visible in formal constructions, period vocabulary, emphatic punctuation, and expressions such as “marriage portion,” “high descent,” “instructive,” and “full of deep learning.”

Sentence structure alternates between compact statements that propel the plot and long, loosely coordinated sentences linked by semicolons, commas, and conjunctions. This paratactic movement resembles oral narration: one incident follows another with little psychological pause or descriptive delay. Repetition supplies rhythm and clarity, especially in patterns such as “he went,” “he flew,” “he said,” and “then.” Dialogue is plentiful and sharply functional. It advances the action, distinguishes comic personalities, and allows social satire to emerge without extended authorial explanation. The characters often speak in declarative, self-important voices, exposing their vanity through their own words.

The embedded kitchen story intensifies the tale’s personifying style. Matches, pots, plates, brooms, fire-tongs, kettles, baskets, and pens are given social identities, family histories, political opinions, artistic ambitions, and class prejudices. Their household functions determine their personalities: the iron pot values usefulness, the tea-urn is affected and status-conscious, the basket advocates order, and the matches boast of aristocratic ancestry. This anthropomorphism creates a miniature human society in which pride, rivalry, nationalism, conformity, and the desire for applause are gently mocked. The matches’ brief blaze provides the inset tale with a concise emblematic ending: they proclaim their supremacy at the very moment they consume themselves and go out.

Structurally, the story is framed by two forms of wasteful fire. At the beginning, the merchant’s son destroys inherited wealth through frivolous display; near the end, his fireworks destroy the magical trunk on which his future depends. This circularity gives the narrative moral coherence. He is imaginative and charming, but he never acquires his father’s prudence. The tale therefore contrasts economic wisdom with extravagance, substance with spectacle, and durable value with momentary brilliance. Its moral is present but not heavily stated: vanity and ostentation carry their own punishment, while stories can create dazzling possibilities without necessarily sustaining them.

Overall, the style blends fairy-tale directness, domestic fantasy, comic dialogue, social allegory, and understated irony. It is lively and episodic rather than psychologically detailed, using exaggeration, personification, repetition, and narrative asides to maintain an intimate storytelling voice. Beneath its childlike enchantment lies a sophisticated interest in class pretension, reputation, artistic performance, and the dangerous attraction of display.

The story is shaped as a comic dramatic monologue delivered almost entirely in the first person. Afzal Khan addresses an unnamed British official whose questions and reactions are never directly given but can be reconstructed from Afzal’s replies: “A bad character?” and “Why does the Sahib laugh?” This one-sided conversational frame creates immediacy and makes the reader occupy the official’s position. It also establishes Afzal as an unreliable yet highly expressive narrator. He repeatedly proclaims his honesty while calmly revealing bribery, fabricated evidence, torture, false arrest, dereliction of duty, and flight from justice. The central irony lies in the widening gap between his self-presentation as “a man of clean heart” and the conduct disclosed by his own story.

The narrative voice imitates oral storytelling. Afzal’s speech is digressive, repetitive, ingratiating, and responsive to the listener’s presumed scepticism. He alternates between pleading, boasting, confessing, flattering, and excusing himself, often within a few sentences. Direct appeals such as “Sahib,” “O Sahib,” and “Know, O Sahib” punctuate the account and keep the social relationship constantly in view. Exclamations, rhetorical questions, sudden denials, and interjections—“Aha!”, “Nay,” “Ho! Ho!”—give the prose a performed quality. Recurrent assurances that he will “speak true talk” become comic verbal tics, since every profession of truth introduces some further incriminating admission.

The diction combines elevated, archaic English with colonial Anglo-Indian vocabulary and culturally marked idioms. Words such as “Sahib,” “Thana,” “Havildar,” “dacoits,” “charpoys,” “tulwar,” “tehsil,” “Sirkar,” and “Mem-sahib” locate the story within British India and present administration through a hybrid lexicon. Biblical or mock-epic constructions—“It fell upon a night,” “behold,” “wherefore,” “ye,” “withal,” and “the Mercy of Heaven”—lend Afzal’s account a ceremonious grandeur at odds with its sordid events. Formulae such as “the heart of the Presence,” “the shadow of his protection,” and “the servants of the Presence” exaggerate deference into comic hyperbole. The representation of Indian speech is stylised rather than naturalistic, using translated idioms, unusual collocations, and archaic grammar to produce an English-language approximation of a culturally distinct voice.

Syntax is highly varied but generally accumulative. Short bursts—“It was here: it was there: it was gone”—convey speed and astonishment, while long coordinated sentences reproduce the momentum of spoken recollection. Frequent dashes, semicolons, colons, and embedded quotations allow the narrator to interrupt himself, revise his claims, or dramatise past dialogue. Parallel constructions and repetition provide an incantatory rhythm: “There was smoke and breaking and blood and trampled earth.” The embedded speeches of the Havildar and Yunkum Sahib turn the monologue into a layered performance, with Afzal reenacting whole scenes while retaining control over their comic framing.

The tonal register blends farce, satire, suspense, and moral darkness. The policemen’s arrangement with the dacoits, their leisure among the melons, and their elaborate manufacture of a crime scene are narrated with cheerful practicality. This matter-of-fact treatment makes corruption appear absurdly routine. Yet the comedy is edged by violence: innocent villagers are framed, tortured, and brought in irons, while the policemen wound themselves and slaughter a goat to authenticate their lie. The story does not isolate corruption as a single villain’s failing; instead, it depicts a whole administrative culture of self-interest, performance, coercion, and fear. Afzal’s charm softens but does not erase his complicity.

Characterisation is achieved chiefly through speech and action rather than authorial explanation. Afzal emerges as quick-witted, adaptable, shameless, fearful, and instinctively theatrical. His concern for his hungry children may be sincere, but it also functions as part of his sales pitch. Ram Baksh is defined by complacent ingenuity and false confidence, while Yunkum Sahib is built up through rumor, metaphor, and delayed action. His nickname, “The Tiger of Gokral-Seetarun,” gives him a legendary presence. The mysterious “devil-carriage,” described through Afzal’s baffled perception as three noiseless silver wheels driven by the rider’s legs, is presumably a bicycle; defamiliarising it in this way makes modern technology seem uncanny and enhances Yunkum’s supernatural reputation.

The plot follows an oral anecdotal arc. The opening job application supplies the frame and motive, after which Afzal’s explanation of why he left the police expands into the main story. Early comic admissions about the pact with the dacoits establish the policemen’s corruption. The theft of the rifles and record book introduces the central mystery, and the false reconstruction of an attack builds elaborate dramatic irony. Suspense depends on Yunkum Sahib’s silence and smile: the reader senses that he knows more than he reveals. The revelation of the missing property in his bathroom provides the climax, retrospectively explaining his nocturnal movements and exposing the entire fabrication. Afzal’s immediate escape then returns the story to the employment interview, where his renewed demand for service completes the circular structure.

Imagery is vivid, concrete, and often proverbial. The hot weather, melon beds, charpoys, bloodied clothes, broken bars, hoofprints, dust, and sweat give physical density to the anecdote. Similes draw on animals, food, folklore, and local experience: the official is a tiger, the bicycle is noiseless as a ghoul, the melons are sweet as sugar cane, and the promised wife is fair as a Peri. These images reflect Afzal’s imaginative world while enlarging ordinary events into legend. The opening proverb, “His own shoe, his own head,” anticipates the story’s pattern of self-inflicted defeat: the policemen’s own corrupt habits, fabricated procedures, and institutional weapons are turned against them.

Overall, the style depends on sustained verbal irony. Afzal believes—or pretends to believe—that his resourcefulness makes him employable, while his confession proves him dishonest. His reverential language toward authority coexists with a frank understanding that authority can be manipulated through flattery, reports, staged evidence, and ritual submission. The final shift from abject supplicant to confident insider—“I also am of the household of the Sahib”—ends on a note of comic audacity. The resulting profile is that of a brisk, voice-driven colonial satire: anecdotal in structure, theatrical in delivery, hybrid in diction, rich in oral rhythms, and morally double-edged beneath its exuberant humour.

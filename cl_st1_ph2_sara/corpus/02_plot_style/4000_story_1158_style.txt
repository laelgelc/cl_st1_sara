The story is written as a one-act comic stage play and functions chiefly as a parody of sentimental “quaint” rural drama. It adopts the apparatus of theatre—character labels, parenthetical performance notes, entrances, exits, pauses and elaborate scene directions—but repeatedly uses that apparatus to sabotage dramatic realism. Directions suggest optional songs, card tricks, risqué jokes and even alternative cues, while the curtain itself becomes a prop in a slapstick routine. The closing assurance that the play “can go on indefinitely” completes the mockery of conventional dramatic form and emotional resolution.

The dominant tone is arch, irreverent and absurdist. Sentimentality is continually raised only to be punctured by bathos: pastoral beauty is crowded with “McCormick harvesters,” a solemn death-like tableau culminates in the gift of a moth-ball, and a transcendent twilight is accompanied by an impossible Beethoven symphony played on a mouth-organ. The narration maintains a knowing, deadpan manner, presenting impossibilities and non sequiturs as though they were ordinary theatrical details. Its comedy depends on sustained incongruity between elevated presentation and ridiculous content.

The stage directions are unusually prominent and often more comic than the dialogue. They act as a satirical narrative voice, offering redundant descriptions, literalizing clichés and commenting on theatrical technique. Characters are introduced through exaggerated visual shorthand: Peter has “a complete set of features,” Rodney Divine is so mundane that his contrast with spirituality is visible from the balcony, and Ulsa possesses the shapeless face of an early Italian painting. These descriptions mimic the pretensions of literary drama while exposing their artificiality.

The dialogue is compact, fast-moving and constructed around reversals, malapropisms, false quotations and epigrammatic punch lines. Solemn statements are answered by literal or deflating remarks: asthma is “worse, thank God,” clergymen have souls instead of glands, and the farmers’ status as the nation’s backbone provokes the question of who would want to be a backbone. Characters speak in polished paradoxes regardless of their social position, reinforcing the sense that they are comic types rather than psychologically realistic people. Deliberately rustic spellings such as “feyther,” “reyther” and “Lunnon” parody stage dialect, while the narrator undercuts the effect by observing that the accent is broad although the content remains “sweet and clean.”

The diction mixes pastoral archaism, contemporary slang, scientific language, literary allusion and fashionable social references. Words such as “laddie,” “’tisn’t” and “whimsey” coexist with “the bunk,” “Jazz,” gland transplantation, Rolls-Royces and psychic research. References to Wordsworth, Conrad, Walter Pater, Henry James, Nero and the Bible are intentionally misattributed or scrambled, turning cultural sophistication into comic confusion. Names such as Mr. Icky and Rodney Divine are overtly symbolic but comically crude, parodying the meaningful nomenclature of allegorical drama.

Sentence structure alternates between clipped exchanges and extravagantly cumulative stage descriptions. Ellipses create a mock-pensive rhythm, especially in Mr. Icky’s speeches, while abrupt replies puncture that rhythm. Repetition—“yes…yes,” “Life! Jazz!,” “It’s damp! It won’t work!”—gives scenes a performative, chant-like momentum. Parenthetical adverbs such as “whimsically,” “gloomily,” “dreamily” and “tremulously” over-prescribe emotion, satirizing plays that rely on conspicuous mood cues.

Characterization is intentionally schematic. Mr. Icky embodies manufactured rural quaintness, Peter precocious spiritual innocence, Ulsa worldly modernity, Divine aristocratic sophistication, and Charles rebellious literary yearning. None remains consistent in a realistic sense; each exists to generate verbal collisions and parodic contrasts. Mr. Icky’s struggle to “be quaint” makes the governing satire explicit: quaintness is shown not as an organic quality but as a self-conscious pose imposed on life.

The plot is loose, episodic and anti-climactic. It moves from pastoral conversation to romantic dispute, filial rebellion, mass departure and apparent death, but causal links matter less than successive comic set pieces. Pacing is manipulated openly: several hours pass in a sentence, songs or tricks may fill the gap, and the curtain rises and falls to signify time. This overt theatrical artificiality creates a proto-absurdist effect in which plot conventions are exposed as arbitrary mechanisms.

Imagery repeatedly converts poetic motifs into material jokes. Soil rubbed on a bald head causes hair to sprout; moths create a solemn shroud before fleeing from a moth-ball; biblical poetry becomes “rather coarse”; and the “Great Potato Tradition” replaces grand ancestral heritage. The overall style is therefore parodic, metatheatrical and densely allusive, combining verbal wit, slapstick, surreal stage business and deliberate bathos to ridicule pastoral nostalgia, literary solemnity, social pretension and the machinery of sentimental drama.

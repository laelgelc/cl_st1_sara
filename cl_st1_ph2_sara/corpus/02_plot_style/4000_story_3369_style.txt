The story adopts a first-person confessional style, presented as a dated memoir written by a narrator whose extraordinary age is announced in the opening sentence. Direct addresses to the reader, rhetorical questions, self-corrections and recurring returns to the question “Am I immortal?” create the sense of a private testimony being composed in real time. The narrator is retrospective but not omniscient about his own condition: he can recount events across three centuries, yet remains uncertain about the meaning and limits of the elixir he drank. This tension between factual recollection and metaphysical doubt supplies the narrative with both intimacy and suspense.

Its mode is strongly Gothic and Romantic. Alchemy, forbidden knowledge, supernatural longevity, social persecution, ruined happiness and obsessive love are treated through heightened emotion and ominous atmosphere. The supernatural premise is embedded in a recognisable human story of jealousy, marriage, ageing and bereavement, so that immortality becomes less a source of wonder than a psychological and moral affliction. The tone shifts from youthful melodrama and enchanted exhilaration to domestic pathos, existential weariness and grave-bound longing. Moments of dark humour and irony temper the solemnity, especially when the supposed “cure for love” intensifies the narrator’s passion, or when the ageing Bertha tries to disguise the disparity between herself and her eternally youthful husband.

The diction is elevated, archaic and literary, with forms such as “methought,” “whither,” “quaffed,” “beset,” “aught” and “alchymist,” alongside abstract nouns including “immortality,” “inconstancy,” “vigilance,” “credulity” and “annihilation.” Emotional states are frequently personified: Hope dawns, Jealousy enters the breast, sleep weighs upon the eyelids and Death becomes an “ill-visaged friend.” Biblical, legendary and classical allusions—the Wandering Jew, the Seven Sleepers, Satan, Cain, the children of Adam and Nestor—place the personal narrative within a broad mythic tradition. Superlatives and intensifiers are common, reinforcing the narrator’s emotional extremity: sensations are “most delicious,” dangers “gigantic,” and love is expressed through the escalating sequence “adored—worshipped—idolized.”

The syntax tends toward long, elaborately balanced sentences containing subordinate clauses, parenthetical reflections and accumulative lists. These are punctuated by abrupt fragments and exclamations at moments of crisis: “For ever!” “Never!” “Death!” Dashes are especially prominent, marking hesitation, interruption, emotional acceleration and sudden changes of thought. Semicolons organise extended reflections, while repeated questions dramatise the narrator’s uncertainty. Parallel structures and anaphora give passages an oratorical rhythm, as in repeated formulations about living, dying, loving and enduring. The prose often moves through antithesis—youth against age, love against indifference, mortality against immortality, delight against despair—and culminates in paradoxes such as “old-hearted youth” and “a mortal immortal.”

Description is selective and symbolically charged rather than densely realistic. Colour, light and scent dominate the alchemical scenes: rose-coloured liquid becomes white, emits golden flashes and releases an irresistible fragrance. Fire signifies both forbidden discovery and destructive knowledge, while mirrors repeatedly measure the narrator’s unnatural preservation. Water carries an opposite but related symbolism, promising peace, dissolution and possible escape through death. Cages, prisons and enclosures recur in descriptions of Bertha’s aristocratic home and of the narrator’s immortal body, linking social confinement with metaphysical imprisonment. Natural and maritime similes—a fawn, a storm-tossed sailor, a traveller lost on a heath—translate inward suffering into vivid Romantic landscapes.

Characterisation is filtered almost entirely through the narrator’s emotional perspective. Bertha is first idealised as a dark-haired, fawn-like beloved, then represented as proud, coquettish, jealous and finally diminished by age. The change reveals both the cruelty of time and the narrator’s imperfect, sometimes ungenerous gaze. Cornelius Agrippa is rendered through a blend of legendary grandeur and practical vulnerability: a feared occult philosopher who nevertheless needs an assistant to tend his furnaces. Dialogue is relatively sparse but highly functional, appearing at decisive moments to provoke action, reveal misunderstanding or sharpen dramatic irony. Speech often has a theatrical quality, using exclamation, accusation and formal declaration rather than conversational naturalism.

The narrative is broadly chronological but framed by the narrator’s present-day crisis. Its pacing is expansive around the drinking of the elixir, the reconciliation with Bertha and the revelation of immortality, then compresses decades into reflective summary. Transitional phrases such as “A year passed away,” “Five years afterwards” and “Since then” facilitate large temporal leaps. The final section slows again into philosophical meditation, circling through possible interpretations of immortality, the fear of suicide and the proposed fatal expedition. This produces an open ending: the narrative closes not with resolution but with an intention to test the body’s endurance.

Overall, the style combines Gothic sensationalism with introspective philosophical fiction. Its central effect comes from converting a marvellous premise into an extended meditation on attachment, ageing, alienation and the human need for death. The voice is impassioned, ceremonious and self-dramatising, but also capable of tenderness, rueful irony and moral self-scrutiny. The result is a tragic memoir whose supernatural machinery serves primarily to intensify ordinary human losses.

The story is a comic detective tale with the atmosphere of early twentieth-century American popular fiction. It combines a small-town legal mystery, a sentimental subplot, and broad character comedy. The central plot imitates the machinery of a conventional detective story—missing persons, microscopic clues, disputed wills, secret documents, and a final revelation—but repeatedly undercuts that machinery through absurdity. The mystery is genuine enough to sustain curiosity, yet its solution depends less on brilliant deduction than on the dead man’s elaborate practical joke and Philo Gubb’s accidental persistence.

The narrator uses a clear, confident third-person voice that is mostly omniscient. It moves freely between present action, explanatory backstory, and concise commentary on characters’ motives and reputations. The narration often maintains a straight face while describing ridiculous circumstances, creating a dry, deadpan effect. Understatement is especially important: assertions such as Gubb’s clues yielding no conclusion “unless the flea in the wool might lead to the conclusion that the dog now, or once, had fleas” parody the solemn logic of detective fiction. The narrator occasionally becomes more openly ironic, as when noting that the inductive method has failed “as it always failed for Mr. Gubb,” but generally lets the disparity between the characters’ seriousness and the absurd facts produce the comedy.

The prose is accessible and energetic, with predominantly medium-length declarative sentences interrupted by longer explanatory passages. Repetition is used for rhythm, emphasis, and comic escalation. The account of O’Hara’s habitual jokes, the duplicated wording of the two wills, and the telegram’s long catalogue of abandoned vegetables all build humor through accumulation. Parallel constructions—“word for word,” “line for line,” and “punctuation mark for punctuation mark”—give the mystery an exaggerated air of exactness. Brief sentences often deliver reversals or punch lines: “But he died. He died and left a will.” The narrative also uses conspicuous transitions and recapitulation, making the plot easy to follow even when it pauses for extended background information.

Dialogue dominates much of the story and is the chief instrument of characterization. Each speaker has a distinctive verbal register. Lawyers Higgins, Burch, and Judge Mackinnon speak briskly and comparatively plainly, while Mustard Bilton uses relaxed rural colloquialisms. Mrs. Mullarky’s speech is represented through an Irish-American stage dialect. Philo Gubb’s language is the strongest stylistic signature: he speaks in malformed, redundantly elaborate pseudo-professional English, using phrases such as “the present time and moment,” “disappeared away,” and “accompany yourself with me.” He mispronounces “detective” as “deteckative,” overuses formal legalistic constructions, and turns simple statements into pompous circumlocutions. His speech reflects both social aspiration and comic self-importance: he has absorbed the vocabulary of correspondence-school detection without mastering its idiom or methods.

Characterization is economical and caricatural but affectionate. Gubb is vain, literal-minded, ceremonious, and largely incompetent as a reasoner, yet he is also honest, diligent, polite, and kind. His inflated sense of professional dignity makes him ridiculous, but his willingness to help Dolly and his refusal to dismiss the loss of a dog give him warmth. O’Hara is characterized indirectly through anecdotes, his will, and the instructions he leaves behind; even after death, he effectively directs the action. The rival heirs are drawn as grasping and cantankerous, while Dolly functions as the story’s sincere emotional center. The supporting cast consists of sharply recognizable comic types: practical lawyers, a genial judge, a lazy loafer, and a mocking housekeeper.

The humor rests on incongruity, verbal distortion, dramatic irony, and parody. Gubb repeatedly interprets mockery as praise, treats elementary observations as advanced deduction, and behaves as though crawling into a dog-house were an act of formidable professional technique. The audience understands more than he does, especially when O’Hara’s instructions reveal that Gubb’s predictable habits have been built into the joke. The story also employs reversals in quick succession: the missing dog leads to the missing witness; the witness fails to resolve the double will; and a hidden third will settles everything. Gubb’s apparent success is therefore both real and comic. He finds the dog, the witness, and the valid will, but does so precisely because O’Hara anticipated his clumsy methods.

Structurally, the story interweaves several plot strands that converge neatly. The opening material about Gubb’s dubious investment and enormous fiancée supplies serial continuity and establishes his gullibility. The immediate mystery then begins with the search for Mustard, expands into the history of O’Hara and the two-sided will, and connects with Dolly’s missing dog. Exposition is substantial, but it is broken up by dialogue, jokes, and small discoveries. The climax arrives in two stages: first, Gubb discovers the envelope in the dog-house and finds both Waffles and Mustard; second, Mustard reveals that neither side of the disputed document is the final will. The closing line restores Gubb’s comic self-image by allowing him to attribute his success to a correspondence-course lesson.

The setting is a lightly sketched Midwestern town whose offices, courthouse culture, bridge, streets, and familiar local personalities create a communal, provincial atmosphere. Riverbank operates as a world where everyone knows everyone else’s business and a disputed will can become the town’s sole topic within hours. The social world is recognizable rather than realistic in depth, serving as a compact stage for eccentricity and farce. Period details—telegrams, handwritten wills, correspondence courses, side shows, cob pipes, and office blocks—reinforce the story’s historical character.

The overall tone is genial, satirical, and lightly sentimental rather than cynical. The story mocks pretension, greed, amateur expertise, and the conventions of scientific detection, but it rewards loyalty and affection. Its emotional material is deliberately simple: Dolly loves her uncle and her dog, while O’Hara’s final joke ultimately protects her inheritance. The resulting style can be described as deadpan comic narration enlivened by dialect dialogue, verbal malapropism, elaborate plotting, and affectionate parody of the amateur-detective genre.

The story adopts the manner of an early twentieth-century legal mystery or “problem” tale, combining cosmopolitan drawing-room conversation with a carefully engineered case history. Its tone is urbane, formal, ironic, and intellectually combative. Polite courtesies—especially the repeated “monsieur,” apologies, bows, and professions of admiration—soften arguments that are often sharply satirical. The central irony arises from the contrast between abstract justice and the technical operation of law: what appears morally obvious is overturned by a precise doctrine of causation.

The narration is third-person and largely external, with occasional access to the collective impressions of the men on the terrace. The narrator frames the old Count as both storyteller and performer, repeatedly describing his gestures, pauses, cigarettes, facial expressions, and effect upon his listeners. His extreme age is rendered through elaborate physical description and historical metaphor, making him seem like a survivor from a vanished civilization. This lends authority and theatrical weight to his critique of modern legal systems.

Dialogue dominates the story. The Count’s speech is ceremonious, digressive, and rhetorically controlled, while the American Justice and Sir Henry Marquis provide objections, clarifications, and prompts. The exchange resembles a Socratic inquiry: the Count establishes hypothetical cases, solicits apparently unavoidable judgments, introduces legal distinctions, and only gradually reveals the contradiction at issue. Repetition of questions such as whether the assassin should be convicted keeps the audience committed to a moral conclusion before the legal reversal arrives.

The syntax is generally long, balanced, and periodic, with subordinate clauses, semicolons, parenthetical qualifications, and carefully delayed conclusions. Short paragraphs and isolated sentences—“He paused,” “They went on,” “There was nothing incomplete in that trial”—interrupt this discursiveness at strategic points. These pauses regulate suspense and reproduce the rhythm of oral storytelling. Exclamation marks, direct address, and recurrent transitional formulas such as “Pardon me,” “Observe, monsieur,” and “Nevertheless” give the Count’s account an oratorical, almost forensic cadence.

The diction is elevated and legalistic, mixing terms such as “reasonable doubt,” “hearsay evidence,” “malice aforethought,” and “murder in the first degree” with classical and biblical allusions. References to the Iliad, Saul, Brutus, Rome, and the pagan world elevate the wounded officer’s suicide into an act of heroic self-determination. By contrast, the assassin is described through strongly pejorative and physiognomic language typical of the period, including “degenerate,” “creature,” and “Apache.” Ethnic and national generalizations are frequent; they contribute to the story’s historical cosmopolitanism but also reflect dated racial, cultural, and class assumptions.

Imagery moves between elegance and brutality. The Cannes terrace, tobacco smoke, and Mediterranean vista create a refined frame, while the embedded narratives contain sapling torture, dislocated limbs, gunshot wounds, and suicide. The contrast is deliberate: civilized men discuss savage violence in exquisite surroundings, while supposedly civilized law produces a result that seems morally perverse. Tobacco functions as a recurring stage prop, marking pauses and transitions and reinforcing the leisurely, club-like atmosphere.

Structurally, the story is nested and retrospective. It begins with a philosophical claim about culturally relative conceptions of justice, passes through the illustrative tale of Corporal Flint, digresses into comparative legal procedure and hearsay, and then arrives at the American murder case. The opening hypothetical foreshadows the real case: in each, one person inflicts fatal suffering while another directly causes the victim’s immediate death. The gradual accumulation of apparently incontrovertible evidence creates the expectation of execution, which is defeated by the appellate ruling. The final legal citation shifts the work from fiction toward case commentary and confirms that the twist depends on an actual common-law doctrine.

The pacing is deliberately slow and oblique. Suspense comes less from discovering who committed the shooting than from discovering why an obvious murderer cannot legally be treated as such. The Count repeatedly postpones the answer, and his digressions initially appear ornamental but later prove relevant to witness credibility, evidentiary rules, and legal reasoning. The ending is dry rather than melodramatic: the assassin survives, the Count must continue supporting an “indigent relative,” and personal financial inconvenience becomes the understated punch line to a profound conflict between moral responsibility and technical causation.

Overall, the style is best characterized as polished, anecdotal, forensic, and paradox-driven. It relies on cultured dialogue, elaborate framing, comparative satire, and a final jurisprudential reversal. Its ideal effect is to make the reader first judge intuitively, then confront the unsettling distance between ordinary moral judgment and the exact categories through which a court assigns criminal liability.

The story uses a retrospective first-person voice that presents an absurd episode as sober personal testimony. The narrator repeatedly insists on the literal truth of his account, adopting the grave manner of a man explaining a genuine catastrophe. This earnest framing is central to the comedy: his ruined health, solemn mission, and emotional distress are described with complete seriousness, while the reader understands that the supposed corpse is a box of rifles and that the “evidence” of decomposition is Limburger cheese. The narrator is therefore reliable about what he experienced but comically mistaken about its meaning. His hindsight allows him to reveal the mix-up early, creating dramatic irony rather than preserving the misunderstanding as a final surprise.

The tone combines mock tragedy, deadpan absurdity, grotesque physical comedy, and affectionate social satire. Elevated expressions such as “poor departed friend,” “infinitely saddening,” and “deleterious” treat a foul smell as a solemn manifestation of death. This ceremonious language collides with blunt colloquialisms, bodily distress, and increasingly ridiculous attempts to suppress the odor. The disparity between dignified interpretation and ignoble reality produces sustained incongruity. Sentiment is repeatedly allowed to swell toward pathos and then punctured by a practical observation, a dialect joke, or an involuntary rush to the broken window.

The prose alternates between expansive narrative passages and sharply staged dialogue. Narrative sentences are often long, cumulative, and loosely coordinated, using semicolons, dashes, repetitions, and parenthetical corrections to mimic an oral storyteller elaborating as he goes. Self-revisions such as “coffin-box—I mean my box of guns” keep the central confusion active while emphasizing the narrator’s retrospective embarrassment. Repeated constructions—“hotter and hotter,” “closer and closer,” “down, down, down”—create rhythmic escalation. Short declarative statements then provide comic closure or sudden reversals, particularly after elaborate preparations fail.

Diction is deliberately mixed. The narrator’s vocabulary is literate, formal, and sometimes mock-scientific, especially when describing odors, illness, or emotional effects. Thompson’s speech is rendered in a strongly marked American vernacular through contractions, nonstandard grammar, phonetic spellings, and regional idioms: “yourn,” “ben,” “ain’t,” “becuz,” “thish-yer,” and “agin.” His mangled biblical quotations parody proverbial wisdom without making him merely foolish; they establish him as practical, genial, superstitious, and naturally rhetorical. His metaphors come from rail travel, cards, politics, military rank, and physical contests, giving his speech a vigorous workingman’s texture.

Dialogue carries much of the characterization and comic timing. Thompson responds to the supposed corpse with a mixture of professional experience, awe, resignation, and competitive admiration. As the smell strengthens, he promotes the dead man from “Colonel” to “Gen’rul,” “Commodore,” and “Governor.” This running title gag turns the odor’s increasing force into a mock hierarchy of power. Thompson also personifies the corpse as an opponent who “holds all the trumps,” “outvotes” the men, and repurposes every remedy. The narrator, by contrast, speaks relatively little during the ordeal; his restraint, embarrassment, and inability to correct Thompson preserve the misunderstanding and allow Thompson’s voice to dominate.

The structure is built as a controlled escalation. An opening account of inexplicable physical decline promises a strange causal story. The railway-station mix-up establishes the dramatic irony, after which the enclosed express car becomes a comic pressure chamber. Each stage follows a recurring pattern: the odor worsens, Thompson interprets it as evidence of decomposition, a remedy is proposed, the remedy fails, and the men retreat into the freezing storm. The attempted solutions grow more extreme—from ventilation and cigars to carbolic acid and a bonfire of malodorous materials—so that repetition never remains static. Instead, each cycle raises the physical danger, intensifies the language, and enlarges the supposed corpse’s symbolic authority.

Sensory description is dominated by smell, heat, cold, suffocation, and bodily weakness. Because odor is difficult to describe directly, the story represents it through metaphor, reaction, and comic understatement. The smell is “searching,” “gamey,” “ripe,” “rich,” and “sublime”; it has “ambition,” “influence,” and a capacity to “warm up to his work.” Euphemism and ironic refinement are especially prominent. Words such as “fragrance,” “perfumes,” and “poetry” describe increasingly intolerable stenches, while physical collapse is narrated in a jaunty, conversational manner. The contrast makes the grotesque material vivid without resorting to purely clinical description.

The humor depends heavily on dramatic irony, understatement, hyperbole, personification, repetition, and bathos. The reader knows that the men’s elaborate theory is false, yet every new event appears to confirm it from their perspective. Hyperbole turns one night of discomfort into a terminal catastrophe, while bathos repeatedly brings lofty reflections on mortality down to broken windows, dropped cigars, gagging, and frantic escapes. The final explanation does not merely expose the mistake; it extends the joke by claiming that imagination permanently destroyed the narrator’s health. Thus the framing voice remains committed to melodrama even after the facts are known.

Pacing is leisurely at the outset, with enough logistical detail to make the mistake plausible, then increasingly rapid as the ordeal develops. Pauses for meditation, moralizing, and scriptural reflection briefly slow the action before abrupt physical interruptions restore momentum. This stop-start rhythm mirrors the men’s movement between the overheated car and the freezing platform. The repeated alternation of endurance and flight gives the narrative a farcical, almost theatrical quality, as though the confined railway car were a stage set designed for entrances, exits, and escalating business.

Overall, the style is that of an oral comic tall tale filtered through mock-memoir. It treats imagination as a force capable of overpowering fact, while gently satirizing sentimentality, masculine endurance, professional confidence, folk wisdom, and the human tendency to interpret sensations according to prior belief. The language remains genial rather than cruel: both men are ridiculous, but their courtesy, loyalty, and shared suffering make them sympathetic. The result is a highly controlled piece of comic realism in which precise social speech and plausible physical detail support an extravagantly absurd misunderstanding.

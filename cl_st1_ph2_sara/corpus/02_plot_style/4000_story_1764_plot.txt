A writer who has criticized expensive, immoral theater debates popular entertainment with a skeptical friend. The friend argues that cheap amusements can be as clever, respectable, and worthwhile as costly plays, describing an enjoyable visit to a dime museum. There he sees curiosities, melancholy “Australian” exhibits, inventors, a clumsy dramatic sketch, a gifted comedian, and two contortionists. Although the performances are humble and sometimes pathetic, several performers show genuine talent and artistic pride.

The conversation broadens into a comparison between low entertainment and literature. The friend contends that writers, like stage performers, belong to the “show business” because they must amuse or interest an audience to survive. He then discusses the circus, praising its thrills but criticizing its excessive simultaneous acts, three rings, and silent clowns, which overwhelm spectators instead of letting them appreciate each performance.

Ultimately, the friend suggests that dime museums and continuous variety shows may suit popular tastes better than either expensive theater or improving books. The dialogue satirically challenges distinctions between “high” and “low” art, arguing that artistic value, pathos, and genuine talent can exist even in the cheapest entertainments.

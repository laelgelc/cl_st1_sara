The story adopts an elevated, essayistic mode of first-person narration. Its narrator is both intimate witness and philosophical commentator: he presents Ellison as a friend, yet often recedes behind abstract arguments about happiness, genius, art, nature, and human perfectibility. This creates a hybrid form combining biographical sketch, aesthetic treatise, speculative philosophy, travel narrative, and visionary landscape description. Ellison functions less as a psychologically conflicted character than as an ideal embodiment of the story’s governing ideas.

The diction is formal, Latinate, and distinctly nineteenth-century, favouring words such as “preconcert,” “susceptibility,” “intermedium,” “expatiating,” and “pecuniary.” Abstract nouns and conceptual distinctions dominate the argumentative passages, while the descriptive passages employ sumptuous terms associated with colour, texture, precious materials, music, and supernatural beauty. Foreign phrases, classical and literary references, and allusions to philosophers and artists give the prose a learned, cosmopolitan character. Parenthetical qualifications and corrective formulations—“or, more properly,” “if not,” “in the widest and noblest sense”—suggest an exacting intelligence continually refining its own claims.

Sentences are generally long, intricately subordinated, and rhythmically cumulative. They often begin with a proposition, complicate it through qualifications and counterarguments, and end in a heightened conclusion. Semicolons, parentheses, dashes, colons, and parallel clauses regulate the flow of thought. Repetition and anaphora provide rhetorical force, especially in constructions such as “not a dead branch—not a withered leaf—not a stray pebble.” Balanced antitheses are frequent: nature is opposed to art, detail to total composition, ambition to happiness, human design to divine creation, and wildness to cultivation. The prose repeatedly defines an idea by distinguishing it from neighbouring ideas, giving the narration the character of a formal aesthetic inquiry.

The tone is reverential, meditative, and intellectually assured, though occasionally marked by personal hesitation. The narrator makes categorical pronouncements about beauty and artistic judgment, then admits that language cannot adequately convey Ellison’s achievement. This tension between analytical confidence and descriptive inadequacy is central to the style. Exclamations, rhetorical questions, and appeals to universal artistic instinct intensify the argument, while phrases such as “I despair of conveying” cultivate the convention of the sublime: the object is represented as exceeding ordinary powers of expression.

The narrative develops slowly and architecturally. It begins with a philosophical thesis about happiness, enumerates Ellison’s four conditions of bliss, establishes the extraordinary fortune that makes his project possible, and then expands into a theory of landscape gardening as the highest poetic art. Only after this prolonged conceptual preparation does it move into scenic narration. The immense inheritance is rendered through a comic-serious cascade of calculations, reducing the sum from annual income to money accrued per minute. This numerical exactitude contrasts with the later dreamlike profusion of the estate and lends material plausibility to an otherwise extravagant vision.

Dialogue is sparse and highly composed. Ellison speaks not in colloquial conversation but in polished lectures, delivering extended aesthetic arguments indistinguishable in sophistication from the narrator’s own discourse. Quoted passages from another writer create a layered, quasi-scholarly structure, allowing Ellison to criticise conventional theories and articulate his own. Characterisation therefore occurs chiefly through ideas, tastes, and judgments rather than action or spontaneous speech.

The descriptive method is panoramic, sequential, and immersive. The approach to Arnheim is staged as a controlled progression from domestic pastoral scenery through retirement and solitude into strangeness, enclosure, revelation, and enchantment. The visitor’s movement by river provides a mobile point of view, enabling the landscape to unfold in carefully timed visual surprises. Curves, narrowing channels, blocked sightlines, sudden openings, and contrasting zones of darkness and radiance make the setting function like a designed theatrical experience. Pacing slows markedly during these scenes as the narrator catalogues spatial relations, angles, dimensions, vegetation, colours, surfaces, and optical effects.

Imagery is ornate and synesthetic. Water becomes a mirror or “inverted heaven”; moss forms funereal drapery; flower-covered slopes resemble a cataract of rubies, sapphires, opals, and onyxes; ripples seem to produce melancholy music. Colours are saturated and jewel-like, textures are tactile and luxurious, and natural forms are repeatedly compared with crafted substances such as satin, ivory, velvet, and arabesque ornament. These comparisons deliberately blur the distinction between organic nature and human artifice. Personification and supernatural suggestion further transform the estate into an enchanted realm governed by invisible intelligence.

The governing aesthetic is one of disciplined excess. The prose delights in magnificence, abundance, and extremity, but insists that beauty depends upon composition, proportion, concealment, and controlled revelation. Arnheim is not merely lush; every apparent wilderness is purged of debris, every vista is framed, and every transition is calculated. The resulting atmosphere combines pastoral tranquillity, Gothic gloom, fairy-tale enchantment, and religious sublimity. Beauty is treated as a spiritual force perceived through physical form, while landscape gardening becomes a mediating art between human craft and divine creation.

Overall, the style is philosophically speculative, rhetorically elaborate, and sensuously pictorial. Its characteristic movement is from abstraction to embodiment: an idea about happiness becomes a life plan, a theory of poetic creation becomes landscape design, and aesthetic doctrine culminates in an immersive paradise. The prose seeks not realism in the ordinary social sense but the credible presentation of an ideal—an earthly environment so vast, refined, and strange that it appears to be the handiwork of beings suspended between humanity and God.

At the dedication of a new building at a boys’ reformatory, a series of prominent speakers praise the State’s generosity and urge the inmates to repay it by becoming upright citizens. Philip Grayson, a respected candidate for governor and the final speaker, grows increasingly disturbed by their condescending platitudes. He realizes that such speeches only reinforce the gulf between supposedly virtuous men and the boys labeled as bad.

Abandoning his prepared remarks and disregarding the risk to his political career, Grayson speaks candidly about his own troubled youth. He reveals that he was rebellious, drank, gambled, and once fired a revolver at his best friend in anger. He escaped prison only because he missed and his companions concealed the incident. The remorse that followed forced him to confront the good and evil within himself and begin the difficult struggle for self-mastery.

Grayson tells the boys that everyone shares this inner conflict and that genuine happiness comes from fighting one’s weaknesses rather than surrendering to them. He promises practical help if, after leaving the reformatory, they find the world less welcoming than they have been told. The boys respond with heartfelt applause and renewed hope. Although his party manager warns that the confession may cost him the election, Grayson accepts the consequence, valuing the boys’ transformation more than political victory. As they leave, the storm clears and the young trees outside rise unharmed, symbolizing resilience and renewal.

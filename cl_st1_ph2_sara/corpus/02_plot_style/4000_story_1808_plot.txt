After the Revolutionary War, promoters draw settlers west of the Ohio River with glowing descriptions of fertile soil, abundant wildlife, and a mild climate. New England pioneers led by veterans such as Rufus Putnam reach the Muskingum River in 1788 and establish Marietta. Despite frost, famine, disease, and threats from Native Americans, they build defenses, farms, mills, schools, churches, and civic institutions, creating a durable community through hard work and mutual aid.

Nearby, French emigrants deceived by the Scioto Company arrive at Gallipolis expecting an easy paradise. Unprepared for frontier farming and survival, they suffer from cold, hunger, fever, and invalid land claims. Most abandon the settlement, and only a small remnant remains when Congress eventually grants them land.

Other groups establish settlements across Ohio. New Jersey settlers found Cincinnati in dangerous territory contested by the Miamis and Shawnees, relying on fortified stations and Fort Washington while enduring attacks and captivity. Virginians establish Massie’s Station, later developing Chillicothe, and Connecticut settlers arrive at Conneaut in 1796. Together, these communities mark the successive beginnings of permanent settlement in Ohio, with their survival depending less on promised abundance than on preparation, resilience, cooperation, and defense.

The story is written in a nineteenth-century Gothic-romance mode, combining psychological allegory, supernatural suggestion, moral reflection, social satire, and melodrama. Its central method is to give an inward moral disorder a grotesquely physical form: the “snake in the bosom” is presented simultaneously as a possible reptile, a delusion, a symptom of disease, and a symbol of jealousy, guilt, egotism, or concealed sin. The narrative deliberately refuses to settle which explanation is literally true. Qualifying phrases such as “if report be trustworthy,” “if a serpent there actually was,” and “whether it be actual or imaginary” preserve this ambiguity while allowing the symbolic meaning to remain emphatic.

The narrator is third-person omniscient and strongly intrusive. Although the action is often filtered through Herkimer’s perceptions, the narrative voice moves freely into Roderick’s history, the opinions of townspeople, medical conjecture, and broad observations about human nature. It regularly pauses the story to interpret events, pose rhetorical questions, or pronounce general truths. These interventions give the prose the character of a moral essay embedded within a tale. The narrator addresses the reader through inclusive formulations such as “we may well imagine,” guides judgment, and openly identifies the emblematic significance of characters and incidents.

The diction is elevated, formal, and markedly period-specific. Latinate abstractions—“beneficence,” “sensibility,” “individuality,” “profligacy,” “morbidness,” and “contemplation”—appear beside biblical, theological, and infernal vocabulary such as “Providence,” “Creator,” “fiend,” “devil worship,” “conscience,” and “Eternity.” Archaic or ceremonious expressions including “‘tis,” “quoth,” “thence,” “whence,” “sui generis,” and “must needs be” reinforce the antique literary atmosphere. Characters are frequently designated by descriptive epithets rather than names: “the young sculptor,” “the snake-possessed,” “the miserable sufferer,” “the brother hater,” and “the divine.” This practice turns them partly into moral types.

The syntax is elaborate and often periodic. Long sentences accumulate subordinate clauses, parenthetical qualifications, balanced alternatives, and explanatory digressions before arriving at their main point. Semicolons and em dashes control layered trains of thought, while parallel phrasing creates an oratorical rhythm. Antithesis is especially prominent: mind is set against body, reality against delusion, love against loathing, concealment against exposure, and self-absorption against self-forgetting. Short exclamations interrupt these extended constructions at moments of emotional pressure. “It gnaws me! It gnaws me!” functions as a refrain, giving Roderick’s suffering an incantatory and obsessive quality.

Imagery is dense, emblematic, and consistently organized around serpents, bodily invasion, poison, disease, and consumption. The snake coils, gnaws, stings, hisses, wriggles, and feeds upon the heart; moral defects become reptiles bred inside the body. Related images of cancers, cankers, ulcers, caverns, fortresses, and poisoned vitals strengthen the fusion of psychological and physical corruption. Roderick’s appearance and movement echo the governing symbol: he has glittering eyes, a greenish complexion, and an undulating gait. Even the sculptor’s artistic perceptions participate in this pattern when he compares Roderick to greenish marble and to a carved image of Envy with snaky locks.

The setting contributes a contrasting symbolic register. Streets, crowds, handbills, quacks, police, gossip, and public scandal create a socially concrete urban world, while the ancestral mansion, old elms, shadowed garden, arbor, and timeless fountain evoke romance and fable. Light and darkness are morally charged: Roderick retreats from sunshine into midnight, whereas the final reconciliation occurs beside clear water and “fleckered sunshine.” The fountain suggests cleansing, renewal, and continuity, making it an apt location for the serpent’s possible departure.

Dialogue is heightened rather than naturalistic. Roderick speaks in taunts, riddles, aphorisms, repetitions, and theatrical exclamations, while Rosina’s language is gentle, devotional, and idealized. Herkimer often serves as rational witness and moral interpreter. Exchanges are built around confrontation and revelation, with gestures—clutching the breast, fixing a penetrating gaze, laying a hand upon another person—carrying as much significance as speech. The reported hiss repeatedly crosses the boundary between sound effect and supernatural evidence.

The narrative structure is framed by Herkimer’s return and mission, but much of the middle consists of retrospective exposition and a catalogue of illustrative encounters. Roderick applies the serpent metaphor to hatred, political ambition, avarice, alcoholism, religious wrath, envy, sexual misconduct, jealousy, and domestic spite. This episodic sequence broadens an individual affliction into a satirical anatomy of society. It also creates cumulative variation: the same image is repeatedly adapted to different vices, sometimes with grotesque humor, sometimes with genuine pathos. The asylum episode intensifies the Gothic horror through increasingly futile medical treatments before the narrative returns to the opening situation and moves toward reconciliation.

The tone shifts among compassion, horror, irony, satire, philosophical seriousness, and religious hope. The grotesque premise is treated with enough solemnity to remain disturbing, but comic touches puncture social authority: doctors, quacks, respectable citizens, politicians, clergymen, and gossips are all exposed to ridicule. The narrator’s irony is especially evident when dyspepsia is proposed as the grand explanation, when society confines the man who reveals its hidden vices, and when citizens appear visibly relieved after he is removed. Yet Roderick is never merely comic; his misery receives sustained psychological sympathy.

Characterization is symbolic and ethically polarized. Roderick embodies diseased self-consciousness and jealousy; Rosina represents forgiving, selfless love; Herkimer functions as artist, mediator, witness, and interpreter. The sculptor’s profession is thematically useful because his trained eye can recognize the human form beneath distortion, just as the narrative searches for moral reality beneath physical appearance. Rosina is rendered less as a psychologically complex individual than as an ideal of redemptive womanhood. Scipio is likewise presented through a conventionalized and historically dated racial-servant type, reflecting the social assumptions of the period rather than receiving independent interior development.

The ending makes explicit what the preceding symbolism has implied. Roderick’s cure comes through forgetting the self “in the idea of another,” so pathological egotism is answered by sympathy, love, forgiveness, and renewed relation. The conclusion does not entirely abandon supernatural ambiguity—the snake may be seen leaving—but it subordinates literal explanation to moral truth. Overall, the style is ornate, allegorical, rhetorically self-conscious, and psychologically Gothic, using a sustained grotesque metaphor to dramatize the destructive power of jealousy and obsessive self-contemplation.

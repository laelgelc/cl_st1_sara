After Parson Carryl’s wife dies, the capable young Huldah stays on as his housekeeper. She manages the household so expertly that the absent-minded minister barely notices the loss of its former mistress and grows increasingly fond of her.

Jealous parish women, especially the widowed Mrs. Pipperidge, insist that Huldah is too inexperienced and urge the parson to supervise domestic matters himself. His attempts end comically: he tries to force a tom-turkey to hatch eggs, only to have them crushed, and later mistakes a new well curb for a pigpen and throws a pig into the well. Huldah quietly repairs each disaster, raises turkeys by setting the eggs under a hen, builds a proper pigpen, and purchases two pigs. Recognizing his incompetence in practical affairs, the parson entrusts everything to her.

Under Huldah’s care, the household and garden flourish. Her singing and cheerful presence inspire the minister’s sermons, while her skill wins over the workers and keeps others from taking advantage of him. When the parish women inspect the house before an important church gathering, they can find no fault. They then begin gossiping that Huldah is improperly pursuing the minister.

Hurt by the rumors, Huldah considers leaving. The parson, who has come to value and love her, proposes marriage as the surest way to stop the gossip. She accepts, and they marry privately. Their appearance together at church astonishes the congregation and frustrates Mrs. Pipperidge, while the parson wryly remarks that, since everyone wanted to talk about him and Huldah, he has finally given them something worth discussing.

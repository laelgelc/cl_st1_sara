The story uses a restrained, observant third-person narrator who moves fluidly between panoramic description, collective psychology, and close access to individual minds. The voice is authoritative and faintly ironic, especially when commenting on religion, madness, and the Gaffer’s severe theology, but it never trivialises the men’s suffering. Supernatural possibilities are kept deliberately unresolved: footprints, the lifted latch, the apparent seventh worshipper, and Long Ede’s vision are rendered as concrete experiences while remaining open to explanations involving hallucination, providence, or both.

The prose combines documentary exactness with atmospheric intensity. The hut is mapped almost architecturally—its dimensions, bunks, trapdoors, fireplace, ventilation, and stores are all carefully specified—and practical details of Arctic survival establish credibility before the uncanny element enters. Material objects recur as psychological anchors: polished brass latches, greasy cards, smoke layers, ash, wreck-wood, books, guns, sleeping-bags, and frozen footprints. Such realism makes small disturbances, particularly the movement of the latch, disproportionately frightening. The enclosed, overheated, smoke-darkened hut contrasts with the vast, lucid, lethal moonlit landscape outside.

Sentence structure varies according to narrative pressure. Long, clause-rich sentences dominate description and reflection, often accumulating spatial or sensory detail through semicolons, dashes, and parenthetical qualifications. During moments of alarm, the prose contracts into short statements, fragments, repetitions, and isolated perceptions: listening, waiting, movement, and silence are broken into discrete beats. Ellipses reproduce hesitation, exhaustion, and disordered thought, while dashes mark interruptions or abrupt emotional turns. Repeated words and syntactic patterns—“listen,” “count,” “the little foxes,” and the successive enumerations of the men—create an incantatory effect and suggest minds caught in obsessive loops.

The diction mixes formal literary narration with nautical, regional, and working-class speech. Technical vocabulary concerning ships, weather, shelter, firearms, clothing, and ice gives the setting occupational authenticity. Dialogue is strongly differentiated through eye dialect and idiom: Scots, Irish, Cornish, and London-inflected voices appear in forms such as “ye,” “mane,” “aught,” “dessay,” and “presairved.” Nicknames such as the Gaffer and the Snipe reinforce the closed masculine culture of the crew. The men’s speech is generally terse and practical, but fear makes it repetitive, accusatory, or prayerful. Their seafaring code of discipline and tenderness is revealed through action and correction rather than explicit moral exposition.

Sensory imagery is dense but controlled. Smoke, cold, moonlight, blood, ash, breath, and sound form the principal image system. Silence is repeatedly made almost physical: it “ached,” breath “tightened,” and ash could nearly be heard falling. Warmth is both protection and confinement, while cold brings danger but also temporary clarity. Colour is limited to greys, blacks, pale violet, blue smoke, white snow, and small red smears of blood, producing a stark, near-monochrome visual field. Against this deprivation, Long Ede’s imagined green fields, running brooks, flowers, spices, and spring scents become sensuously excessive and signal both delirium and spiritual longing.

Biblical language is central to the story’s texture. Long Ede’s reading of the Song of Solomon supplies the refrain about “the little foxes,” which shifts from devotional quotation to mental compulsion and then to an ambiguous key for the nocturnal visitation. The lush fertility of the verses sharply opposes the sterile Arctic night. Prayer, election, intercession, resurrection-like recovery, and the long-awaited return of the sun give the plot a religious shape, yet the narrator maintains gentle scepticism. The Gaffer’s reading of *The Turkish Spy* and *Paradise Lost* adds another layer of literary and theological irony: books are sources of comfort, bewilderment, distraction, and interpretive possibility rather than stable truth.

Characterisation is economical and behavioural. Each man is introduced through origin, role, habitual posture, possessions, illness, or speech. The Gaffer’s calm authority appears in his measured commands and private anxieties; Cooney’s volatility in his opening and closing hands and repeated retreats; Lashman’s deterioration in his incessant complaints; and Long Ede’s reliability in the very fact that he fears losing his reason. The group functions almost as a single organism whose nerves are fraying. Counting becomes especially important because the fixed number of occupants represents order: the inexplicable “seventh” destabilises arithmetic, identity, and reality itself.

The narrative is tightly staged and progressively suspenseful. It begins with static exposition, introduces interpersonal strain, then narrows attention to an ambiguous sound at the door. The lifted latch serves as the first major uncanny climax; the bloody footprints deepen the mystery; Long Ede’s concealment of them isolates knowledge within one unstable consciousness. His discovery at the threshold resembles a return from death, and the mistaken count during prayer extends the haunting into the communal space. The later revelation of the sun releases the accumulated pressure and retrospectively connects the apparition with hope and survival. A final summer scene provides temporal distance and physical rescue, but the closing exchange preserves metaphysical ambiguity through the Gaffer’s dry, sectarian qualification.

The overall tone moves from claustrophobic endurance through psychological horror to guarded wonder and understated comedy. Humour remains low-key and character-based, arising from the Gaffer’s theology, the battered card game, the incongruity of popular song in the Arctic, and the final claim that the others may have been saved only “incidentally.” This tonal balance prevents the story from becoming either straightforward ghost fiction or simple religious allegory. Its distinctive style lies in the fusion of exact maritime realism, dialect-rich ensemble drama, Biblical symbolism, slow-burn suspense, and an ending that permits miracle and madness to coexist.

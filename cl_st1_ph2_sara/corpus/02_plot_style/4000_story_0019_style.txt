The story adopts the style of a frontier reminiscence crossed with an oral tall tale. It begins in a matter-of-fact first-person plural voice, grounding the action in a specific cattle drive, historical period, and geography. This framing narrator speaks as an experienced trail hand and establishes an atmosphere of communal leisure in which practical jokes and storytelling arise naturally. The narrative then shifts into an extended story within the story, delivered almost entirely in Stubb’s first-person voice. That embedded account dominates the piece and reproduces the expansive, digressive manner of a camp-fire performance rather than the compression of a tightly plotted modern short story.

The tone blends documentary realism, affectionate comedy, swagger, and understated violence. Precise references to herd sizes, brands, ages of cattle, river crossings, routes, distances, equipment, and trail duties give the story an air of firsthand authenticity. This occupational detail is not merely decorative: it supplies the standards by which the cowboys judge competence, courage, and status. Against this realistic background, Stubb’s boastful self-presentation creates comic exaggeration. He repeatedly depicts himself and his men as exceptionally capable while dismissing rivals as inept, windy, or cowardly. His insistence that he dislikes discussing the episode, despite telling it at enormous length and casting himself in a heroic role, is the central irony.

The diction is heavily marked by period cowboy vernacular. Expressions such as “boss-man,” “on herd,” “nooned,” “moseying,” “rustle,” “on the prod,” “copper every play,” and “the stuff’s off” immerse the reader in the social and linguistic world of the cattle trail. Figurative insults and comparisons are especially prominent: people are measured against hens, toads, hogs, squaws, augers, and other concrete objects drawn from frontier life. Euphemisms for death—“cashed in,” “taking their last nap,” and having one’s “light” go out—keep the violence within the narrator’s laconic idiom. Eye dialect and colloquial grammar, including forms such as “Injun,” “drownding,” “ain’t,” and “plumb,” reproduce spoken cadence and signal regional identity, though some terms reflect the stereotypes and prejudices of the historical milieu.

Dialogue is the chief engine of characterization. The men reveal themselves through teasing, threats, boasts, evasions, and ritualized challenges rather than through psychological analysis. Much of the conflict develops as a contest of speech before it becomes a gunfight. Politeness and aggression continually overlap: antagonists address one another as “sir,” “pardner,” or “old man” while issuing insults and ultimatums. Stubb’s language is theatrical and strategically provocative, full of mock courtesy, elaborate put-downs, gambling metaphors, and claims of reluctant self-defence. The rival foreman answers in the same code of masculine pride, so that the eventual violence appears as the culmination of escalating verbal performances.

The syntax alternates between compact comic statements and long, loosely coordinated narrative sentences. Stubb frequently begins paragraphs and incidents with “Well,” “You see,” or “Now,” mimicking spontaneous speech and allowing him to digress without losing his audience. Clauses accumulate through “and,” “but,” and “so,” producing the forward roll of an anecdote told aloud. Parenthetical qualifications and side stories interrupt the main line, as in the accounts of old Andy, the drowned horse, river-crossing technique, and Stubb’s drinking habits. These digressions deepen the sense of oral authenticity and delay the promised explanation, making Lucy—and the reader—wait for the revelation.

Humour depends on incongruity, hyperbole, deadpan understatement, and character contrast. Stubb is introduced as a short, fat, amiable drinker with a perpetual laugh, yet he casts his younger self as an intimidating trail boss surrounded by expert gunmen. The narrator describes lethal conflict in the same practical tone used for moving cattle or saddling horses. A gunfight that kills three men becomes “a minute’s work,” while burying the bodies is a “planting act.” The final explanation that the corpses were moved because sand was easier to dig punctures the grandeur of the preceding tale with an anti-climactic joke. Lucy’s literal-minded question also restores the frame narrative and exposes how far Stubb has wandered from the original subject.

Characterization is broad, vivid, and socially based. Stubb is defined by physical caricature, drinking, humour, professional pride, and performative bravado. Lucy functions primarily as the innocent audience whose curiosity makes him vulnerable to the joke. Ike Inks quietly stages the entertainment, while the other cowboys form an implicit audience that understands the deception. Secondary figures in Stubb’s tale tend to be identified by occupation, nickname, physical trait, or practical ability—“one-eyed Jim,” “Big Dick,” the cook, the boss-man—reflecting a masculine working culture in which reputation and competence outweigh interior complexity.

The structure is circular and strongly controlled beneath its apparent discursiveness. The opening establishes the forked trails, the three graves, and Lucy’s curiosity. Stubb then invents a causal history that connects all three features through rivalry, stampede tactics, and a shoot-out. The close returns to Lucy’s original concern and supplies a bathetic practical explanation for the burial site. Repetition of “I never like to refer to it” brackets Stubb’s performance and heightens its comic unreliability. The tale remains deliberately ambiguous in factual status: its abundance of technical detail makes it sound credible, while its heroic self-inflation and the prank’s setup mark it as a likely fabrication.

Overall, the style is robust, anecdotal, masculine, and performative. It values local colour, occupational precision, verbal sparring, and communal humour over introspection. Violence is treated through frontier fatalism rather than moral meditation, while pride and aggression are both celebrated and gently mocked. The result is a mock-heroic cowboy yarn whose realism lies in its trail knowledge and speech patterns, and whose literary force comes from the tension between Stubb’s solemn delivery and the audience’s awareness that he is spinning a story.

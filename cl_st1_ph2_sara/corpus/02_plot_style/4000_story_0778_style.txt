The story uses a polished late-nineteenth- or early-twentieth-century realist style, enriched by aestheticism, psychological symbolism, and touches of the Gothic. Its social world—Parisian studios, salons, exhibitions, fashionable households, and European travel—is rendered with cosmopolitan assurance. References to Circe, Manet, the Comédie-Française, medieval grotesques, and French geography establish an art-conscious milieu in which physical beauty and deformity become both the subject of painting and the governing metaphors of the narrative.

The narration is third-person and largely omniscient, though it remains closely aligned with Aaron Dunlap’s perceptions. Much of the story’s psychological force comes from free indirect discourse: the narrator moves fluidly into Dunlap’s pity, revulsion, idealism, disappointment, and suppressed anger without consistently marking these thoughts as direct interior speech. This closeness makes his interpretations initially persuasive, but the ending exposes their limitations. Dunlap repeatedly imagines Virginia’s scar as evidence of hidden depth and suffering, and the narrative gradually reveals that this belief is an aesthetic and sentimental projection. The resulting irony is subtle rather than openly corrective.

The prose is formal, cultivated, and emotionally controlled. Its diction is often elevated—“physiognomy,” “lineaments,” “expiator,” “vituperation,” “tragic serenity”—and is interwoven with French terms and phrases that reinforce the continental setting. Long, balanced sentences permit fine distinctions of motive and perception, while semicolons, dashes, appositive phrases, and cumulative lists create an elaborate, measured rhythm. Short sentences and direct dialogue are reserved for moments of rupture, such as Dunlap’s reference to Virginia’s scar or the account of Eleanor Vane’s burning, and therefore strike with unusual force.

Description is painterly and tactile. Faces, bodies, clothing, light, and color are treated with the precision of portraiture: “flower-tinted” skin, “twining, silken hair,” violet twilight, gray quays, pink chiffon, gold ornaments, scorched hair, and blackened hands. The story frequently arranges scenes as visual compositions, controlling profile, angle, concealment, and revelation as a painter might. Virginia is first apprehended through the contrast between her unmarred profile and the scarred side of her face, a visual opposition that becomes the structural basis of Dunlap’s entire understanding of her.

Figurative language is abundant but disciplined. Beauty is associated with flowers, light, youth, delicacy, and fleetingness, while cruelty and resentment are expressed through animal and reptilian imagery. The opening herd of hybrid creatures prepares for later references to masks, enchantment, beasts, coils, shell-like eyes, and tortured animals. The scar itself expands symbolically: at first it is a local wound that Dunlap romanticizes, but eventually it seems to spread across Virginia’s whole countenance and to signify a deeper moral injury. Fire likewise functions as both literal agent and symbolic force, joining Virginia’s original disfigurement to Eleanor Vane’s suspiciously caused burns.

The narrative is carefully architectured. An introductory debate about whether bodily mutilation belongs in art establishes the central aesthetic question before the protagonist is introduced. Three numbered sections then trace a movement from sympathy and idealization, through marriage and disillusionment, to confrontation, violence, and ironic aftermath. Information is often delayed so that the reader experiences revelation alongside Dunlap: Virginia’s scar is initially referred to only as “it,” and its nature emerges through covert observation. The final paragraph withdraws from intimate psychological narration into the simplifying voice of society, reducing a morally tangled history to the crude coincidence that both of Dunlap’s wives bore facial scars.

Characterization depends strongly on contrast and reversal. Dunlap is compassionate yet capable of cruelty; Virginia appears courageous and wounded but becomes vain, neglectful, and possibly murderous; Eleanor Vane is quiet, nurturing, and physically sacrificed. The story repeatedly reverses surface and essence. Visible deformity does not guarantee inward nobility, beauty does not imply goodness, sympathy can become possessive, and artistic sensitivity can conceal vanity or moral presumption. These reversals generate the story’s tragic irony.

Dialogue is sparse, socially restrained, and often more revealing through implication than declaration. Characters speak in the guarded idiom of cultivated society, using carelessness, understatement, or unnatural laughter to conceal emotional violence. Virginia’s apparently casual remarks, Dunlap’s brittle criticism, and the servants’ factual account of the explosion all rely on subtext. The climactic crime is never explicitly named or proved; suspicion arises through the lamp’s history, Virginia’s departure, and the sequence of events. This indirection preserves social decorum on the surface while allowing menace to gather underneath.

The emotional tone evolves from wistful tenderness to claustrophobic dread. Early Paris is personified as a benevolent feminine teacher who heals Dunlap’s childhood wounds and educates his eye. As his marriage deteriorates, the same city becomes sordid and estranging. The story’s sentiment is therefore moderated by irony and fatalism: Dunlap believes beauty, love, art, and maternity can redeem suffering, but each belief is defeated. The overall effect is elegant, melancholy, psychologically probing, and morally ambiguous, with violence conveyed less through spectacle than through the gradual corruption of perception, intimacy, and sympathy.

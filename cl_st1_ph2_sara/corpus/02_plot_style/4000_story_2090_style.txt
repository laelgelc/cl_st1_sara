The story uses a closely observed realist style, combining detailed urban description with understated moral irony. Its Dublin setting is rendered through precise place names, streets, landmarks, shops, trams, public houses and social routines, giving the narrative a strong sense of geography and making movement through the city central to its form. The opening description briefly elevates the city into a luminous, almost impressionistic spectacle: lamps resemble “illumined pearls,” the crowd becomes a “living texture,” and sound rises as an “unchanging unceasing murmur.” This lyrical surface is gradually undercut by poverty, exploitation, weariness and emotional sterility.

The narration is in the third person and initially observes both men from outside, but it later moves into a close alignment with Lenehan’s perceptions and thoughts. The resulting free-indirect mode allows the narrative to register his hunger, insecurity, envy and faint hopes without a formal change of voice. Character is disclosed less through explicit judgment than through gesture, dress, bodily detail, habits of speech and patterns of movement. Lenehan’s “wheezing laughter,” scant grey hair, jaunty clothing and “ravaged look” reveal the contradiction between performed youthfulness and premature exhaustion. Corley’s bulk, oily head, mechanical gait and habit of staring forward express vanity, coarseness and self-importance. Physical description often becomes implicit moral portraiture, though the narrator retains enough distance to avoid simple denunciation.

The prose alternates between long, carefully balanced descriptive sentences and short, plain narrative statements. Repetition and parallel construction give certain passages a rhythmic, incantatory quality, as in the repeated language of changing form and unchanging sound. At other moments, accumulations of concrete details create a cataloguing effect: clothes, facial features, food, shop signs and street furniture are recorded point by point. The diction ranges from elevated, literary phrasing—“repose,” “mournful music,” “vanquished in spirit”—to colloquial Dublin speech and slang such as “tart,” “slavey,” “the real cheese,” “pull it off” and “takes the biscuit.” This contrast exposes the gap between the characters’ grandiose self-presentation and the sordidness of their conduct.

Dialogue is frequent, economical and strongly individualized. Corley speaks in repetitive, self-satisfied anecdotes, repeatedly using “man” and “you know,” while Lenehan relies on theatrical compliments, borrowed sophistication and comic exaggeration. His phrases such as “gay Lothario” and “recherche biscuit” display verbal agility but also servility: language is a social tool used to flatter, insinuate and secure inclusion. Pauses, ellipses, exclamations and clipped questions reproduce the hesitant rhythms of speech and intensify the suspense surrounding the unnamed “ticklish job.” Much of the scheme remains euphemistic until the final image, so implication carries more weight than direct explanation.

The tone is detached, ironic and quietly bleak. Humour arises from inflated language, grotesque bodily description and the discrepancy between how the men see themselves and how the narration presents them. Yet the comedy is not carefree. Lenehan’s solitary wandering reveals fatigue, loneliness and economic desperation, complicating his role as comic parasite. The narrative grants him a limited inward pathos without absolving him of complicity. Corley receives less psychological depth and functions more starkly as a figure of domination, treating courtship as conquest and the servant woman as a source of money and goods.

Imagery repeatedly links people with objects, mechanisms and performances. Expressions move across Lenehan’s face like waves; Corley’s head is a ball on a pivot and his hat resembles one bulb growing from another; his walk has “something of the conqueror” in it. Clothing acts as social camouflage, expressing aspirations to youth, gentility or status. Food imagery is equally important: biscuits begin as a comic idiom, while the peas and ginger beer later expose actual hunger. Money remains unnamed for much of the story but silently governs relationships, companionship and desire.

Sound and music provide a symbolic countercurrent. The weary harp and the mournful air briefly interrupt the men’s coarse conversation, lending the city an atmosphere of historical sadness. The personified harp, exposed and tired of both spectators and its player, suggests degradation and exploitation; its melody later controls Lenehan’s steps and fingers, following him into solitude. The moon, twilight, dim streets and eventual light rain likewise create an atmosphere of uncertainty and decline. Light is used selectively: lamps illuminate the crowd at the beginning, while the final lamp reveals the gold coin that confirms the scheme’s success.

The structure is linear and organized around walking, waiting and return. It begins with the two men together, separates them while Corley pursues the servant woman, follows Lenehan through a long interval of aimless wandering, and reunites them for the final disclosure. The middle section slows considerably, turning outward suspense into inward character study. Clock references and meeting times create a quiet temporal framework, while repeated routes and street names make the characters seem trapped in circuits rather than progressing toward meaningful change. The closing sequence accelerates through short actions, pursuit and unanswered questions.

Suspense depends on concealment and delay. The precise object of Corley’s plan is never fully stated beforehand, and Lenehan’s mounting uncertainty directs attention toward the outcome. The ending supplies a compact visual epiphany rather than an explanatory resolution: Corley slowly opens his hand beneath a lamp, and a gold coin shines in his palm. The grave, ceremonial gesture grotesquely ennobles a petty act of exploitation. Ending on the coin concentrates the story’s concerns—money, power, betrayal, performance and moral emptiness—into a single image.

Overall, the style is naturalistic in social detail, lyrical in selected descriptions, comic in dialogue and characterization, and modernist in its use of free-indirect thought, symbolism, omission and epiphanic closure. Its moral force remains indirect. Rather than openly instructing the reader, it allows urban atmosphere, bodily observation, verbal irony and the final material symbol to expose lives shaped by idleness, poverty, dependency and predation.

The story adopts the form of a comic social essay rather than a conventionally plotted short story. An authoritative first-person narrator briefly surfaces in phrases such as “as I have said,” but the dominant mode is generalized third-person observation: “the barber” and “the customer” function as social types rather than individualized characters. The narrator speaks with mock certainty, presenting absurd claims as established facts and thereby parodying the tone of journalism, sociology, professional instruction, and academic commentary.

Its prevailing tone is dry, urbane, satirical, and increasingly farcical. Ordinary aspects of a barber-shop visit—sports conversation, hot towels, product recommendations, and extra services—are reinterpreted as indoctrination, cooking, interrogation, extortion, and physical assault. The humor depends heavily on deadpan delivery: the narrator never signals disbelief but calmly explains that shaving is merely “incidental” to the barber’s true vocation of imparting information. This tonal composure makes each escalation seem logically connected to the last, even when the customer is being gagged, boiled, varnished, or attacked with clippers.

Hyperbole is the central comic device. Minor discomforts become scenes of captivity and violence: customers are “strapped, manacled, gagged with soap,” subjected to “mental torture,” and surrounded by barbers fighting to perform unnecessary services. The exaggeration develops cumulatively. A recognizable annoyance is introduced, expanded into an absurd premise, and then pushed toward grotesque physical comedy. Culinary imagery turns the customer into something being boiled and served with vegetables; medical and penal imagery casts him as a patient, victim, or prisoner; sporting imagery converts the country shave into a “rough-and-tumble” fight. These shifting metaphorical frames continually renew the central joke.

The prose combines formal, Latinate language with colloquial speech and blunt physical action. Phrases such as “proximate loss,” “indomitable industry,” “subsidiary services,” and “natural aptitude” lend mock dignity to trivial or ridiculous matters. Against this elevated register, the barber’s sales talk is brisk and repetitive: “Better let me give you a shampoo?” followed by the customer’s recurring “No.” The contrast between elaborate narration and clipped dialogue creates comic rhythm. Invented commercial names such as “Hairocene,” “Rejuvenator,” and the “Hydro-magnetic treatment” parody the pseudo-scientific language of advertising and beauty products.

Sentence structure is generally expansive and cumulative. Long sentences are built from coordinated clauses, lists, qualifications, and escalating consequences, allowing an absurd idea to gather momentum before arriving at a comic final image. Enumeration is especially prominent in the fictional barber-college curriculum and the catalogue of services performed on the customer. The mock syllabus, with numbered subjects and capitalized course titles, imitates institutional documentation while filling it with grotesque content such as “The Scalp and How to Lift it.” Short sentences and one-word replies interrupt these longer constructions, sharpening punch lines and accelerating exchanges.

Characterization is schematic and role-based. The barber is energetic, intrusive, omniscient, commercially predatory, and obsessed with sport; the customer is passive, immobilized, intimidated, and gradually reduced from a person to “an inanimate thing.” This deliberate imbalance supplies much of the comedy. The barber controls speech and action, while the customer’s resistance is limited to gurgles, eye movements, and repeated refusals. Even apparent concern about baldness or wrinkles is transparently a sales tactic, giving the satire a recognizable target in professional intimidation and manufactured consumer anxiety.

The structure is episodic and thematic. It moves from the barber’s sporting expertise to the social necessity of barber-shop information, then to questioning the captive customer, professional education, shaving technique, product sales, supplementary services, and finally the contrast between city and country shops. Transitions often take the form of mock-logical extensions—“But it must be remembered” or “But even…”—which create the illusion of a systematic argument. The ending delivers a vivid slapstick climax through the image of clippers driven up the customer’s spine like a lawn-mower through long grass. In the supplied text, the entire piece is duplicated; this repetition appears to be a file-level artifact rather than an intentional stylistic feature.

Overall, the style is best described as deadpan comic satire built from mock exposition, sustained exaggeration, incongruous metaphor, pseudo-technical diction, rhythmic lists, and escalating physical farce. Its distinctive effect comes from treating everyday barber-shop irritations with the solemnity of scientific analysis while describing increasingly impossible brutality as routine professional practice.

The story uses a brisk, comic-realist style with strong elements of social satire and caricature. Its central perspective stays close to Croly Addicks, a timid, underpaid office clerk whose lack of a prominent chin becomes both a physical joke and a symbol of his inability to assert himself. The narration is third-person and broadly omniscient, but it frequently enters Croly’s thoughts and feelings through free indirect discourse. This creates sympathy for him while preserving enough narrative distance to expose the absurdity of his self-pity, his fantasies of rebellion, and the social world that equates aggression with competence.

The narrative voice is witty, intrusive, and fond of ironic commentary. It often pauses to explain institutions, customs, or motives in mock-serious terms, as in the account of the “conscience beanery” and its elaborate system of surveillance. Such passages imitate factual exposition while steadily accumulating comic incongruities. The narrator also addresses the reader directly or invokes shared popular experience—“as you’ve seen it done in the movies”—which gives the story an informal, companionable quality. Hyperbole, anticlimax, and dry qualifications are recurrent devices: the piano company is a “vast far-flung enterprise,” its slogan is true “with only slight exaggeration,” and Croly is buried in the ludicrously subordinate position of “assistant to the assistant purchasing agent.”

Characterization is highly visual and often grotesque. Faces and bodies are reduced to exaggerated shapes: Croly’s convex face retreats from Cowdin’s concave features; Cowdin has a “well-razored knob of blue-black jaw”; the spotter possesses bulk, a derby hat, and a calculated instinct for safe victims. The opposition between Croly’s missing chin and the jutting jaws of dominant men organizes both the imagery and the psychology. “Chinlessness” becomes a repeated motif, almost a governing metaphor for weakness, low status, and lack of conviction. The narration makes the symbolism explicit—“his character should become as chinless as his face”—in keeping with the story’s broad, fable-like comic method rather than subtle psychological realism.

The prose combines energetic physical verbs with elaborate comic description. Cowdin “rotated,” “prowled,” and “thrust” his jaw; the spotter “pounced”; Croly “quavered,” “quailed,” “stumbled,” and fidgeted with his tie. These verbs turn office and lunchroom interactions into miniature scenes of predation. Long, carefully balanced sentences are often used for satirical explanation, while short clauses and fragments sharpen moments of embarrassment or emotional injury: “Chinless! The old epithet. The old curse.” Figurative language is vivid, accessible, and deliberately overstated, including the “safety valve” of hard work, the “bushel of chinlessness,” and Croly’s heart rising to lodge below his Adam’s apple.

Dialogue is central to the story’s rhythm and social portraiture. Cowdin’s repeated, venomously respectful “Mr. Addicks” converts formal politeness into intimidation. Working-class and urban speech is rendered phonetically through forms such as “Gawsh,” “wadda yuh expeck,” “Fawty-fi’,” “pahdun,” and “come awn.” These spellings establish class, occupation, and attitude quickly, while also supplying much of the comedy. Dialogue tends to be confrontational: characters appraise weakness, ridicule inferiors, or force Croly into defensive explanations. Even minor exchanges reinforce the story’s central social law—that assertive appearance and physical confidence matter more than honesty, seniority, or knowledge.

The humour is frequently cruel, because it arises from repeated public humiliations. Croly is bullied by his superior, mocked by stenographers, falsely accused at lunch, dismissed by a soda clerk, and haunted by childhood taunts. Yet the narrative directs most of its satire toward the bullies and the institutions that reward them. Authority figures prey selectively on those unlikely to retaliate: the lunchroom spotter ignores muscular cheats but confronts Croly because experience has taught him whom he can safely attack. This pattern gives the comedy a clear social-critical edge, exposing workplace hierarchy, masculine posturing, low wages, consumer indignity, and the myth that merit naturally earns advancement.

The urban setting is built through dense sensory detail and recognizable modern institutions: offices, stenographers, invoices, basement cafeterias, Madison Square, newspaper photographs, soda fountains, and company promotion notices. Sound is especially prominent—the “jangle and clatter” of crockery, the crackle of paper, the boy’s sirenic cry—and contributes to an atmosphere in which Croly is continually overwhelmed. Brand-like names and precise sums of money lend specificity, while references to movies, boxing, the Waldorf-Astoria, and the Metropolitan Tower situate the piece in an early twentieth-century metropolitan culture fascinated by business efficiency, mass entertainment, and the “go-getter.”

Structurally, the story proceeds through an escalating sequence of indignities interrupted by exposition and retrospective reflection. The opening immediately stages Croly’s conflict with Cowdin; the lunchroom scene broadens the pattern of victimization; the park-bench passage supplies biography and romantic stakes; the soda-fountain episode confirms that the prejudice is universal; and the return to the office concentrates suspense around the promotion. Repetition is purposeful: each encounter restates Croly’s powerlessness in a new setting, while references to his chin build toward an anticipated crisis or transformation. The pace alternates between lively dramatic scenes and slower passages of explanatory satire or introspection.

Overall, the style belongs to the comic urban magazine-story tradition: accessible, plot-driven, verbally exuberant, sentimental beneath its mockery, and organized around a conspicuous personal trait that functions as both running gag and moral symbol. Its tone mixes amusement, indignation, and sympathy. Although Croly is caricatured, the accumulation of injustices gives his predicament emotional weight, while the approaching promotion decision supplies a strong narrative hinge and prepares for a likely reversal in which suppressed knowledge or anger may finally become visible.

The story adopts a stark maritime-naturalist style, combining documentary precision with escalating psychological horror. Its opening is immediate and matter-of-fact: the disaster is announced before it is dramatized, creating a sense of inevitability rather than surprise. The narrator treats the wreck as the consequence of poor seamanship, failed discipline, and human weakness, then follows those causes through an almost forensic chain of physical and moral collapse. This deterministic approach makes the sea, hunger, cold, and exhaustion feel like forces steadily stripping away civilization.

The narration is third-person omniscient but emotionally restrained. It offers judgments—describing men as “lubberly cowards,” selfish, brutal, or ineffectual—yet rarely pauses for abstract reflection. Character is revealed chiefly through conduct under pressure. Mahoney and O’Brien emerge as courageous because they act, while the captain, Sullivan, Behane, and the others expose themselves through evasion, coercion, and collective cruelty. The narrator’s controlled distance intensifies the horror: even mutilation and murder are rendered in lucid, sequential detail rather than through melodramatic exclamation.

The diction mixes nautical terminology, physical exactness, and morally charged description. Terms such as “mizzentopsail,” “lee-rail,” “hatch-coamings,” “beam ends,” “shrouds,” and “broach to” establish technical authenticity and assume familiarity with seafaring life. Alongside this specialist vocabulary are forceful sensory phrases: seas deliver “icy deluges,” a loose mast beats like a “thunderous sledge-hammer,” and the survivors stand in “sloshing water.” The physical world is repeatedly presented through weight, impact, cold, exposure, pain, and bodily depletion. Figurative language is used sparingly, so striking images—especially the rescue ship’s forefoot cutting the water “like a golden knife”—stand out sharply.

Sentence structure alternates between long, cumulative descriptive sentences and short declarative blows. Extended clauses reproduce the motion of the sea, catalogue suffering, or track a sequence of failed actions. Short sentences mark irreversible facts: men drown, food runs out, the veins prove dry, the ship appears. Repetition contributes to the sense of endurance and entrapment, particularly in the recurring measurements of time, hunger, cold, and physical weakness. Chronological markers such as “three in the morning,” “by the end of the second day,” and “by the sixteenth day” give the narrative the grim authority of a survival record.

The pacing is carefully graduated. The wreck itself occurs rapidly, but the aftermath expands into a prolonged account of deprivation. The story slows further during the drawing of lots and the attempted killing, breaking the process into minute gestures, questions, hesitations, threats, and bodily details. This temporal dilation forces attention onto the group’s moral degradation. Suspense comes less from uncertainty about whether violence will occur than from repeated opportunities for the men to stop themselves. Each hesitation briefly permits hope, only for social pressure and fear to push the action forward.

Dialogue is central to the later sections and is rendered in a strongly phonetic Irish vernacular. Spellings and constructions such as “yez,” “ut,” “iv,” “b’ys,” “wan,” and “slape” differentiate the speech from the narrator’s formal standard English and create a vivid communal voice. The dialect also makes the arguments sound immediate and embodied rather than literary. Repeated phrases—especially “a good deed,” “do yer duty,” and claims that the drawing was fair—function as coercive slogans. Euphemistic language becomes a tool of self-deception: murder is reframed as sacrifice, justice, duty, or preparation of “mate.” O’Brien’s speech moves between principled argument, brave acceptance, terror, bargaining, and curses, giving him the story’s fullest emotional range.

The dominant tonal progression is from grim realism to claustrophobic cruelty and finally to savage irony. The men’s physical suffering is never sentimentalized, but neither is it allowed to excuse their choice of a scapegoat. Their persecution of O’Brien begins before the proposed sacrifice, so the lottery appears not as an impartial survival measure but as the formalization of an existing group hatred. The supposedly fair procedure is transparently manipulable, and communal repetition converts suspicion into an accepted verdict. The story is therefore attentive to mob psychology: individuals surrender responsibility by distributing it among the group, while the weakest and most reluctant participant, the cook, is threatened into becoming the executioner.

The imagery grows increasingly bodily and grotesque. Salt sores, trembling limbs, empty veins, sawing blades, a gurgling death, and the tureen cover reduce human life to damaged flesh and potential food. Domestic objects become sinister: a tureen cover first gathers rainwater, then is used to catch blood. This transformation reflects the collapse of ordinary moral categories. Yet the most uncanny image is bloodlessness itself. O’Brien’s dry veins suspend the action between physiological realism and providential warning, allowing Mahoney to interpret the phenomenon as a moral sign without requiring the narrator to confirm a supernatural explanation.

The ending depends on extreme situational irony. Rescue arrives at the precise moment when the murder has been completed, proving that the act was not merely cruel but useless. The survivors fail to see the approaching ship because they are absorbed in killing the boy who had earlier helped maintain the lookout. This structural reversal gives the conclusion the force of judgment without explicit authorial sermonizing. The tarpaulin thrown over the corpse signifies immediate concealment and shame, while Gorman’s escalating laughter provides the final note of psychological rupture. Instead of closure or consolation, the story ends on an auditory image of madness greeting salvation.

Overall, the style is muscular, unsentimental, technically informed, and morally severe. It relies on concrete action, maritime detail, dialect dialogue, cumulative physical suffering, and tightly controlled irony. Its central effect comes from placing extraordinary horror within a plain, credible narrative frame: the prose remains disciplined while the characters lose discipline, and that contrast makes the descent into brutality especially disturbing.

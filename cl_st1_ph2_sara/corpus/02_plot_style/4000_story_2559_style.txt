The story adopts a first-person frame-narrative style in which an educated, sceptical outsider recounts an encounter with an elderly Hawaiian fisherman and oral storyteller. The narrator’s voice is urbane, ironic and self-consciously rational, but his confidence is repeatedly unsettled by Kohokumu’s wit, physical competence and imaginative authority. This creates a productive tension between Western empiricism and indigenous myth: the narrator calls traditional tales “whoppers,” yet notices that modern science oddly echoes some of their claims, while Kohokumu exposes the comparable extravagance of biblical creation. Although the period perspective sometimes carries a patronising, colonial vocabulary, the old fisherman is ultimately the more agile thinker and the dominant personality.

The prevailing tone combines comic irritation, affectionate mockery, adventure and philosophical wonder. Much of the humour comes from reversals: the frail old man is vigorous while the younger narrator is seasick and aching; the supposedly credulous fisherman defeats the rationalist in argument; and solemn myth is punctured by modern jokes, as when the sun is imagined as a trade unionist negotiating a shorter working day. Kohokumu’s shark story develops a broader folktale comedy based on repetition, absurd logic and escalating consequences. Beneath this playfulness lies a reflective, melancholy current concerning age, death, dreams, cultural memory and return to the sea.

The diction moves between several registers. The narrator uses elevated literary language—“Promethean demi-god,” “empery,” “formidable creature”—alongside colloquial judgements such as “bore,” “whopper” and “devilish fine form.” Nautical and naturalistic terms give the setting physical credibility, while Hawaiian words, names and translated expressions root the story in a specific cultural environment. Parenthetical glosses such as “wahine” and “luau” reveal an intended non-Hawaiian readership. Kohokumu’s English is rendered in ceremonious, slightly archaic patterns, including inversions, parallel clauses and vocative forms such as “O Lakana” and “O Young Wise One.” His speech therefore sounds at once translated, biblical and oral-traditional.

Sentence structure is generally expansive and cumulative. Long sentences gather mythic feats, environmental observations or bodily actions through repeated relative clauses and coordinated phrases. These are balanced by short, emphatic exchanges in dialogue, which sharpen the comedy and establish a contest of minds. Repetition is central to the oral texture: formulas recur, sharks perform the same actions in diminishing numbers, and chants employ anaphora and parallelism. Dashes, parentheses, exclamations and ellipses reproduce interruption, qualification and the drifting rhythms of speech. The narrative frequently shifts from descriptive prose into inset song, chant, prayer or tale, giving it a layered, performative quality.

The descriptive style is sensuous and strongly embodied. Heat, glare, stagnant air, nausea and the unstable motion of the outrigger convey the narrator’s discomfort, while the squid episode is rendered with vivid, almost grotesque precision: sliminess, suckers, coiling tentacles and biting teeth make the action tactile and immediate. This realism contrasts with the fantastic content of the legends, yet also supports it. Kohokumu’s extraordinary dive and mastery of the octopus make him seem nearly mythic himself, narrowing the distance between heroic tradition and observable experience. The sea is both a materially exact setting and an archetypal maternal force—dangerous, nourishing and regenerative.

Structurally, the story proceeds by digression rather than by a tightly linear plot. A present-day fishing excursion forms the outer frame, within which chants lead to debate, the squid hunt leads to a meditation on the sea, and a discussion of dreams leads to the embedded Water Baby folktale. The fishing poles repeatedly recall the characters to immediate reality and provide transitions between conversation and action. The embedded tale has the patterned architecture of oral folklore: a crisis is announced, a clever child faces numerous predators, a trick is repeated, and each repetition increases the comic absurdity. Kohokumu closes the story by offering physical “evidence” for an impossible narrative, leaving fact, memory and invention deliberately entangled.

Dialogue is the principal engine of characterization and theme. The narrator questions, interrupts and objects; Kohokumu concedes strategically, counters with analogies, anticipates objections and takes control of the exchange. His answers often turn literal reasoning against scepticism. This produces a Socratic but comic pattern in which categories such as truth, myth, dream and proof become unstable. Kohokumu’s storytelling also dramatizes oral tradition as communal knowledge: truth is guaranteed not by direct observation alone but by inheritance, place, repetition and the persistence of visible signs.

Overall, the style blends travel writing, maritime realism, ethnographic observation, philosophical dialogue and trickster folktale. Its signature qualities are robust sensory description, elaborate oral performance, ironic clashes of worldview, affectionate comic exaggeration and an unresolved movement between the factual and the marvellous. Myth is neither simply endorsed nor dismissed; instead, it becomes a way of expressing truths about nature, ancestry, identity and mortality that rational explanation cannot fully contain.

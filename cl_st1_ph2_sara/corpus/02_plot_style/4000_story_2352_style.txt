The story adopts an oral, vernacular mode rooted in the culture of colonial Australian bush camps and goldfields. Its language is strongly regional and working-class, using contractions, clipped forms and period slang such as “chaps,” “mates,” “swag,” “shanty-whisky,” “bullocky,” “ratty,” “blanky,” “yarning,” and “my oath.” Nonstandard grammar—“them sort,” “it ain’t,” “so’s,” “nor none of them”—is used deliberately to reproduce speech rather than to create a polished literary register. Explanations of local customs and character types are folded naturally into the talk, making the setting legible without abandoning the speaker’s idiom. Profanity is partly suppressed with dashes, preserving both roughness and period decorum.

The narrative is structured as a campfire frame tale. A brief exchange between Sympathy Joe and Mitchell opens into Joe’s long account of the Oracle’s past, with Mitchell’s questions and reactions periodically returning the reader to the present. Within Joe’s narration lie further layers of transmission: much of the history comes from Uncle Bob, Aunt Bob, local gossip and inference. Repeated qualifications such as “I think,” “no doubt,” “perhaps,” “I suppose,” and “anyway” give the story the texture of communal memory rather than documentary certainty. The ending closes the frame abruptly when the Oracle approaches, returning attention from the remembered young Tom Marshall to the damaged older man standing just outside the conversation.

Point of view is technically third-person at the framing level but is dominated by Joe’s first-person voice. Joe is a highly present narrator: opinionated, digressive, compassionate and sometimes teasing. He interprets motives, anticipates objections and directly instructs Mitchell how to behave around the Oracle. His storytelling often moves by association rather than strict chronology, and his repetitions and self-corrections simulate spontaneous speech. Mitchell mainly acts as listener and prompt, though his brief interjections—an oath, a question, an impatient “go on”—regulate pace and register the emotional force of each revelation.

The syntax is expansive and conversational. Long sentences accumulate clauses through conjunctions, dashes, parentheses and lists, reproducing a speaker thinking aloud. These are counterbalanced by short exchanges and blunt revelations, most notably “It wasn’t his!” The prose frequently uses parallel constructions and patterned repetition: “to and fro, to and fro,” “perhaps…or…or,” and repeated formulations built around “the sort of girl” or “anyway.” Dashes are especially prominent, marking pauses, afterthoughts, shifts in emphasis and interruptions. Capitalized words such as “ARE,” “MAKE,” “THEIR,” “COULD,” and “PRETEND” act like vocal stress, turning typography into a guide for performance.

The tone combines masculine banter, sardonic social observation, broad comedy and deep pathos. Joe begins by mocking precocious young cynics who claim to understand “this world,” then uses the Oracle’s life to expose the shallowness of that pose. The story’s humour is character-based and physical, particularly in the fight episode and Jimmy Nowlett’s excitable attack on Duigan. Yet the comedy never erases the central sorrow. Instead, tonal contrast intensifies it: boisterous goldfield violence and camp humour surround scenes of sacrifice, betrayal, illness and death.

Characterization is largely indirect. The Oracle is built through habitual gestures—walking with his hands behind him, staring across a clearing, falling deaf during conversation, reacting to old tunes—as well as through the retrospective contrast between his youthful strength and present abstraction. Joe’s opening catalogue of these behaviours initially makes him seem eccentric; the ensuing history reinterprets each habit as an outward trace of grief. Other figures are drawn rapidly through vivid physical and moral shorthand: Bertha is idealized as naturally good and faithful, Redmond is noisy and brutal, and Jimmy is “hard as cast iron” but comically volatile. Dialogue, posture and action carry more weight than psychological exposition.

The pacing follows a sequence of escalating disclosures. Joe first establishes the Oracle as misunderstood, then recounts the deceptive first relationship, the renunciation of Bertha, the discovery that the child is not Tom’s, the violent confrontation, and finally Bertha’s death. Questions from Mitchell create pauses before crucial turns, while Joe’s deliberate hesitations prolong suspense. The fight scene speeds up through forceful verbs, shouted dialogue and comic detail; the deathbed scene becomes compressed and restrained. Bertha’s death is delivered in a single concentrated gesture, followed by an ellipsis and the ordinary announcement that the Oracle is approaching.

The thematic style is moral without being formally preachy. The narrative values endurance, loyalty, mercy and quiet suffering over fashionable cynicism or performative toughness. It repeatedly contrasts appearance with reality: the supposedly “soft” Oracle possesses exceptional strength; the worldly young men know little; the fine singer is morally corrupt; and the man mocked as an “old ass” has survived experiences his mockers cannot imagine. Masculinity is treated ambivalently. Fighting, drinking and rough fellowship belong to the social world, but genuine manhood is located in restraint, responsibility, tenderness toward women and children, and the capacity to bear pain without self-dramatization.

Social criticism emerges through Joe’s reflections on gender and communal judgment. The story condemns the unequal treatment of unmarried pregnant women and criminals, and the Oracle’s ethics are based on accepting personal responsibility for broader male wrongdoing. At the same time, the portrayal of women is shaped by period conventions, dividing them into idealized, faithful womanhood and destructive sexual deceit. Joe’s casual generalizations about gossiping women likewise preserve the masculine assumptions of his milieu. The style therefore combines humane sympathy with historically specific gender attitudes.

Imagery is sparing but evocative. Bush details—coals, tea-billy, saplings, fences, clearing, surveyor’s camp and gloaming—anchor the narrative in physical life. The most memorable image converts memory into distance: the Oracle looks “a thousand miles across country” and “about twenty years back into the past.” Railway imagery in the “red light on the line” turns a flash in his eyes into a warning signal. Such images are plain, concrete and accessible, fitting the oral register while carrying emotional and symbolic weight.

Overall, the style is that of a dramatic bush yarn whose apparent casualness conceals careful construction. Colloquial speech establishes authenticity and camaraderie; layered hearsay creates the feel of oral history; comic episodes sustain narrative energy; and restrained climactic writing prevents the tragedy from becoming excessively sentimental. The final return to the tea-billy and the approaching Oracle is characteristically understated, allowing an ordinary camp action to absorb the emotional aftermath.

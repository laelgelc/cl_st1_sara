The story uses a spare, understated realist style built from concrete action, plain diction, and emotional omission. The prose records what characters say, do, notice, buy, drink, and carry, while rarely explaining their motives directly. Feelings emerge through behaviour and friction: Peduzzi’s elaborate confidence conceals desperation, the wife’s sullenness suggests a deeper marital quarrel, and the young gentleman’s politeness masks embarrassment, fear, and a wish to escape. This restraint creates an “iceberg” effect, in which the unstated tensions matter more than the ostensible fishing expedition.

The narration is third-person but moves flexibly among limited viewpoints, concentrating chiefly on Peduzzi and the young gentleman. It can enter Peduzzi’s buoyant, alcohol-fed hopes—“Life was opening out”—and then shift to the young gentleman’s private anxiety about gamekeepers or his strained exchanges with his wife. These movements are not formally announced, giving the narrative a fluid, lightly detached quality. The narrator often echoes a character’s idiom, so that description shades into free indirect discourse. Repeated claims that it is “a wonderful day,” for example, carry Peduzzi’s intoxicated optimism while becoming increasingly ironic.

Sentences are generally short or moderately coordinated, with simple declarative structures and frequent use of “and.” This paratactic syntax gives events a sequential, reportorial rhythm: people walk, stop, purchase drinks, argue, assemble rods, and turn back. More complex emotion is compressed into blunt statements such as “None of it makes any difference.” Fragments and elliptical phrases are common in dialogue—“Forty minutes or an hour,” “Nothing. Anything,” “Marsala, maybe”—reproducing uncertainty, intoxication, and imperfect communication. Occasional grammatical roughness, repetition, and unpunctuated speech preserve the immediacy of spoken language.

Dialogue dominates and is minimally framed. Speech tags are simple, and quotation marks are omitted, allowing conversation to blend into narration. The resulting surface can feel abrupt and unstable, especially when the story moves between spoken dialogue, translated speech, and private thought. Repetition performs much of the characterization: Peduzzi continually invokes marsala, legality, local influence, the promise of trout, and the next morning’s plans; the young gentleman repeatedly postpones or withdraws; the wife returns to accusations of cowardice and futility. Their verbal patterns expose incompatible desires without overt authorial judgment.

The diction is concrete and economical, with precise references to money, clothing, fishing equipment, weather, drink, and local institutions. Objects carry social and emotional weight: the old military coat suggests Peduzzi’s diminished status; the new fishing tackle contrasts with his improvised expertise; the missing lead becomes both a practical obstacle and a convenient release; the bottle of marsala briefly turns failure into triumph. Exact denominations—four lira, ten lira, five lire—keep poverty and dependence constantly visible. Bodily and material details can be sharply unsentimental, as in the beggar’s “spittle-thickened beard” or the gray hairs moving in Peduzzi’s neck as he drinks.

The multilingual setting is rendered through Italian, German, dialect, and untranslated local terms such as “grappa,” “marsala,” “municipio,” “piombo,” and “caro.” Language is less a bridge than a source of confusion and social performance. Peduzzi switches dialects in an effort to be understood, yet the couple comprehend almost nothing; the husband offers partial translations, and comic mishearings intensify the wife’s irritation. This linguistic dislocation reinforces the foreigners’ alienation and Peduzzi’s unstable role as guide, beggar, entertainer, and supposed local authority.

Characterization depends on contrast and dramatic irony. Peduzzi imagines himself mysterious, respected, and indispensable, while the townspeople stare, ignore him, or laugh. His grandiose greetings and claims of influence reveal his marginal position more clearly than direct description would. He is comic but also pathetic: every minor success becomes evidence that “life was opening out,” even as the reader sees that his anticipated future is unlikely to materialize. The young couple are comparatively opaque. Their quarrel predates the outing, but its cause remains unstated; the failed expedition merely gives their resentment a temporary form. The affectionate nickname “Tiny” clashes with the coldness of their exchange.

The setting is established through selective sensory detail rather than extended landscape description. Wind, intermittent sunlight, rain, muddy snowmelt, a dump heap, hotel construction, and the distant campanile create a cold, transitional mountain town. Weather repeatedly mirrors shifts in confidence without becoming conventionally symbolic: rain accompanies discomfort and illegality, while the returning sun coincides with the relief of abandoning the fishing attempt. Peduzzi interprets every change optimistically, so the natural setting also participates in the story’s irony.

The pacing is linear and deliberately anti-climactic. Much of the narrative consists of preparation, delay, walking, drinking, and negotiation. The promised trout fishing never begins; the excursion collapses because of a missing piece of lead. This bathos is central to the style. The story builds expectation around an adventure, then replaces action with embarrassment and financial solicitation. Its ending remains open and unresolved: Peduzzi treats the next morning as guaranteed, while the young gentleman repeatedly says he probably will not come. The final effect combines comedy, discomfort, and sadness, leaving Peduzzi suspended in hope while the reader anticipates disappointment.

Overall, the tonal profile is dry, ironic, and compassionate without sentimentality. Humour arises from repetition, mistranslation, social awkwardness, and the discrepancy between Peduzzi’s self-image and his circumstances. Beneath that comedy lie loneliness, class inequality, marital estrangement, failed communication, and the human tendency to sustain hope through small fictions. The style refuses explicit moral commentary, trusting concrete detail, dialogue, and omission to produce the emotional force.

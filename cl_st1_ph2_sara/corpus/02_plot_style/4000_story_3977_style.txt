The story uses a brisk, urbane comic style built on the contrast between romantic aspiration and prosaic reality. Its narrator is confident, intrusive, and knowingly amused, repeatedly stepping outside the immediate action to generalise, comment, or address the reader. Statements such as “What a blessing is poverty, properly considered” begin as apparent wisdom and then unfold into irony. The narrative voice treats Paul’s disappointments with mock gravity, inflating the struggles of a poorly paid waiter into epic, artistic, and mythological ordeals while remaining sympathetic to his hopes.

The point of view is third-person omniscient, centred chiefly on Paul but free to move into the perceptions of minor characters, including the restaurant customer, the policeman, and the American businessman. The narrator alternates between access to Paul’s grandiose inner life and an external view that exposes his absurdity. Free indirect touches allow Paul’s fantasies to colour the prose: his painting is a “masterpiece,” its hypothetical buyer a “wealthy connoisseur,” and his defence of Jeanne an act of heroism. These inflated terms are undercut by material facts such as his eighteen-shilling wage, the rejected canvas, and the use of bread rolls and a cream bun as weapons.

Humour arises primarily through incongruity, hyperbole, simile, bathos, repetition, and anticlimax. Paul’s work is compared at once to a Marathon finish, a juggling act, and a sideshow monologue; Jeanne resembles “a cow in a boiler-factory” yet also a goddess in a Homeric battle; Bredin is elaborately reduced to a prize pig. Lofty references to Chaucer, the Odyssey, Hades, Andromeda, Perseus, and Homer are applied to commercial art, restaurant service, jealousy, and employment trouble. The prose often builds toward a dignified or melodramatic expectation and then punctures it with a blunt practical detail. Even the suicide sequence is converted into farce when Paul indignantly objects to nearly being run over just before deciding to use a motorcar to kill himself.

The diction mixes polished literary English with slang, commercial language, foreign-inflected speech, and comic verbal distortion. Elevated phrases such as “porcine austerity,” “ponderous amiability,” and “outer darkness” coexist with “Poor devil,” “pop off,” “cracker jack,” “bear-cat,” and “you’ve got the goods.” Paul’s French identity is suggested through emphatic phrasing, inverted constructions, repeated honorifics, and extravagant declarations rather than through heavy phonetic transcription. Jeanne’s language is terse, practical, and contemptuous, while the American speaks in energetic advertising slang. The policeman’s clipped colloquialisms establish his pragmatic temperament immediately. Dialogue therefore functions as rapid characterization as well as a source of social and linguistic comedy.

Sentences vary between expansive, carefully balanced descriptions and short comic blows. Long sentences accumulate parallel clauses, escalating comparisons, or catalogues of misfortune before ending in a deflationary phrase. Short sentences—“Paul wilted,” “It shall be condensed,” or “He would kill himself”—create sharp changes of rhythm and often parody melodrama. Parenthetical qualifications and narrator’s asides continually redirect expectations. Repetition is used structurally and verbally: dealers repeatedly have “no room,” Jeanne repeatedly asks whether the picture has sold, and descriptions of Bredin’s pig-like qualities return near the end through the policeman’s perspective.

Characterization is caricatural but precise. Each principal figure is organised around a dominant comic trait: Paul is energetic, sentimental, volatile, and self-dramatising; Jeanne is physically imposing, slow, practical, and responsive to money and status; Bredin is wholly porcine; the American is brisk, benevolent, and commercially inventive. Physical description is rarely neutral. Bodies, movement, and manner become visual jokes, as with Bredin’s body quivering “like a stricken jelly” or Jeanne’s face rising from her apron “like a full moon.” The figures are exaggerated types, yet Paul retains emotional appeal because his vanity, artistic longing, romantic pain, and need for sympathy are recognisably human.

The story is tightly plotted as a comic reversal. It begins with Paul’s double life as waiter and artist, establishes his linked desires for artistic success, financial independence, and Jeanne, then subjects each hope to repeated frustration. Rejection leads to humiliation, unemployment, and attempted suicide; the apparent catastrophe produces the chance meeting that fulfils his financial dream. The crucial twist converts his idealised woodland painting into an advertisement for braces. This is both an absurd desecration of “high” art and a practical recognition of Paul’s marketable talent. The final restaurant scene mirrors the opening but reverses the hierarchy: the former servant returns as a wealthy customer, Bredin becomes powerless, Jeanne turns solicitous, and Paul adopts the cool condescension once directed at him.

Pacing is fast and episodic. Summary compresses repetitive searches and failures, while scenic treatment expands moments of maximum comic potential: the declaration of love, the food fight, the attempted suicide, the advertising proposition, and the triumphant return. Asterisks divide the narrative into distinct comic movements, each advancing Paul from confidence to defeat or from defeat to sudden success. Transitions are often handled by narrator’s commentary, allowing large spans of effort to be skipped without losing momentum.

The setting is rendered through socially specific details rather than atmospheric description. The noisy Soho restaurant, the attic studio with its skylight, London dealers and magazine offices, the dark Park, and the expensive hotel create a movement across class and commercial worlds. Food, wages, banknotes, uniforms, and consumer goods keep the story grounded in material economics. Art is repeatedly assessed not by transcendent standards but by whether it can be sold, reproduced, or used to advertise a product.

Thematically, the story satirises romantic idealism, artistic pretension, social ambition, and the commercialization of art, but it does so lightly rather than bitterly. Success comes not through critical recognition of Paul’s “masterpiece” but through its accidental suitability for advertising. Love is similarly exposed as conditional: Jeanne’s respect grows with the prospect of money and revives once wealth becomes visible. Paul’s final dismissal of her supplies the story’s neat retaliatory sting. The governing style is therefore comic, ironic, highly visual, and rhythmically controlled, combining affectionate mockery with a strong taste for reversal, verbal escalation, and perfectly timed deflation.

The story is written as a religious-moral folktale grounded in the everyday life of a Russian peasant village. Its realism lies in concrete domestic and agricultural detail—livestock, sowing, taxes, tools, clothing, courts, and household labour—while its governing logic is parabolic. Ordinary events are arranged to demonstrate a clear Christian principle: resentment must be extinguished at its first spark, because retaliation magnifies both suffering and guilt. The biblical passage at the opening supplies the interpretive framework in advance, making the narrative an extended practical illustration of forgiveness “from the heart.”

The narrator is third-person, omniscient, authoritative, and largely unobtrusive. The narration moves freely between external action, communal observation, and Iván’s thoughts, but it does not dwell on subtle psychological analysis. Character is revealed chiefly through conduct, speech, repeated choices, and visible reactions. The narrator occasionally generalises beyond the immediate scene—“In a farmstead there is always plenty for the master to do”—or introduces a homely analogy, such as the comparison between the feuding peasants and fighting dogs. These interventions give the voice the quality of an oral storyteller or moral instructor: plain, confident, and concerned with practical wisdom.

The diction is simple, concrete, and accessible. Most of the vocabulary belongs to domestic life, farming, kinship, religion, and local administration. Elevated or abstract language is reserved mainly for the biblical epigraph, legal pronouncements, and the old father’s moral teaching. This produces a deliberate contrast between lived peasant experience and institutional or scriptural authority. Archaic biblical forms such as “thee,” “thou,” and “ye” establish a solemn scriptural register, while the main narrative uses direct, idiomatic English coloured by rural expressions, insults, proverbs, and exclamations. Russian names, social titles, and objects—Iván, Tarás, vódka, the District Court, the village Elder—preserve the cultural setting without making the prose ornate.

The syntax is generally straightforward and cumulative. Sentences often link actions with repeated conjunctions, especially “and,” reproducing the forward movement and additive rhythm of oral narration. This paratactic style is particularly effective in quarrels, work sequences, legal escalation, and the spread of the fire, where one action rapidly produces another. Longer sentences are used to inventory possessions, losses, accusations, or stages in a process; shorter sentences mark transitions and moral emphasis. Lines such as “It all began about a trifle” and “That was how it all began” are stark, memorable pivots. Repetition is central to the prose: grievances are answered by counter-grievances, lawsuits by further lawsuits, and warnings by renewed warnings. The style thereby enacts the self-perpetuating nature of resentment.

Dialogue occupies a large proportion of the story and is strongly functional. Speakers express themselves in blunt, emotionally transparent language rather than nuanced or highly individualised idioms. The women’s quarrel is rendered through fragments, overlapping accusations, insults, and rapid escalation; the legal scenes juxtapose rigid bureaucratic language with appeals to conscience; the old father speaks in patient questions, practical examples, imperatives, and proverbial formulations. His teaching does not remain abstract: he calculates the economic cost of hatred, points to neglected sowing, and recommends a specific act of reconciliation involving a visit, tea, and vódka. This fusion of spiritual counsel with material common sense is characteristic of the story’s didactic method.

The characterisation is archetypal but not entirely flat. Iván is initially prosperous, strong, proud, and susceptible to wounded honour. Gabriel functions as both antagonist and mirror: each man interprets punishment as justification for further revenge. The women and children show how hostility spreads through imitation and household culture. The old father is the moral centre, physically weak but spiritually clear-sighted, and the old Judge briefly echoes his belief that divine law stands above procedural law. Iván’s conversion is prepared through moments of pity and hesitation, though these are repeatedly overcome by suspicion and social pressure. His final repentance therefore feels like the culmination of an internal struggle rather than an entirely abrupt change.

The structure is linear, symmetrical, and carefully escalatory. It begins with an image of material sufficiency and neighbourly cooperation, introduces a trivial cause—the disputed egg—and then traces a chain from words to fighting, from fighting to litigation, from litigation to humiliation, and from humiliation to arson. The feud lasts seven years, a duration that recalls the epigraph’s symbolic language of repeated forgiveness while showing its opposite: repeated retaliation. After the catastrophic fire, the pattern reverses. Iván accepts responsibility, conceals Gabriel’s guilt, abandons revenge, and restores the former neighbourly order. The ending returns to the opening ideal of families living “as neighbours should,” giving the tale a circular form in which harmony is lost and consciously regained.

The pacing alternates between broad summary and sharply dramatised scenes. Years of lawsuits are compressed into a few paragraphs, while decisive moments—the first quarrel, the sentencing, the father’s counsel, Iván’s nocturnal watch, the ignition of the thatch, and the deathbed confession—are narrated in detail. The fire scene slows perception through darkness, uncertain movement, brief flares of light, stealthy footsteps, pursuit, impact, and stunned repetition. Sensory imagery becomes more intense here: wind in the thatch, flames reaching the roof, smoke and burning straw, roaring and crackling, and sparks before Iván’s eyes. This heightened physicality turns the story’s governing metaphor into literal disaster.

Fire is the dominant symbol and organising metaphor. Anger begins as a negligible spark that could easily be stamped out, but pride allows it to spread until it destroys both households and much of the village. The father’s warning—“Put out the fire before it spreads”—operates simultaneously as practical foresight, moral instruction, and narrative prophecy. Iván’s anguished refrain after the catastrophe, “One need only have pulled it out and trampled on it,” refers both to the burning straw and to the original grievance. Other recurring motifs include debt, reckoning, punishment, confession, and mercy, all of which connect the village story to the biblical parable.

The tone shifts from calm and explanatory to comic-grotesque, increasingly bitter, suspenseful, tragic, and finally redemptive. There is restrained humour in the pettiness of the egg dispute, the torrent of mutual insults, and the observation that the women’s tongues do more work than their arms at the riverside. Yet the humour serves a serious purpose by exposing the absurd disproportion between cause and consequence. The narration avoids sentimentality even at the father’s death: emotion is conveyed through physical difficulty, pauses, gestures, and sparse speech. The final tone is serene and affirmative, presenting forgiveness not merely as saintly self-denial but as the most practical foundation for communal and material well-being.

Overall, the style is plain, repetitive, concrete, and explicitly didactic. It relies on narrative clarity, dramatic dialogue, folk analogies, biblical parallels, symbolic action, and causal escalation rather than lyrical description or psychological complexity. Its central stylistic strength is the conversion of a moral maxim into a vividly observable sequence: a single egg leads to an insult, the insult to revenge, revenge to institutional punishment, and punishment to a consuming fire, while one act of mercy reverses the entire pattern.

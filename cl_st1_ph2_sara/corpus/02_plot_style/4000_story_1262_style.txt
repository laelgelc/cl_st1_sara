The story uses a controlled late-Victorian/Edwardian realist style, combining social observation, psychological portraiture, and bleak tragicomedy. Its omniscient third-person narrator moves freely between outward description, biographical summary, authorial judgment, and close access to Mr. Farmiloe’s agitated thoughts. The narrative voice is polished, confident, and faintly intrusive, often commenting on events with urbane irony: apparently neutral statements are shaped so that the reader sees both Farmiloe’s genuine suffering and the absurdity with which he repeatedly worsens it. Phrases such as “the good man,” “the luckless apothecary,” and “a victim of destiny” sound sympathetic while carrying a satirical edge.

The opening establishes the setting through an extended, sensuous panorama of a prosperous western suburb. Its “pretty, clean, fresh-coloured” shops, evergreen gardens, cathedral music, cheerful delivery boys, and “flowery and bowery” appearance form an idealised social surface. Brightness, order, freshness, and ease dominate the imagery. Against this environment, Farmiloe’s sour face, awkward voice, shabby domestic arrangements, and emotional volatility appear especially incongruous. The suburb is not merely a backdrop but an active social organism governed by appearances, manners, reputation, and gossip. Its prettiness conceals a punitive conformism: anyone unable to bow, smile, speak suavely, and satisfy middle-class expectations is rapidly excluded.

The prose is formal and lexically rich, with period-specific commercial and bureaucratic vocabulary such as “pharmaceutist,” “postal authorities,” “direct remuneration,” “book-post,” “postal-order,” and “dispense.” Long, balanced sentences frequently accumulate descriptive or explanatory clauses, giving the narrator room to qualify judgments and expose contradictions. Lists are a recurrent device. Catalogues of suburban tradespeople, postal requests, Farmiloe’s grievances, and stages of emotional escalation create both social density and comic pressure. Short sentences and fragments then puncture this expansiveness at moments of frustration: “The post-office, always the post-office.” Repetition turns the institution into an obsessive refrain and gives verbal form to Farmiloe’s entrapment.

Irony governs the story’s structure. The post office is purchased as an advantage but becomes the principal source of torment; public service promises dignity but brings humiliation; an inheritance improves Farmiloe’s position but leaves him embittered; a prestigious prescription seems to offer rescue but precipitates his ruin. Even his professional qualification—proudly advertised as “Chemist by Examination”—is both technically valid and practically hollow. The narrator repeatedly contrasts expectation with result, allowing hopes to rise only far enough for their defeat to become comic and painful. The ending extends this method into grim understatement: Farmiloe has “just enough money left to bury him.” Its restraint makes the conclusion more brutal than overt lament would have done.

Characterisation is built through the interaction of temperament and social circumstance. Farmiloe is neither a simple villain nor an innocent martyr. He is incompetent under pressure, resentful, tactless, self-pitying, and prone to displaced aggression, yet he is also shy, lonely, financially frightened, and painfully aware of his own deficiencies. His sourness is shown as both innate and self-reinforcing. Every humiliation increases the irritability that causes the next humiliation, producing a psychologically credible downward spiral. The narrator sometimes generalises about him in aphoristic terms—his face and demeanour are “against him,” and fate seems unable to help him—but concrete episodes demonstrate the pattern rather than leaving it as abstract diagnosis.

Free indirect discourse and close focalisation intensify this portrait. Farmiloe’s vocabulary and emotional logic seep into the narration, especially in references to “the blessed tendering of a prescription,” “that pestilent Dr. Bunker,” and the “never-sufficiently-to-be-condemned handwriting” that obstructs him. Exclamations, rhetorical questions, dashes, and abrupt shifts reproduce his nervous agitation without requiring continuous interior monologue. His mind interprets ordinary interruptions as persecution, so mundane commercial life acquires the emotional scale of catastrophe. The result is comic disproportion, but the persistent references to debt, bankruptcy, suicide, and bodily collapse ensure that the comedy remains uneasy.

Dialogue is concise, socially revealing, and often organised as confrontation. Farmiloe’s exchanges expose his inability to manage tone: he speaks defensively, literally, or aggressively when politeness demands tact. Other characters tend to use clipped formulas of authority and respectability, as in the postal official’s refusal to consider his circumstances. Repetitions such as “‘I’m not.’ ‘You are.’” reduce adult disagreement to schoolboy quarrelling and underline Farmiloe’s regression under stress. Speech is also stratified by class and role: ladies issue peremptory demands, officials speak bureaucratically, tradesmen defend their dignity, and servants become agents through whom damaging stories circulate.

The pacing is cumulative and episodic. Much of Farmiloe’s history is compressed into retrospective summary, after which a sequence of increasingly specific scenes dramatises his decline. Each incident follows a similar rhythm: hope or irritation arises, an interaction tests his self-command, he responds badly, and the social consequences spread beyond the immediate encounter. Gossip functions almost like a chorus, converting private mishaps into public identity. This repetition creates inevitability while the stakes steadily rise from lost customers and complaints to professional disgrace, financial ruin, exile, illness, and death. The climactic prescription episode is narrated in greater detail and at greater speed, with rushing verbs—“seized,” “rushed,” “tore,” “sped,” “wrenched”—that mirror panic and contrast with the measured exposition preceding it.

The humour is predominantly satirical and tragi-comic rather than farcical. It arises from incongruity, bathos, hyperbole, and the gap between Farmiloe’s grand emotional responses and the trivial demands placed on him. Requests about postage become existential assaults; closing the post office for the evening feels like liberation; a minor doubt about a medicine triggers a frantic public scene. Yet the narration does not wholly side with the suburb against him. Its smiling respectability, appetite for scandal, and readiness to invent alcoholism or professional danger are also targets of criticism. The social world is courteous in form but ruthless in effect.

Thematically, the style emphasises maladaptation, economic insecurity, masculine loneliness, reputational fragility, and the cruelty of ordinary institutions. Farmiloe’s tragedy comes less from a single moral failing than from a disastrous mismatch between personality and occupation. Commerce requires charm, pharmacy requires calm precision, and postal service requires patience; he possesses none of these under pressure. The story’s fatalism is therefore social and psychological rather than supernatural. “Destiny” consists of temperament, bad decisions, chance interruptions, financial constraints, and communal judgment tightening around one another until escape becomes impossible.

Overall, the style is lucid, observant, ironic, and steadily darkening. Decorative suburban description gives way to claustrophobic scenes of counters, bells, bottles, account-books, and official demands. The polished narrative manner keeps emotional excess at a distance, while close psychological detail preserves compassion for the protagonist. That balance between satire and pity is the story’s defining stylistic feature: Farmiloe is ridiculous, culpable, and doomed, but never merely a joke.

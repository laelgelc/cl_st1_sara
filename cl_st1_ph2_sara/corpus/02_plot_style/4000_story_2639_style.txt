The story uses a richly ornate mode of cosmic fantasy and antiquarian horror, blending New England local colour with mythic, dreamlike imagery. Its dominant atmosphere is one of enchanted unease rather than direct terror: the sea, mist, cliffs and sky are beautiful, but their beauty suggests inaccessible realms, prehuman powers and dangers beyond ordinary perception. Wonder and dread remain inseparable throughout, so that the supernatural can appear inviting, liberating and spiritually destructive at the same time.

The narrator is omniscient, impersonal and steeped in communal folklore. Much of the tale is filtered through what “people say,” what sailors fear, what old residents remember and what rumour has preserved. This gives the narrative the quality of a legend accumulated across generations rather than a private eyewitness account. The voice speaks with considerable authority while repeatedly relying on hearsay, conjecture and uncertain recollection. Phrases such as “it must be guessed,” “perhaps,” and “rumor said” preserve ambiguity and imply that the full truth lies beyond human knowledge. The narrator also shifts easily between panoramic description, Olney’s immediate perceptions and the collective consciousness of Kingsport.

The diction is elevated, archaic and deliberately elaborate. Words such as “betwixt,” “aether,” “olden,” “centuried,” “unhallowed,” “primeval” and “antediluvian” create historical and mythical distance. Adjectives are abundant and often cumulative, especially those expressing age, strangeness, height or obscurity: “gray,” “ancient,” “queer,” “cryptical,” “singular,” “furtive,” “illimitable” and “inconceivable.” Maritime, architectural, astronomical and mythological vocabularies overlap, making the setting feel simultaneously tangible and otherworldly. Proper names—Kingsport, Arkham, Miskatonic, Ulthar, Hatheg-Kia and Kadath—lend geographical solidity to invented places while expanding the implied mythology far beyond the immediate plot.

Sentences are frequently long, winding and clause-heavy, with semicolons, repetitions and coordinated phrases producing a ceremonial cadence. Descriptions often proceed by accumulation, moving from concrete landscape features into increasingly fantastic associations. Shorter sentences appear at moments of discovery or danger—when a sound is heard, a door opens or a visitor arrives—but the prevailing rhythm is incantatory rather than urgent. Periodic constructions delay the main point and allow suspense to gather gradually. Biblical or chronicle-like inversions, including formulations such as “Alone it is,” intensify the archaic register.

The imagery is strongly atmospheric and synesthetic. Mist functions as the central symbol and connective medium, carrying dreams, memories, music and ancient lore between sea, earth and sky. Bells, conchs, horns, gongs, knocking, laughter and distant voices create an acoustic landscape in which unseen worlds announce themselves through sound. Visual motifs centre on whiteness, gray rock, dim yellow windows, green aqueous light, black silhouettes and the cold colours of the aurora. Vertical imagery is especially important: cliffs, abysses, celestial heights and plunging drops repeatedly place characters on thresholds between the human world and the cosmic unknown.

The setting is personified and mythologised. The mist has “brothers,” the crag “consort[s] with celestial things,” shadows become furtive or bold, and the coast appears to possess memory. Kingsport itself behaves almost like a collective character: old, superstitious, picturesque and instinctively defensive toward the supernatural. Architectural details—gambrel roofs, crooked alleys, worm-eaten shingles, bull’s-eye panes and Tudor furnishings—anchor the fantasy in a decaying colonial past. This antiquarian realism makes the impossible house and its visitors feel like concealed extensions of local history.

Characterisation is selective and symbolic rather than psychologically detailed. Thomas Olney is defined chiefly by the conflict between disciplined respectability and repressed longing. His “stout wife,” romping children and ponderous academic work embody conventional domestic life, while the cliff and cottage embody imaginative escape. After his supernatural experience, he returns outwardly functional but inwardly diminished; the prose treats correct citizenship, usefulness and modern suburban order with quiet irony. The nameless hermit serves as a threshold guardian and storyteller, while the Terrible Old Man and other elderly residents act as custodians of communal memory. Direct dialogue is almost entirely absent, which keeps the characters remote and sustains the feeling of legend.

The plot follows a gradual threshold structure. It begins with broad, lyrical evocation, narrows toward the forbidden cottage, follows Olney’s ascent into an isolated supernatural encounter, and then widens again to show the consequences for his family and the whole town. Suspense comes less from action than from spatial impossibility, withheld explanation and ritualised sounds at the door. The visit itself becomes increasingly visionary, culminating in a procession of sea gods and ancient beings. The story then refuses to explain exactly what happened, focusing instead on the apparent exchange or loss of Olney’s soul.

Repetition gives the story cohesion and a circular, incantatory form. The opening images of white mist, sea caves, tritons, strange lore and tolling buoys return almost verbatim at the close. Recurrent descriptions of the cottage as gray, ancient, low-eaved and joined to the firmament turn it into an emblem rather than merely a location. Repeated references to the “rim of all earth” reinforce the idea that the cliff is a boundary of ordinary reality. This circular ending suggests that the supernatural cycle continues and that Olney’s experience is only one episode in a much older process.

The tone combines reverence, melancholy, irony and apprehension. The supernatural realm is depicted with ecstatic beauty, yet contact with it may drain a person’s worldly desire and volition. Conversely, respectable normality appears safe but spiritually barren. The humour is dry and understated, especially in the contrast between cosmic events and Olney’s family worrying about umbrellas, drains and proper behaviour. This tonal counterpoint sharpens the story’s central tension between imaginative transcendence and mundane stability.

Overall, the style is lyrical, baroque, mythopoeic and deliberately archaizing. It favours atmosphere over explanation, suggestion over explicit revelation, and cosmic scale over intimate realism. Its characteristic effect comes from placing a carefully detailed old coastal town beside an immeasurable dream-world, then allowing mist, music and rumour to blur the boundary between them.

The story adopts the manner of a comic travel memoir that gradually turns into a satirical case study. Its first-person narrator presents himself as an experienced, sociable traveller, moving easily from geographical description and personal reminiscence to anecdote, dialogue and argumentative reflection. He is observant but deliberately digressive: the opening discussion of a Bohemian health resort wanders into an enthusiastic account of a hidden Viennese beer cellar before returning, with the self-conscious remark that the episode is “all incidental,” to the ostensible subject. Such detours create an informal, conversational impression while also establishing the narrator’s comic personality.

The prevailing tone is dry, genial and irreverent. Serious institutions—medicine, health tourism, dietary doctrine and respectable social custom—are treated with amused suspicion. The humour often depends on stating absurdities with complete confidence. Austria is described as an empire made up of health resorts that “distributes health to the whole world,” while its supposedly medicinal waters are exported because the natives prefer beer. The appetite clinic’s grotesque menu is likewise administered with professional solemnity, allowing the contrast between the doctor’s composure and the patient’s horror to generate comedy. Hyperbole is central: trains are phenomenally slow, a biscuit could be fired to a nearby resort with a twelve-inch gun, and disagreeable food is described as fit to “gag a cannibal.” These exaggerations remain anchored in recognisable frustrations, so the absurdity sharpens rather than replaces the social satire.

The diction combines cultivated, occasionally old-fashioned prose with colloquial American phrasing. Elevated words such as “outlanders,” “domicile,” “repose,” “paucity” and “surreptitious” sit beside idioms such as “hand-spring,” “buzzard-fare,” “right along,” “set them up” and “I can’t spare my mouth.” This mixture allows the narrator to sound both literate and plain-speaking. Mock-technical language is especially important. Terms including “Appetite Anstallt,” “robust treatment,” “the corrective” and “Nature’s Appetite-Cure” imitate the authority of medical discourse, while grotesque images expose the simplicity concealed beneath it. Concrete food vocabulary recurs obsessively, ranging from Vienna bread and coffee to raw ham, sailor-boots, butterflies and filtered sewage. Accumulated lists of dishes give the prose sensory force and turn appetite itself into a structural motif.

The syntax is highly varied. Descriptive and expository passages favour long, cumulative sentences joined by semicolons, dashes and repeated clauses. These sentences mimic spontaneous speech, permitting qualifications, jokes and side comments to interrupt the main line of thought. Short sentences then provide comic emphasis: “It was refused me,” “It is all so handy,” or “They were human beings.” Dashes frequently control timing, postponing a conclusion or introducing a sharper afterthought. Colons are used for catalogues and mock demonstrations, while repetition supplies rhetorical momentum. The doctor’s insistence that the cure “never failed—never” and his repeated commands not to eat create both persuasive force and farce.

Dialogue dominates much of the narrative and is constructed as brisk verbal sparring. The narrator plays the sceptical, indignant patient, while Professor Haimberger answers with unwavering literalness. Their exchanges often proceed through short questions, reversals and delayed explanations. A proposition that initially sounds insane is slowly revealed as practical, whereas respectable conventional wisdom comes to appear foolish. The doctor’s voice is authoritative, blunt and paradoxical; he uses categorical statements, analogies and provocative generalisations about human stupidity. The narrator’s voice is more reactive and figurative, full of mock outrage, but he is willing to concede defeat when experience proves the system effective. This interplay keeps an extended argument lively and prevents the didactic material from becoming a conventional lecture.

Structurally, the story has two distinct movements. The first is an experiential comic narrative: arrival at the clinic, confrontation with the menu, confinement, mounting hunger and eventual surrender. Its tension is produced by deprivation and escalation. As the hours without food accumulate, the patient’s standards steadily collapse until the once-repellent dishes become desirable. The climax arrives with the deliberately nonsensical “soft-boiled spring chicken—in the egg,” followed by an enormous ordinary meal. The second movement explains the theory behind the cure through the doctor’s shipwreck story and a long Socratic conversation. This section is more discursive, but it retains narrative energy through miniature examples, grotesque catalogues and argumentative reversals. The final assertion that the narrator has personally tested the method gives the piece the form of humorous testimony.

The pacing alternates between leisurely digression and rapid escalation. Scenic descriptions of Vienna and the mountain resort are spacious and appreciative, with visual phrases such as “green glades and valleys” and “tumbled hills clothed with forests.” By contrast, the period of confinement is compressed through counted hours and repeated attempts to order food. The second half slows to examine the cure’s principles, though comic images—patients eating eggs, butterflies and rose leaves or walking off their medicinal water—continually refresh the exposition. Enumeration is a major pacing device: lists initially overwhelm the reader, then culminate in a succinct punch line or blunt conclusion.

The narrator’s reliability is playfully unstable. He reports places, meals and experiences with documentary specificity, yet embellishes them so extravagantly that factual truth becomes secondary to comic truth. He is also the target of the satire as well as its vehicle. His attachment to routine, refined food and inherited assumptions makes him one of the foolish patients he describes. The eventual conversion does not erase his vanity; instead, it permits him to join the doctor in judging humanity. This self-implicating quality softens the satire and preserves the narrator’s amiability.

Thematically, the story attacks unexamined habit, fashionable medicine and dependence on authority. Its central paradox is that deprivation restores pleasure: hunger, rather than delicacy or elaborate treatment, makes food nourishing and desirable. The appetite cure also serves as a broader allegory of common sense obscured by convention. Human beings ignore direct bodily evidence, submit to schedules and superstitions, and then pay experts to disguise simple remedies as complicated regimens. Yet the satire is not entirely anti-medical. Professor Haimberger succeeds because he understands psychology, manipulates expectation and forces patients to discover a truth through experience. The resulting style blends travel writing, tall tale, mock-scientific discourse, social criticism and practical sermon, all unified by deadpan delivery, vivid exaggeration and a strong instinct for the comic reversal.

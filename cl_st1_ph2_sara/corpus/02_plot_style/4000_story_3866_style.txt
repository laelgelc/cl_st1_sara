The story adopts a first-person retrospective viewpoint, filtering events through a confident, rational young narrator whose scepticism gradually collapses into panic. His voice begins controlled, educated and faintly sardonic, with formal constructions such as “said I” and “scarce expected,” but becomes increasingly fragmented and exclamatory as fear takes hold. This contrast between composed narration and emotional disintegration is central to the style: the reader watches not only a supernatural-seeming event, but the failure of a supposedly reliable, modern mind to master its own imagination.

The prose belongs strongly to the Gothic tradition. Its setting is an old, isolated castle filled with subterranean passages, spiral stairs, moonlit corridors, dark panelling, antique furniture, mirrors, alcoves and decaying custodians. The atmosphere is built through coldness, silence, echo, shadow and firelight. Colour is used selectively but forcefully, especially the sombre reds and blacks of the haunted room, the pallor of age, the silver of moonlight and the final engulfing darkness. The house is presented as a residue of the past, populated by objects and fashions that seem less preserved than ghostly. This makes history itself feel oppressive and gives the physical setting psychological force.

The diction is formal, literary and period-inflected. Archaic or elevated phrases—“eight-and-twenty,” “to-night,” “beyond denying,” “her ladyship,” “whither”—help establish social and historical distance. Descriptions are precise but often distorted by grotesque imagery. The old custodians have withered limbs, inflamed eyes, decaying teeth and “bent carriage,” while their shadows crouch and mock them. Senility is described in unsettling, almost evolutionary terms as “atavistic” and “inhuman.” Such language prevents the human figures from offering comfort; before the narrator reaches the haunted room, the story has already made ordinary bodies and objects seem uncanny.

Sentence structure shifts with the emotional arc. Early sentences are measured, balanced and discursive, often containing reflective qualifications and rational explanations. Longer sentences trace the narrator’s observations through rooms and passages, accumulating architectural and sensory detail. During the candle-extinguishing sequence, the syntax becomes faster and more kinetic. Clauses pile up around repeated actions—candles vanish, matches fail, shadows advance—and short bursts of direct speech interrupt the narration: “Odd!”, “This won’t do!”, “What’s up?” The climax uses breathless coordination, repetition and lists of collisions to imitate frantic movement and the loss of orientation. The prose therefore enacts fear rhythmically rather than merely describing it.

Dialogue is stylised, repetitive and ominous. The custodians speak in short warnings and ritual-like refrains, especially “It’s your own choosing” and “This night of all nights!” Their repetitions create a fatalistic chorus and foreshadow the narrator’s ordeal without explaining it. By contrast, the narrator’s early speech is brisk, logical and dismissive. Later, his jokes about ghosts and candles become “half-hysterical facetiousness,” revealing humour as a failing defence mechanism. After the ordeal, the dialogue becomes interpretive and almost allegorical, culminating in the identification of the haunting as Fear itself.

The story builds suspense through gradual escalation and controlled misdirection. Initial threats repeatedly receive rational explanations: a crouching figure is a bronze statue, movement belongs to a porcelain ornament, and shadows are produced by the candle. These false alarms temporarily restore the narrator’s confidence while training the reader to scrutinise every visual detail. The central scene then reverses that pattern. The extinguished lights cannot be comfortably explained, and each new loss of illumination narrows the narrator’s physical and psychological space. The repeated cycle of extinction and relighting gives the sequence an almost mechanical relentlessness, turning darkness into an advancing antagonist.

Light and darkness provide the dominant symbolic system. Light represents reason, order, visibility and self-command; the narrator systematically lights candles, inspects locks and arranges the furniture as though rational procedure can conquer uncertainty. Darkness is personified as something that springs, creeps, advances, embraces, seals and crushes. It behaves like a living force without ever becoming a visible ghost. Particularly characteristic are similes that transform abstract terror into physical action: darkness closes “like the shutting of an eye,” surrounds him in a “stifling embrace,” and extinguished lights resemble stars swept away by a storm cloud. Fear is thus externalised while remaining psychologically plausible.

Characterisation is economical and largely symbolic. The young narrator embodies modern confidence, empirical inquiry and masculine bravado. The three elderly custodians represent decay, inherited knowledge and submission to the castle’s legends. They are differentiated by recurring physical markers—the withered arm, the eye shade, the woman’s pale stare—rather than by names, which gives them the quality of grotesque archetypes. Their initial hostility changes to pity after the narrator’s collapse, subtly confirming that his experience has initiated him into their world of fear.

Structurally, the narrative follows a clear descent from scepticism to confrontation, collapse and retrospective interpretation. The opening establishes the challenge; the journey through the castle forms a threshold passage; the locked red room becomes a confined arena; the extinguishing candles create the action climax; and the daylight conversation supplies the thematic resolution. The final explanation preserves ambiguity. The narrator denies the presence of a conventional ghost, yet describes Fear as an autonomous “power of darkness” that followed and fought him. The style therefore balances psychological horror with supernatural suggestion, refusing to settle entirely on either explanation.

Overall, the prose is atmospheric, visual, controlled and increasingly visceral. Its most distinctive technique is to convert an intangible emotion into a seemingly material presence through personification, patterned imagery and accelerating syntax. The horror arises less from revelation than from deprivation: the progressive loss of light, sound, spatial certainty and reason. The final effect is philosophical as well as Gothic, presenting fear itself—not any identifiable apparition—as the most powerful and universal haunting.

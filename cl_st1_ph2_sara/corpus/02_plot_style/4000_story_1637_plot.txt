Major Pendleton Talbot and his practical daughter Lydia move from Mobile to a Washington boarding house while he seeks a publisher for his nostalgic book about the antebellum South. There, vaudeville actor Henry Hopkins Hargraves befriends the major, listens closely to his stories, manners, and speech, and learns even his elaborate method of making mint juleps.

The Talbots soon run out of money, and the major’s manuscript remains unpublished. Using his last two dollars, he buys tickets to a new play, only to discover Hargraves performing a Southern colonel modeled unmistakably on him. Hargraves reproduces his appearance, gestures, stories, and julep-making to great acclaim. Feeling personally betrayed and ridiculed, the major rejects Hargraves’s apology and angrily refuses his offer of financial help.

Later, an elderly Black man named Uncle Mose visits the Talbots, claiming to be a former worker on their plantation who prospered after the war. He gives the major three hundred dollars, supposedly repayment for two mule colts given to him long ago. The grateful Talbots accept the money, believing it a touching sign of old loyalty, and their financial troubles ease.

A letter later reveals to Lydia that “Uncle Mose” was actually Hargraves in disguise. Unable to help the proud major openly, the actor had used his talent and knowledge of the major’s past to give the family three hundred dollars without wounding his pride. Lydia conceals the truth from her father.

The story uses a genial late-Victorian comic style built on first-person retrospective narration, social observation, and sustained irony. The narrator presents himself as a sensible, mildly naïve witness whose literal interpretations expose the absurdity of accepted behaviour. His voice is conversational and urbane, combining apparent sympathy with quiet mockery. Rather than openly condemning the central figure, he reports incongruities with deadpan restraint, allowing the reader to perceive the gap between the man’s genuine desires and the emotions or duties he believes he must display.

The humour depends chiefly on contrast. Public appearances are repeatedly contradicted by private realities: lovesick fasting conceals a midnight raid on the pantry; paternal anxiety becomes a performance staged with a hat, a cold chop, and an inconvenient seat in the passage; religious observance is dictated by the cook; wealth brings compulsory hunting, yachting, politics, and culture rather than freedom. Each episode establishes a socially approved pose and then punctures it with a concrete, bodily fact such as hunger, nausea, exhaustion, fear, or boredom. Understatement strengthens the effect, as injuries, seasickness, and social misery are described in calm, almost practical language.

The structure is chronological and episodic, following the protagonist from schooldays through courtship, marriage, fatherhood, country life, wealth, politics, and fashionable artistic society. Time is compressed through brisk transitions, while selected scenes are expanded into dialogue-driven comic set pieces. The episodes accumulate around the same governing trait: the protagonist’s inability to resist convention or risk others’ disapproval. This repetition gives the story the shape of an escalating character study, with each rise in status producing a more elaborate form of self-denial. The final question, “what would people say?”, acts as both punchline and summary.

Characterisation is economical and behavioural. The central figure is defined through gestures, clothing, food, bodily discomfort, and habitual phrases rather than psychological exposition. Secondary characters are rendered as social types through a few sharp details: the gleeful maiden aunt, the approving nurse, the strict Scotch cook, the careless shooting party, and the fashionable cultural clique. Descriptions often verge on caricature, as in the “weak-eyed female cousin,” the “thick-lipped undergraduate,” and the grotesquely fashionable paintings of green cows, pink moonlight, and elongated corpses.

Dialogue is frequent, concise, and central to the comic timing. Exchanges proceed through literal questions, evasive answers, repetitions, and delayed revelations. The narrator often asks reasonable questions that other characters regard as foolish because he fails—or pretends to fail—to accept the conventions they take for granted. Colloquial expressions such as “young ’un,” “punch your head,” and “what would people say?” sit beside more formal narrative diction, creating a flexible register that moves between everyday speech and mock-serious commentary.

The syntax is generally clear and flowing, but often extended by parenthetical qualifications, balanced clauses, and carefully postponed conclusions. Dashes and semicolons control comic pauses, while long sentences gather circumstantial detail until the central absurdity becomes unmistakable. Lists are used for escalation, especially in descriptions of social obligations and cultural entertainments. The prose also employs mock-heroic phrasing, treating trivial discomforts as grave trials and social conformity as an inexorable fate.

The vocabulary mixes polished, slightly formal narration with concrete domestic and social detail. Period-specific references—to fifth form, county families, Parliament, private views, At Homes, hunting, fashionable theatres, and literary movements—anchor the satire in upper-middle-class and aristocratic society. Cultural allusions function less as displays of learning than as comic evidence of compulsory taste. Overall, the style is light, dry, observant, and cumulative: an affectionate but pointed satire on conformity, performative emotion, social ambition, and the surrender of personal pleasure to the imagined judgment of others.

The story uses a didactic, nineteenth-century historical-biographical style, presenting episodes from Christian history as moral examples rather than as psychologically complex fiction. Its governing comparison is with Antigone: Beatrix and, more fully, Margaret Roper are cast as “Christian Antigones” whose devotion to the dead is strengthened by Christian faith. Historical narration is therefore shaped by an explicit ethical purpose, praising courage, filial duty, conscience, charity, religious constancy, and self-sacrifice.

The narrator is omniscient, authoritative, and openly evaluative. Rather than remaining neutral, the voice continually interprets people and events through moral judgments such as “noble,” “true-hearted,” “faithful,” “cruel-hearted,” and “plain, dull.” It also addresses the reader’s possible objections, most notably in the statement that, whether or not Sir Thomas More’s views are shared, his willingness to risk danger by following his conscience must be respected. This produces a guiding, almost instructional relationship between narrator and reader. Historical uncertainty is acknowledged through phrases such as “It is said,” “One story says,” and “It is far more likely,” but the narrator still selects the version that seems most plausible and morally coherent.

The diction is elevated but accessible, combining formal historical vocabulary with affectionate domestic language. Words and phrases such as “tribunal,” “pursuivant,” “oath of supremacy,” “mortal remains,” “dutifulness,” and “transgression” create an antiquarian and ecclesiastical register. At the same time, descriptions of the More household use warm terms such as “happy,” “joyous,” “kind,” “bright,” “tender,” and “loving.” The prose contains period-flavoured expressions—“in good soothe,” “wouldest thou,” “ere,” “pitieth me,” and “tilly vally”—especially in quoted speech, helping evoke Tudor England without making the main narration difficult to follow.

Sentences are frequently long, cumulative, and coordinated, with clauses connected by commas, semicolons, dashes, and conjunctions. These sentences often begin with background information and move gradually toward a moral or emotional conclusion. The syntax is characteristic of expansive historical storytelling: explanatory qualifications, causal links, and interpretive comments are embedded within the narrative. Shorter sentences are reserved for emphasis, especially at moments of danger or transition, as in the movement from domestic happiness to political persecution. Parallel and antithetical constructions are common, particularly contrasts between earthly safety and spiritual integrity, affection and conscience, royal favour and moral independence, or death and heavenly hope.

The structure proceeds chronologically but is framed by thematic analogy. A brief account of Beatrix establishes the pattern of a woman protecting or recovering the bodies of persecuted loved ones. The story then shifts “nearer home” to Margaret Roper and greatly expands the same motif. The Margaret section begins with the happiness of the More household, introduces the political and religious crisis, traces Sir Thomas More’s refusal to compromise, narrates his imprisonment and execution, and culminates in Margaret’s recovery and preservation of his head. This progression moves from tranquillity to threat, separation, martyrdom, and commemorative devotion. Antigone functions as the classical template, while Christian belief supplies what the narrator regards as a fuller spiritual meaning.

Characterisation is exemplary and strongly polarized. Sir Thomas More is defined by conscience, tenderness, wit, learning, piety, and emotional self-command. Margaret is characterised primarily through filial love, courage, and spiritual sympathy with her father. Henry VIII is depicted as dangerous, wilful, and susceptible to offence, while Lady More is treated more comically and dismissively as incapable of understanding refined matters of conscience, though her loyalty is eventually acknowledged. Characters are built less through subtle interior analysis than through representative actions, anecdotes, judgments, and memorable sayings.

Dialogue is used sparingly but effectively. Most of it consists of historically attributed remarks that crystallize character: More’s jokes reveal courage and composure, his exchanges with Margaret show intimacy and shared understanding, and Lady More’s “Tilly vally” marks her practical incomprehension. The dialogue retains archaic forms and is often followed by direct explanation of its meaning, ensuring that the moral significance remains clear. Quotations also break up long stretches of exposition and make the historical figures feel vivid and immediate.

The pacing is selective rather than scene-by-scene. Years of background can be summarized rapidly, while emotionally or morally decisive moments—the river journey after More’s interview with the King, the summons from Chelsea, Margaret’s farewell on the Tower wharf, and the recovery of the severed head—are narrated in greater detail. Anecdotes provide rhythmic pauses and tonal variation. More’s humour on the scaffold, for example, tempers the solemnity without weakening it; instead, wit becomes evidence of spiritual steadiness.

The tone combines reverence, pathos, domestic warmth, moral seriousness, and occasional gentle humour. Sentiment is direct and unapologetic, especially in scenes of family separation and mourning. Physical violence is mentioned plainly but not described graphically; the emotional and religious significance of suffering matters more than bodily detail. The ending closes the narrative in a commemorative mode, transforming the recovered head into a relic of filial love and making Margaret’s burial with it the final emblem of devotion.

Overall, the style is best described as morally instructive historical narrative with hagiographic qualities. It blends biography, religious exemplum, family chronicle, political history, and classical comparison. Its signature features are an interventionist moral narrator, elevated yet readable diction, lengthy explanatory sentences, affectionate domestic detail, emblematic anecdotes, archaic dialogue, strong ethical contrasts, and a climactic focus on courageously preserving the memory and remains of the dead.

The story is written in a late-nineteenth-century regional-realist mode, combining close observation of village life with gentle humor, domestic sentiment, and an understated tragic ending. Its setting is established through concrete, repetitive details—the two cottage patterns, white paint, green blinds, geometrical flower beds, shoe factories, footpaths, gardens, and piazzas—which make Leyden feel visually coherent and socially enclosed. The village is not merely a backdrop but a collective organism whose physical uniformity reflects its inhabitants’ habitual, slow-moving lives. The repeated contrast between the “natty little white cottages” and the “great, ugly” factories gives the setting both picturesque charm and an economic foundation.

The narration is third-person omniscient, initially panoramic and communal before narrowing toward Maria Brewster and David Emmons. It moves easily between historical summary, descriptions of collective routine, village opinion, and intimate access to Maria’s feelings. The narrator’s voice is observant, sociologically alert, and gently intrusive, occasionally offering aphoristic commentary, as in the reflection that a practical nature can dry tears quickly. This creates a sense of affectionate authority: the narrator understands both the comedy visible to the village and the private pathos hidden beneath it.

The tone balances amusement and tenderness. David’s extreme slowness generates recurring comic exaggerations—the meetinghouse supposedly passing him on his way to work, or the uncertainty that he can reach Maria’s before Monday evening—but the humor is never cruel. It gradually acquires emotional weight as his slowness changes from an amusing personal eccentricity into the cause of a wasted lifetime. Maria’s practicality is treated in the same double register. Her concern that a tear might stain the pearl-colored silk is comic, yet it also reveals her disciplined way of containing grief. The dominant effect is compassionate irony: readers can see the approaching loss long before the characters fully confront it.

The prose alternates between long, carefully coordinated descriptive sentences and short declarative statements used for emphasis. Lists and parallel constructions are frequent, especially when describing cottages, daily work, household duties, and communal habits. Repetition is central to the style: “every Sunday night,” weekly baking, repeated visits, repeated near-proposals, and the habitual journey between the two cottages all create a cyclical rhythm. This repetition enacts Leyden’s inertia and makes the final interruption of routine especially powerful. Transitional phrases such as “Years before,” “That was a long time ago,” “As the years went on,” and “One Sunday night” carry the narrative across decades with the smoothness of oral storytelling.

The diction is generally formal and literary in the narration, with occasional elevated or playfully ornate phrasing applied to humble subjects. Expressions such as “materials for an innocent and delicious repast” or the comparison of David’s vegetable dinner to Herrick’s “pulse and herbs” lend comic dignity to ordinary rural life. Alongside this, domestic and occupational vocabulary—cording boots, baking, mending, sewing, pegging, gardening—grounds the story in material labor. Color and texture are used selectively but memorably: spring-green blinds, black and grimy factory walls, brown loaves, flaky pies, the slippery horsehair sofa, and the lustrous pearl-colored silk.

Dialogue is sparse and therefore highly significant. Village speech is rendered through mild regional dialect and eye dialect: “natur’,” “gittin’,” “leetle,” “jest,” “allus,” and “ag’in.” The dialect distinguishes spoken language from the narrator’s polished prose while conveying locality, class, age, and character. David’s halting syntax, hesitations, and self-effacing phrases embody his temperament. Maria’s replies are plainer, steadier, and more practical. Their final exchange gains force from this restraint: neither becomes suddenly eloquent, and David’s deathbed declaration remains fragmented by weakness and lifelong hesitation.

Characterization proceeds largely through habitual action rather than dramatic self-analysis. David is defined by slow movement, gardening, vegetable gifts, punctual courtship, and apologetic speech. Maria is revealed through baking, cleaning, mending, caring for her mother, preserving the wedding silk, and finally giving it away. Small objects carry disproportionate emotional meaning. The torn Sunday coat expresses David’s neglected solitude more powerfully for Maria than abstract reflection could; the wedding dress represents hope, suspended expectation, resignation, and generosity; the basket of vegetables and covered baking embody a marriage-like exchange of care without marriage itself.

The story’s structure moves from broad setting to social portrait, then to the long courtship, Maria’s inward resignation, David’s physical decline, and the final deathbed disclosure. Much of the couple’s twenty-five-year relationship is handled through summary and recurring scenes rather than continuous dramatic action. This compressed chronology gives the sense of an entire life passing almost imperceptibly. Near the end, the pace slows into scene: the ten o’clock departure, the anxious morning, the messenger, the crowd, the cleared sickroom, and David’s broken final words. The ending stops at the instant of belated confession, refusing consolation or extended aftermath. Its abruptness converts the story’s running joke into tragedy.

The principal stylistic tensions are between repetition and change, comedy and grief, public gossip and private feeling, sentiment and practicality, and domestic order and emotional incompletion. The village treats the courtship as communal entertainment, while the narration gradually uncovers its moral seriousness. The resulting style is warm, restrained, and quietly devastating: it avoids melodramatic declarations until the final line, allowing decades of ordinary gestures, routines, and missed opportunities to accumulate their own emotional force.

The story uses a warm, elevated mode of domestic realism, blending sentimental fiction with rural regionalism and religious consolation. Its emotional centre is the lifelong union of the elderly couple, presented not through dramatic conflict so much as through accumulated memories, humble sacrifices, domestic objects, and habitual acts of care. The tone is tender, reverent, nostalgic, and openly compassionate. Suffering is treated seriously—the death of a child, poverty, disability, age, and blindness—but the narrative repeatedly transforms loss into evidence of devotion, familial continuity, and spiritual grace.

The narration is third-person omniscient, moving freely among the perceptions of the grandparents, children, and other family members. The narrator often interprets experience directly, explaining the emotional or symbolic meaning of events rather than leaving it entirely implicit. At moments the voice addresses the reader with phrases such as “you would never know” and “You will see,” creating the intimacy of an oral storyteller or morally reflective observer. The point of view is especially sympathetic to Grandma’s inward life: present sensations readily dissolve into vivid recollections, so that memory becomes almost another temporal setting. The old couple’s love is described as instinctive and elemental, something they scarcely need to analyse because it has become “a fact of the universe.”

The prose alternates between long, flowing descriptive sentences and short, emphatic statements. Extended sentences often gather clauses through commas, semicolons, dashes, and repeated conjunctions, producing a meditative, cumulative rhythm suited to reminiscence. Parenthetical asides and interruptions imitate the associative movement of memory. Brief sentences and repeated phrases—“It’s gone!” or “I was afeared”—sharpen moments of alarm and emotional crisis. Parallel structures, anaphora, and balanced contrasts are common, particularly when the narration sets youth against age, sight against blindness, wealth against love, or worldly loss against heavenly restoration.

The diction divides into two complementary registers. The narrator employs polished, literary language, including words such as “beneficence,” “tremulously,” “commiseration,” and “transcend,” along with lyrical metaphors and biblical cadences. The characters speak in a marked rural vernacular, represented through contractions, nonstandard spellings, and grammatical forms such as “allers,” “their’n,” “her’n,” “ben,” “sech,” “suthin’,” and “so’t.” This dialect individualises the setting and class background while also giving the dialogue humour, warmth, and credibility. It is not used to ridicule the speakers; instead, their plain speech often carries the story’s deepest wisdom. The contrast between the doctor’s abstract language about natural law and Grandma’s simple account of divine comfort is especially characteristic.

Imagery is sensuous and pastoral. Dawn skies, misty meadows, dew-soaked leaves, roses, elm trees, stars, birds, moths, flowers, and sunlight surround the domestic world with natural beauty. Nature frequently reflects or enlarges human feeling: the wedding evening seems to bring heaven down around the couple, while two apples grown together on one stem become an emblem of their inseparable lives. Visual imagery is particularly abundant, making Grandma’s eventual blindness more poignant. Light and darkness function both literally and spiritually, with darkness signifying fear and deprivation but also becoming a condition in which faith, memory, and the love of others provide another form of sight.

Concrete objects carry substantial symbolic weight. The two canes embody age, labour, and physical decline; the topaz-and-pearl breastpin condenses courtship, poverty, sacrifice, bereavement, and enduring affection into one keepsake. Its disappearance and recovery give the otherwise episodic narrative a small thread of suspense while reinforcing the idea that ordinary possessions acquire sacred value through memory. The handmade coffin, cotton wool, candle, sewing, household trunk, almanac, and braided rug similarly anchor large emotions in the material details of rural life.

The structure is retrospective and episodic rather than tightly linear. It begins with the grandfather as the children know him, then moves backward into the couple’s youth and early marriage, passes through selected family memories, and returns to old age, blindness, and the multigenerational household. Transitions are often associative: the breastpin prompts recollections of the dead baby; the husband’s unchanged love leads into reflections on marital unity; the imagery of sight prepares for the revelation of blindness. This mosaic of remembered scenes creates the sense of an entire life distilled into representative moments. The final movement broadens the marriage into a family legacy, showing the grandparents’ influence on the children.

Dialogue supplies much of the story’s humour and pathos. The grandfather’s test involving the supposed burdock burr is gently comic, as is Jerry’s admission that his nose “itched turrible.” Elsewhere, dialogue becomes prayer, confession, or consolation. Emotional scenes rely on repetition, exclamation, direct address, and physical gestures—embracing, kissing, holding, resting a hand on a bowed head—to intensify feeling. The prayers are especially dramatic, contrasting the husband’s fierce demand for a miracle with the wife’s quieter plea for strength to accept darkness.

The story’s principal stylistic effect comes from joining the ordinary and the sacred. Farm labour, child-rearing, sewing, and inexpensive jewellery are described with the gravity of religious symbols, while faith is expressed through homely language and practical tenderness. Biblical allusions, providential language, and images of angels, Madonna-like figures, and resurrection give the domestic sphere a devotional aura. The ending crystallises this method in a concise sentimental apotheosis: Grandma’s blindness does not remove her from family life but makes her the beloved moral and spiritual centre of the house.

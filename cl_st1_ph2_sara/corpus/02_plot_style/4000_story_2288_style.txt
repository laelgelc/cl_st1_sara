The story adopts the form of a retrospective character sketch, narrated in the first person by an older speaker looking back on childhood. Its opening establishes memory as uncertain and porous: personal experience, family testimony, and stories heard or read may have merged over time. This reflective frame gives the narrative both intimacy and qualified reliability. Although the narrator professes doubt about the factual origins of some memories, the descriptions themselves are vivid, confident, and rich in concrete detail. The mature narrator also repeatedly revises childhood interpretations through later experience, creating a double perspective in which childish affection and curiosity coexist with adult irony and psychological insight.

The prose is leisurely, expansive, and anecdotal. Rather than being driven by a tightly unified plot, the story accumulates episodes that gradually reveal Yasha’s personality: his pride, devotion to the narrator’s father, care of his possessions, periodic drunkenness, resistance to dismissal, abortive marriage proposal, and enigmatic suicide. These incidents function like linked reminiscences, with each adding another contradictory trait. The structure moves from broad portraiture and comic domestic scenes toward increasingly dark material. The late shift into tragedy is abrupt but prepared by references to Yasha’s melancholy, humiliation, extreme emotions, and inscrutability. His death remains deliberately unexplained, preserving the mystery announced at the beginning.

The dominant mode is realist description inflected with affectionate caricature. Physical appearances, clothing, gestures, household objects, and regional customs are rendered precisely. Yasha’s “shaven face and thin pursed lips,” Matsko’s crooked legs and “bladder-like growths,” and the miscellaneous contents of the tin-bound trunk all make the remembered world tangible. Enumerations are frequent and often long, particularly when describing collections, habits, or social types. These lists create visual abundance while also revealing character: Yasha’s hoarded coats, cups, pictures, mirrors, and food embody his thrift, vanity, sentimentality, and desire for order.

The syntax is generally formal and elaborate, with long compound-complex sentences, balanced clauses, qualifications, and parenthetical asides. Rhetorical questions appear especially in reflective passages, reinforcing the narrator’s uncertainty about memory and motive. Dashes are used for interruptions, appositions, sudden qualifications, and emphatic turns of thought. The narration often begins with a general observation about human behaviour or provincial society and then narrows to a particular illustrative incident. This gives the prose the manner of an experienced raconteur who interprets events while recounting them.

The diction is literate and somewhat old-fashioned, combining elevated psychological language with domestic and regional vocabulary. Words such as “veneration,” “implacable,” “affliction,” “degradation,” and “matrimony” lend gravity to events that may initially seem comic or mundane. References to Podolia, Volhynia, Polish horses, the tarantass, versts, copecks, the Bible, and popular Russian imagery establish a specific Eastern European social world. Occasional explanatory remarks and the magazine footnote suggest a text addressing readers who may not know this cultural setting.

Humour arises chiefly from incongruity, understatement, exaggeration, and mock-heroic elevation. Yasha turns the family’s shabby carriage into a ceremonial departure; the decrepit Matsko is described in quasi-military terms; Bouton’s sociability is treated as moral disgrace; and a village policeman’s duties are said to consist mainly of serving as godfather to farmers’ children. The narrator often applies solemn language to ridiculous conduct, producing a gentle serio-comic effect. Animals are anthropomorphised in the same register: Bouton possesses “sympathy and devotion,” observes social rules, and joins Yasha in a “strange and touching duet,” while Matsko’s movements express hesitation and wounded dignity.

Characterisation is indirect and cumulative. Yasha is not reduced to a single explanation but built from clashes between dignity and absurdity, discipline and self-destruction, pride and servility, emotional reserve and extravagant repentance. His devotion to the father is nearly feudal, yet it gives him a peculiar authority within the household. His contempt for books, women, and other people coexists with meticulous service and deep attachment. Even his comic speeches carry an undertone of vulnerability. The narrator repeatedly labels him an “enigma,” and the story resists resolving his motives, especially in the marriage episode and suicide.

Dialogue is vivid, theatrical, and strongly individuating. Yasha’s speech is deliberate when sober but becomes repetitive, exclamatory, insulting, and rhythmically broken when drunk. His habitual forms of address—“brother,” “sir,” and “master”—express both hierarchy and self-importance. Ellipses reproduce hesitation or distress, while repeated questions and commands intensify confrontations. Dialogue scenes often unfold like miniature plays, with entrances, gestures, spectators, and abrupt reversals. They provide immediacy within the otherwise reflective narrative and allow Yasha’s contradictions to emerge without direct authorial explanation.

The tone blends nostalgia, amusement, tenderness, social observation, and melancholy. The narrator is affectionate but not sentimental in a simple way: he exposes vanity, drunkenness, cruelty, class prejudice, and self-deception while maintaining sympathy for Yasha. Comic episodes are frequently shadowed by pain, as when the children teasingly judge Yevka the better cleaner without understanding the severity of the wound to Yasha’s pride. Likewise, Bouton’s apparent selfless devotion is humorously reinterpreted as gratitude to the person who feeds him, yet the dog’s mourning after Yasha’s death restores genuine pathos.

The story’s social vision is observant and gently satirical. Provincial pretensions, class relations, ethnic tensions, household hierarchies, and weak institutions are presented through concrete behaviour rather than abstract argument. The narrator can reproduce the assumptions and terminology of the remembered milieu, but his mature commentary introduces distance from them. Authority is shown as informal and personal: the policeman is ineffectual, the father is indolently benevolent, and Yasha’s service depends more on attachment than contract.

The ending returns to the reflective voice of the opening and transforms the collection of anecdotes into a meditation on character and cultural identity. Its final sequence of adjectives—“faithful, pure, contradictory, absurd—and great”—compresses the story’s method into a paradoxical catalogue. The concluding question elevates Yasha from an eccentric servant into a possible embodiment of the “Slav soul,” but the interrogative form keeps that interpretation tentative. Overall, the style is that of a humane realist memoir: digressive, concrete, ironic, culturally textured, psychologically curious, and increasingly elegiac.

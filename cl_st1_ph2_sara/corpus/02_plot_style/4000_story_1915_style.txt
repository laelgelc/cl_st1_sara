The story is a comic maritime yarn told through a framed first-person monologue. It opens with the night-watchman offering a broad, apparently philosophical observation about bullying at sea, then moves into an anecdote that illustrates—and mischievously complicates—his point. The narrative voice is that of an experienced, self-assured sailor speaking to an implied listener. His tone combines worldly wisdom, mock solemnity and selective innocence: he reports vindictive or absurd behaviour as though it were reasonable, repeatedly defending the crew’s conduct while the reader is invited to see their cowardice, self-interest and hypocrisy.

The most conspicuous stylistic feature is the sustained representation of vernacular speech. Pronunciation is rendered through eye dialect and elision—“’e,” “’is,” “ses,” “arter,” “fust,” “wot,” “agin,” “cert’nly”—while nonstandard grammar includes constructions such as “we was,” “them two men,” and “if I was them.” Maritime contractions and vocabulary, including “fo’c’s’le,” “hands,” “barque,” “mate,” “skipper,” “v’y’ge,” “holy-stone” and “turn in,” anchor the narrative in shipboard culture. The dialect is consistent enough to establish class, occupation and oral immediacy, but remains broadly intelligible. Period racial terms and stereotypes are embedded in the sailors’ speech and comic disguise plot; they function as markers of the story’s historical social attitudes rather than as neutral contemporary diction.

Dialogue dominates the story and drives both characterization and action. Exchanges are quick, adversarial and often built around literal-minded replies, insults, evasions and reversals. Characters reveal themselves less through description than through what they say under pressure. Joe Smith’s soft-spoken suggestions mask manipulation; Bill’s threats and complaints expose his volatility; the carpenter’s professional pride shades into irresponsibility; the mate’s boastfulness traps him; and the skipper’s confident deductions complete the misunderstanding. Repeated speech tags such as “ses Bill” and “ses Joe” reinforce the oral, anecdotal rhythm and keep the narration unpolished in a deliberate, performative way.

The prose alternates between short conversational volleys and longer narrative sentences linked by “and,” “but,” “then” and “well.” This paratactic syntax resembles spontaneous storytelling: events appear to be added as the speaker remembers them, with frequent transitional formulas such as “Well,” “To cut a long story short,” and “I remember.” Comic detail is accumulated through lists and escalating clauses. The attempts to remove the blacking—soap, butter, linseed oil, tea leaves and turpentine—form a particularly effective catalogue, each failed remedy increasing the farcical pressure. Descriptions are concrete, exaggerated and visual: the mate returns with his necktie around his ear, Bill and Bob resemble “two wax figgers,” and the mate swells with anger until the narrator wonders how he avoids bursting.

The humour depends chiefly on dramatic irony, reversal and escalating misunderstanding. Bill and Bob disguise themselves in order to attack the mate without punishment, only to receive a severe beating and discover that their colouring will not wash off. The mate preserves his pride by inventing five German attackers; the disguised sailors then appropriate the same lie, causing the skipper to identify a fictitious gang and to treat all three men as victims of one assault. The mate understands the truth but cannot reveal it without admitting that two supposedly “mild and meek” local men defeated him. Thus vanity becomes a mechanism of self-silencing. The final reversal is especially neat: the disguise that threatened to expose the crew becomes the evidence that protects them.

Structurally, the tale follows a clean comic arc: generalized reflection, introduction of the bully, conspiratorial plan, offstage fight, disastrous aftermath, threat of discovery, public confrontation and ironic resolution. Much of the crucial violence occurs offstage and is reconstructed through damaged bodies, conflicting accounts and boastful testimony. This keeps the emphasis on social embarrassment rather than physical brutality. Suspense arises not from uncertainty about what happened, but from whether the deception will survive contact with authority. The blacking that cannot be removed acts as both a physical comic prop and a visible countdown to exposure.

Characterization is economical and caricatural, suited to a short comic tale. Each figure has a dominant trait—the mate’s bullying pride, Bill’s resentment, Joe’s slyness, the carpenter’s complacency, the skipper’s decent but limited judgment—but these traits interact in ways that generate the plot. The crew often behaves as a collective, expressed through “we,” communal speculation, meetings and shared efforts at concealment. Yet that solidarity is opportunistic: the men encourage the attack, then suggest that Bill and Bob desert, and finally restrain them when they threaten to confess. The narrator’s insistence on the crew’s “better feelings,” “kindness” and sincerity creates a persistent gap between his moral account and their actual conduct.

The tone remains genial even when the material involves bullying, assault, threats with a razor and racial impersonation. Violence is stylized through hyperbole, slapstick and aftermath rather than treated with psychological gravity. Sentimental language is repeatedly used inappropriately—“pore black fellers,” “wictims of a cruel outrage,” “better feelings prevailed”—producing mock pathos. Understatement has a similar effect: severe beatings become “a turn with the mate” or “a doing,” and conspiracy is described as taking “a great interest” in an idea.

Overall, the style is that of a compact Edwardian comic sea story: strongly voiced, dialect-heavy, dialogue-led and built around an ingenious social trap. Its signature effect comes from the mismatch between the narrator’s complacent version of events and the reader’s clearer perception of collective guilt, vanity and absurdity. The ending restores shipboard order without delivering formal justice; instead, punishment is distributed informally, with the mate humiliated, the conspirators bruised, and the entire crew still subject to his displaced resentment.

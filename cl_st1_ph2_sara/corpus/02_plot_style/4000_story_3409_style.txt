The story uses a retrospective first-person narrator whose voice is measured, respectable, and mildly self-important. He presents domestic concerns with earnest gravity, treating gardens, poultry, household security, and social reputation as matters of great consequence. This sober perspective forms the comic baseline: the narrator rarely announces a joke, but his composure allows ordinary problems and Pomona’s extreme solutions to become increasingly absurd. His relationship with Euphemia is affectionate and companionable, expressed through shared deliberation rather than sentimentality.

The tone is genial, domestic, and gently satirical. Humour arises through incongruity, escalation, and understatement: a simple trip produces elaborate security measures; an unwanted tradesman leads to siege tactics; a dogfight unexpectedly creates the prospect of a church appointment; and a fraudulent tax notice becomes a practical defence against salesmen. The narrator and Euphemia react with conventional alarm, while Pomona regards each extraordinary action as obvious common sense. This contrast sustains the story’s deadpan comedy without making the characters cruel or contemptible.

The diction combines polished nineteenth-century narration with strongly differentiated vernacular dialogue. The narrator’s language is formal and circumlocutory, using phrases such as “it became necessary,” “the prospect was delightful,” and “painful apprehensions.” Pomona’s speech is colloquial, energetic, and nonstandard, marked by contractions, dialect spellings, double negatives, dropped endings, and idioms such as “that wasn’t my lookout.” Her spoken grammar conveys social position and personality, but she is also the story’s most capable and inventive figure.

A second linguistic register appears in Pomona’s unfinished “novel.” Here she parodies melodramatic popular fiction through inflated vocabulary, archaic constructions, portentous foreshadowing, stock chapter titles, and exaggerated emotional formulas. Mundane actions become “frugal repasts,” “verdant pathways,” and confrontations with “vile intriguers.” The conspicuously syllabified pronunciation of words such as “man-si-on” and “occur-red” creates an oral-performance joke, suggesting that Pomona understands “novel language” as something ceremonious that must be spoken differently. Her malapropisms and inventive expressions, including “burst into bark-ter,” add verbal comedy while revealing enthusiasm rather than stupidity.

The syntax of the framing narration tends toward long, balanced sentences with multiple clauses, qualifications, and parenthetical comments. Repetition often imitates careful reasoning and gives trivial decisions mock-solemn weight. Pomona’s oral account moves more rapidly, relying on coordination, repeated “and,” direct quotation, abrupt reversals, and action verbs. Exchanges are frequently built from “says I” and “says he,” creating the rhythm of an anecdote told aloud. Interruptions from Euphemia and the narrator punctuate the account, supply reactions, delay explanations, and sharpen punch lines.

Structurally, the story is a comic frame narrative. The opening establishes the journey and the owners’ anxiety about leaving Rudder Grange; their early return then creates a mystery through locked gates, missing objects, and the alarming tax placard. Pomona’s written record becomes an embedded narrative that explains these signs retrospectively. The account proceeds episodically—lightning-rod salesman, hostile bulldog, dogfight, minister, tree salesman—with each episode generating consequences for the next. Suspense is repeatedly sustained by postponing the explanation of the tax notice, even as Pomona digresses into increasingly outlandish adventures. The anticlimactic revelation that the notice was merely a deterrent resolves the apparent catastrophe while confirming her unconventional practicality.

Characterization depends more on speech and conduct than on physical description. Pomona is resourceful, literal-minded, proud, fearless, imaginative, and largely indifferent to conventional propriety. The narrator sees her as socially inexperienced, yet the plot repeatedly proves her more effective than the supposedly responsible adults. Euphemia functions as an emotionally expressive audience, voicing humane objections and astonishment. The dogs are treated almost as comic characters, their aggression becoming both a household complication and an improvised security system.

The setting is a recognizable late-nineteenth-century semi-rural household, with its garden, barn, livestock, servants, itinerant tradesmen, church vestry, and dependence on rail travel. Concrete domestic details anchor the absurdity and give the narrative a pastoral, middle-class texture. The story lightly satirizes household respectability, aggressive commerce, social aspiration, sensational fiction, and the gap between official authority and practical competence. Its governing style is therefore one of controlled comic contrast: formal narration against vernacular storytelling, respectable appearances against chaotic events, and naïve literary ambition against shrewd real-world intelligence.

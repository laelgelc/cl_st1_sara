The story uses a collective first-person narrator, the communal “we” of Bibi Haldar’s female neighbours. This creates a voice that is intimate yet diffuse: the narrators observe, assist, gossip, judge, and occasionally admit the limits of their compassion. Because no single speaker is fully individuated, the town itself becomes a character and a social chorus. The voice moves easily between sympathy and complicity, allowing the narrative to expose communal prejudices without turning the neighbours into simple villains. Retrospective phrases such as “for years afterward” give the account the quality of a remembered local history.

The prose is lucid, controlled, and densely particular. Its vocabulary alternates between plain domestic language and more formal or clinical terms such as “auscultations,” “paroxysm,” “malaise,” and “conjugal life.” This contrast supports the story’s dry irony: Bibi’s condition is surrounded by elaborate diagnoses and remedies, yet nobody understands it or addresses the social deprivation underlying her distress. Long accumulative sentences and catalogues are a defining feature. Lists of healers, treatments, household skills, cosmetics, wedding rituals, foods, and market goods create comic abundance while also locating Bibi within a material and ritual culture that continually measures her fitness as a woman.

Sentence rhythms shift according to emotional pressure. Extended, clause-heavy sentences summarize years of treatment or layers of social custom, while short declarative sequences intensify seizures and moments of abandonment: “She shook. She shuddered. She chewed her lips.” Parallel structures and repetition reinforce obsession, especially Bibi’s repeated “never” and the neighbours’ recurrent questions. Parenthetical remarks and abrupt qualifications add the texture of oral storytelling, as though the community is correcting, elaborating, or distancing itself from its own account.

Dialogue is frequent, compressed, and socially revealing. Characters speak through practical judgments, clichés, accusations, and lists of expectations rather than through extended introspection. Bibi’s speech is louder, more theatrical, and more emotionally exposed than that of those around her. Her laments about marriage rely on ceremonial images—milk, sandalwood, turmeric, scarlet invitations—showing that she imagines belonging through the visible forms of domestic life. Haldar and his wife speak in the language of expense, respectability, contamination, and inconvenience, reducing Bibi to a liability. The neighbours’ advice often sounds affectionate but also reproduces conventional assumptions about feminine appearance, marriage, and service.

The tone combines compassion, deadpan comedy, social satire, and quiet horror. Comic effects arise from contradictory medical advice, absurd folk remedies, the blunt matrimonial advertisement, and the town’s intrusive speculation. These elements are never merely whimsical; their humour gradually reveals exploitation and neglect. The narration usually avoids overt moral pronouncements, allowing irony to emerge from juxtaposition—for example, the community’s energetic concern during Bibi’s public attacks contrasts with its acceptance of leaving her alone each night. The understatement surrounding her pregnancy is especially disturbing. The story refers to an apparent sexual assault obliquely, through missing memory, unanswered questions, and the unexplained orderliness of the room.

The setting is rendered through concrete domestic and neighbourhood detail rather than broad exposition. Rooftops, clotheslines, stairwells, shops, courtyards, fish ponds, ration queues, religious sounds, seasonal foods, fabrics, and cosmetics create a crowded urban world in which privacy is scarce but genuine protection remains uncertain. Sensory imagery is strong but economical: incense thickens the dusk, film songs blare from trees, labels fade, colognes turn rank, and pigeon droppings cover parapets. Seasonal transitions organize time and subtly register Bibi’s changing circumstances, from oppressive heat and seizures to autumn commerce, winter isolation, April pregnancy, and September birth.

Structurally, the narrative is episodic and largely chronological, compressing long periods before slowing around decisive scenes. Bibi’s successive “treatments” provide an organizing motif, with marriage proposed as the most outrageous cure. A brief backward movement recounts her father’s attempts to understand her illness, adding emotional depth while preserving narrative momentum. The plot then turns through exclusion, abandonment, unexplained pregnancy, motherhood, and economic independence. The final transformation echoes earlier details: the storage room changes from a place of confinement and unpaid labour into Bibi’s home and shop, while the inventory she once kept for Haldar becomes the basis of her own livelihood.

Characterization is indirect and cumulative. Bibi is built from physical details, habits, complaints, fantasies, practical limitations, and changes in conduct rather than from interior narration. At first she appears dependent, socially awkward, and consumed by matrimonial longing; by the end, she is competent, self-supporting, and maternal. This arc remains morally ambiguous because her “cure” follows a likely violation. The closing sentence—“She was, to the best of our knowledge, cured”—is deliberately restrained and unsettling. It echoes medical language while questioning what cure means, exposes the narrators’ incomplete knowledge, and leaves unresolved whether Bibi’s recovery results from motherhood, autonomy, social recognition, or the community’s need to impose a consoling explanation.

Overall, the style is realist, observational, and socially incisive. It draws emotional force from restraint rather than sentimentality, even while depicting a character prone to melodramatic speech. Its signature qualities are the communal narrative voice, culturally specific inventories, rhythmic accumulation, understated irony, domestic material detail, and an ending that appears neat on the surface while preserving profound ethical uncertainty.

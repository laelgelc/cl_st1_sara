The story uses a retrospective first-person frame in which an inquisitive, largely passive narrator draws an authoritative older man into recounting a concealed historical episode. The country-house setting, tea on the terrace, ancient oaks, evening light, and a distant memorial establish an atmosphere of cultivated English calm against which the darker wartime narrative unfolds. Sir Henry Marquis functions as both detective and storyteller: his superior knowledge controls the disclosure, while the narrator’s brief questions voice the reader’s confusion and prompt transitions between apparently disconnected parts of the mystery.

The prose belongs to the early twentieth-century mystery and espionage tradition, combining country-house detective fiction with a wartime tale of secret intelligence. Its diction is formal but accessible, with period expressions such as “My word,” “decent chap,” “half-penny,” and “the tragic muse.” National and historical references—to Herodotus, Rugby, Alfred’s White Horse, Attila, Chemosh, Beelzebub, and the gods of the seas—give the narration a learned, mythic register. These allusions enlarge a practical intelligence failure into an example of classical irony and tragic fate. The repeated use of “England,” “the English people,” and “the Hun” also reflects the emphatic national rhetoric and enemy stereotypes of First World War popular fiction.

Dialogue dominates, but Sir Henry’s speeches often become long, polished narrative monologues. His voice is grave, assured, analytical, and aphoristic. He repeatedly states a public interpretation and then reverses it: the suicide presented as murder, apparent German chivalry exposed as cruelty, and celebrated heroism revealed as the accidental consequence of personal catastrophe. Phrases such as “Every visible impression of the thing was wrong” make this method explicit. The storytelling depends on antithesis—glory and despair, legend and fact, accident and calculation, monument and private shame—and on the gradual correction of appearances.

The syntax alternates between expansive descriptive sentences and short emphatic declarations. Longer sentences accumulate clauses, qualifications, and parallel constructions, especially when reconstructing military preparations or moral contradictions. Short sentences—“It was all grim calculation!” or “And he did use them”—punctuate these passages with certainty and dramatic force. Repetition is a major rhetorical device. The heroic phrase “Don’t threaten, fire if you like!” recurs until it becomes almost monumental, while repeated references to the white shaft, the bandage, Plutonburg’s grin, and St. Alban’s mental “blur” bind the separate stages of the revelation together. Sir Henry also circles around crucial facts before naming them, repeating judgments and implications to heighten suspense.

Description is selective and strongly visual. The framing landscape is rendered through broad, picturesque elements—the “dark, creeping river,” wooded hills, filtered sunlight, and the memorial like a “white needle.” Wartime scenes use cinematic tableaux: the choppy Channel, the submarine’s “long whaleback,” the crowded deck, the injured man in the center, and the armed commander before him. Character portraits are similarly emblematic rather than psychologically subtle. Sir Henry is physically imposing and controlled; St. Alban is marked by livid features and nervous gestures; Plutonburg is grotesquely stylized through infernal, pagan, and militaristic imagery, his face seeming “set in iron.” Gestures involving hands, cups, sleeves, and pacing provide pauses in the monologue and externalize restrained emotion.

The pacing is deliberately delayed. The story begins by dismissing the apparent mystery of St. Alban’s death, then spends substantial time establishing the national legend surrounding him. Only afterward does it introduce doubts about the submarine encounter, the failure of the Somme offensive, St. Alban’s anti-espionage work, and the journey through France. These “unjointed segments,” as the narrator calls them, are finally connected by the revelation that the secret military map was hidden inside the dressing on St. Alban’s arm. The structure therefore resembles a detective explanation delivered backward: consequences and false interpretations come first, while the decisive physical clue arrives near the end.

The tonal surface is restrained, ceremonious, and reflective, but underneath it lies melodrama and moral horror. Sir Henry remains emotionally level even when describing espionage, suicide, and psychological collapse, which intensifies the effect through understatement. At climactic points, however, the prose becomes exclamatory and sensational, using words such as “Satanic,” “hellish,” “awfully,” “ghastly,” and “devil.” This mixture of gentlemanly reserve and lurid Gothic imagery is central to the story’s appeal.

The governing theme is tragic irony. St. Alban devotes himself to preventing espionage yet unknowingly becomes the carrier of the enemy’s most valuable intelligence. His private humiliation produces his public immortality; the words spoken in a state of shock become a perfect expression of English courage; and the enemy’s attempt to condemn him to shame inadvertently helps create a hero. The monument symbolizes the permanence of the false but socially useful legend, while Sir Henry’s account preserves the unbearable private truth. The style repeatedly emphasizes that history is made not only from facts but from memorable scenes, phrases, and national desires.

The text also contains several apparent transcription or source errors—such as “forge” for “forget,” “may lies,” “There wag,” and “tit, Alban”—along with irregular punctuation and spacing. These are best treated as textual corruptions rather than intentional stylistic features. Beneath them, the prose is highly patterned: oratorical, suspenseful, allusive, morally emphatic, and constructed around the progressive inversion of a heroic public narrative.

After returning to his hometown, the narrator learns that the Wightmans lost their business and are reputed to be “very poor.” He first visits Payson, a supposedly prosperous friend who lives extravagantly. Beneath Payson’s showy home and luxurious lifestyle, however, lie debt, financial anxiety, family dissatisfaction, and an endless desire for greater wealth and status.

The narrator then visits the Wightmans. Though they live modestly and work hard, their home is warm, orderly, and cheerful. Wightman, now a clerk, has gradually improved his salary, his daughters contribute through honest work, and the family has just finished paying for its small house. Their contentment, gratitude, and affection make them feel rich despite limited means.

The narrator tells Payson that true poverty belongs not to those with little, but to those who are never satisfied with what they possess. Payson recognizes that his own family is poor in this deeper sense because they are neither contented nor thankful. Soon afterward, his unsustainable lifestyle leads to financial failure, leaving him poor both materially and spiritually.

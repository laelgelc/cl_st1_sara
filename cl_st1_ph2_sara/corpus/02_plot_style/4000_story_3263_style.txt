The story uses a polished, economical nineteenth-century narrative style that combines romantic subject matter with urbane irony. Its prose is formal but generally lucid, moving briskly through social background, emotional crises, historical change, and final revelation. Although the lovers initially appear in the conventional language of sentimental romance—eternal vows, cruel parents, secret correspondence, ominous dreams—the narrator maintains a gently amused distance from their behaviour. Phrases such as “Needless to mention,” “quite naturally,” and “this happy idea” quietly expose the artificiality of their romantic assumptions without wholly dismissing the sincerity of their feelings.

The narrator is omniscient, sociable, and conspicuously present. The narrative voice addresses the reader through inclusive transitions such as “we will return to our young lover” and “let us return,” openly directing attention between parallel strands of the plot. It also pauses to make witty generalisations about love, women, society, and self-justifying morality. These comments create the impression of an experienced storyteller recounting extraordinary events with composure. The irony is mild rather than cruel: characters are indulged for their vanity, sentimentality, or impulsiveness, while their suffering remains emotionally credible.

The diction belongs to an upper-class provincial and military world. Russian names, ranks, estates, sledges, versts, regiments, servants, and domestic rituals supply social and historical specificity, while French and Italian quotations reflect the cosmopolitan education of the gentry. The vocabulary often alternates between romantic elevation—“invincible strength of passion,” “insurmountable barrier,” “torment and consolation”—and dry practical detail concerning meals, witnesses, horses, clothing, roads, and household arrangements. This contrast is central to the comedy: exalted emotion repeatedly collides with weather, geography, fatigue, misunderstanding, and chance.

Sentence structure is predominantly balanced and syntactically formal. Long sentences coordinate actions in orderly sequences, especially during journeys and summaries of elapsed time, while shorter sentences provide comic or dramatic emphasis. The abrupt answer “Nothing,” following the question of what happened at Nenaradova, is a characteristic example of controlled anticlimax. Repetition is used to convey frustration and delay: Vladimir drives “on and on,” the sledge is repeatedly overturned, and the missing destination is repeatedly invoked. Semicolons, colons, dashes, and ellipses regulate shifts between narration, reflection, and emotional suspension. Some punctuation and phrasing have the slightly stiff or ornate quality of an older English translation.

Description is selective rather than luxuriant. Characters are introduced through a few socially legible traits: Maria is “pale” and “slender,” Bourmin has an “interesting pallor,” a wounded arm, black eyes, and a military decoration. Settings are likewise reduced to decisive visual and atmospheric elements. The snowstorm receives the most sustained descriptive attention. Its obscured roads, indistinguishable earth and sky, snowdrifts, darkness, and exhausted horses generate suspense while also functioning as the story’s governing mechanism of fate. Weather is lightly personified—the wind seems to resist Maria’s escape—and acquires both symbolic and practical force.

The pacing depends on sharp variation. Large periods of illness, war, bereavement, and mourning are compressed into summary, whereas the failed elopement and Bourmin’s confession are rendered scene by scene. The first half uses cross-cutting between Maria’s departure and Vladimir’s disastrous journey, allowing the reader to understand that the plan has miscarried while withholding the crucial event at the church. A substantial temporal leap then converts youthful romantic tragedy into mature social comedy. The final courtship slows the pace again, concentrates attention on hesitation and secrecy, and prepares a retrospective explanation of the concealed episode.

Dialogue is economical and functional. Domestic exchanges are plain and repetitive, revealing the parents’ affectionate innocence. The peasant dialogue during Vladimir’s journey is terse, practical, and faintly comic, intensifying his desperation through the old man’s unhurried responses. By contrast, declarations of love adopt the elevated formulas of sentimental fiction. Bourmin’s confession becomes an extended embedded narrative, reconstructing the missing wedding scene and transforming earlier mystery into recognition. Maria’s brief interruptions accelerate the revelation, and the story ends at the precise instant of mutual recognition rather than supplying an explanatory aftermath.

Characterisation is achieved through behaviour, social observation, and ironic categorisation rather than deep psychological analysis. Maria’s imagination has been shaped by French novels, so she interprets life through literary models and consciously arranges romantic scenes. Vladimir is ardent and energetic but helpless before chance. Bourmin combines decorum, mystery, recklessness, and remorse, making him both a romantic hero and an agent of absurd accident. Secondary figures are rapidly sketched through rank, clothing, age, or manner, often with comic precision.

Structurally, the story is built around concealment, coincidence, reversal, and delayed recognition. The apparent tragedy of a missed wedding conceals a different wedding; Vladimir’s failure enables Bourmin’s accidental entrance; Maria’s fidelity to a dead lover prevents ordinary marriage until the unknown husband reappears. The snowstorm links the two men’s journeys and turns random misdirection into apparent destiny. Foreshadowing appears through Maria’s dreams, the ominous weather, the secrecy surrounding the church, and Bourmin’s unexplained reserve, but the decisive information is withheld fairly enough for the conclusion to reinterpret the entire plot.

The dominant tonal mixture is sentimental, comic, suspenseful, and fatalistic. The story borrows the apparatus of romance—secret elopement, parental opposition, prophetic dreams, war, mourning, a mysterious officer, and a confession by a pond—but handles it with classical restraint and ironic intelligence. Its governing effect comes from the tension between human plans and uncontrollable contingency. Characters believe they are living out literary destinies, yet their lives are redirected by a wrong road, a snowstorm, an impulsive prank, and incomplete information. The ending converts these accidents into the shape of providence, producing a concise, theatrical dénouement that is at once improbable, emotionally satisfying, and knowingly playful.

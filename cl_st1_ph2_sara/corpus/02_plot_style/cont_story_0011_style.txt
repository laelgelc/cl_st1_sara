The story uses a conversational, observant realism shaped by collective small-town narration. Its dominant voice is the first-person plural “we”: a communal consciousness that watches, labels, jokes about, and judges Gloria before gradually discovering the limits of its judgment. This “we” is intimate enough to know local histories and habits but not fully reliable; it frequently speculates through phrases such as “I imagine,” “likely,” “perhaps,” and “we thought.” At key moments the narration shifts into “I,” then into direct second-person address—“imagine you are”—to move the reader from detached ridicule toward identification with Gloria and Randy. The flexibility of viewpoint allows the narrative to enter several minds while retaining the feel of town gossip.

The tone begins as dry, amused, and mildly cruel. Gloria is reduced to birdlike features, predictable remarks, and social blankness, while Randy and his mother are rendered through similarly comic caricature. Yet the satire is tempered by curiosity and, increasingly, tenderness. The story does not abruptly condemn the townspeople’s mockery; instead, it implicates the narrators in it and lets their confidence erode as the apparently unpromising romance succeeds. By the end, the tone has become warm and cautiously hopeful without turning sentimental. The qualification that the marriage “may not end well” preserves the story’s realism, while “it has not ended badly yet” offers a modest, durable form of optimism.

The diction is largely plain, colloquial, and contemporary. Common expressions—“raining cats and dogs,” “hung the moon,” “off to the races,” “fly on the wall”—are central to the characterization and thematic design. At first, clichés seem to prove Gloria’s lack of originality, but the narrators themselves repeatedly rely on stock phrases, exposing their own conventionality. Informal words and phrases such as “goading,” “no skin off her ass,” “big honker glasses,” “or whatnot,” and “thank you very much” create a spoken, locally grounded texture. Against this casual language, occasional elevated or precise formulations—“a strongly self-certain lightning bolt of constant opining,” “a series of light but painful little blows,” “theatrical beneficence”—lend psychological and moral depth.

The syntax alternates between brief, deadpan statements and long, accumulating sentences. Gloria’s dialogue is short, literal, and repetitive, often merely naming or affirming what is already evident: “A horse,” “Some fresh air,” “It’s a good sandwich.” The narration, by contrast, frequently expands into winding sentences filled with parentheses, qualifications, lists, corrections, and comic digressions. These sentences imitate both gossip and thought in motion, continually revising themselves as they approach a fairer understanding of the characters. Repetition is prominent: “She had a way,” “And imagine,” “whether she is nearby or not,” and “singing his/her praises.” Such recurrences create rhythm while marking changes in perception.

Characterization depends on sharply selected physical details, habits, and objects rather than extensive exposition. Gloria’s darting movement, brown apron, curling iron, perfume, and rehearsed laughter establish her vulnerability and awkward attempts at self-fashioning. Randy is defined by his truck, Friday washing ritual, dirty jokes, damaged-orange voice, snacks, notes, and cartoon drawing. His mother is condensed into her inherited men’s glasses, tanned face, alcoholic growl, and pride in “laceratingly honest” judgment. These details are comic but also revealing: ordinary possessions and routines become evidence of loneliness, vanity, loyalty, and the desire to matter.

Dialogue is used sparingly and functions less as dramatic exchange than as social evidence. Gloria’s predictable responses initially support the town’s dismissive portrait of her, while Randy interprets the same responses as generosity, confidence, and compatibility. The stockroom kiss crystallizes this dynamic: “Not bad” and “Not bad at all” are banal phrases, yet they become meaningful because the lovers receive them as mutual affirmation. The story repeatedly shows that intimacy can arise not from verbal brilliance but from a sympathetic interpretation of ordinary words.

Structurally, the narrative proceeds as a comic social fable. It opens with a static portrait of Gloria, introduces her unreturned attraction, tracks her unsuccessful attempts to become interesting and feminine, then pivots when Randy begins to enjoy being admired. The repeated “imagine” passages form the emotional center, slowing the plot and placing the reader inside each lover’s transformative experience. The anticipated “fall” becomes instead a courtship, kiss, wedding, and continuing marriage. This reversal defeats both the townspeople’s predictions and the conventional expectation that naïve love must end in humiliation.

Imagery is modest but pointed. The sparrow motif initially makes Gloria seem small, nervous, caged, and insignificant; by the end, the description “small, glowing lady” revises rather than erases that first image. The bell-less church tower provides a gently absurd emblem for an imperfect but functional union: it lacks what a church tower is expected to possess, just as the couple lacks conventional glamour, yet it still serves its purpose. The chocolate mint, Twinkie, handwritten notes, and stars pouring from a cartoon man’s head turn cheap, everyday objects into credible tokens of passion.

The story’s central stylistic effect comes from the gradual moral correction of its own voice. It begins by treating Gloria as an object of communal scrutiny and ends by showing that the observers were no more imaginative than the woman they mocked. Love does not make Gloria and Randy sophisticated; it changes the meanings attached to their ordinary qualities. Their agreement, predictability, and mutual praise become sustaining virtues. The resulting style is comic, digressive, socially acute, and compassionate, using gossip, cliché, caricature, and free-ranging perspective to reveal how easily apparent dullness can conceal a rich capacity for devotion.

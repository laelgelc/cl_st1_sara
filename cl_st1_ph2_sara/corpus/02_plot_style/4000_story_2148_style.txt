The story adopts the manner of an oral “just-so” folktale: playful, intimate, explanatory and knowingly fanciful. An intrusive storyteller addresses the child listener as “Best Beloved,” offers asides and instructions such as “Say that quickly aloud,” and repeatedly assures the audience that improbable events really happened. This creates the feeling of a bedtime performance rather than an impersonal written narrative. The narrator’s voice is affectionate and authoritative but also mischievous, frequently sharing jokes with the listener and gently mocking adult seriousness.

The diction mixes nursery language, comic invention and exaggerated formality. Childlike contractions and distortions—“’Member,” “’sclusively,” and “’em”—sit beside mock-scientific phrases such as “present habitat of the aboriginal Fauna” and “aboriginal Flora.” The contrast makes educated, grown-up speech sound pompous and unnecessarily indirect. Invented or intensified compounds such as “patchy-blatchy,” “speckly-spickly,” “slippery-slidy” and “sprottled and spottled” give the prose a tactile, musical quality. Long strings of colour terms—“greyish-yellowish-reddish,” “rich fulvous orange-tawny”—turn visual description into comic verbal abundance.

Rhythm depends heavily on repetition, accumulation and patterned phrasing. Lists of animals, landscapes, colours and markings create a rolling, incantatory cadence, while refrains such as “He knew,” “They didn’t indeed!” and “I can smell … and I can hear … but I can’t see” imitate spoken storytelling and invite anticipation. Sentences often expand through chains of coordination, especially repeated “and” clauses, before ending in a punch line or emphatic comment. Exclamations, interjections and sound effects—“Fiddle!”, “Ummm,” “Hi! Hi!” and “Ho! Ho!”—make the narration lively and performable aloud.

The imagery is highly visual and concerned with colour, pattern, light and camouflage. The bare High Veldt is rendered through sandy monotones, while the forest is built from “stripy, speckly, patchy-blatchy shadows.” Description frequently imitates what it depicts: clustered adjectives suggest spots, recurring parallel lines evoke stripes, and dense catalogues reproduce the forest’s broken light. Similes are concrete, humorous and deliberately incongruous, as in “ripe bananas in a smokehouse,” “a bar of soap in a coal-scuttle” and “a mustard-plaster on a sack of coals.”

Dialogue drives much of the story and gives each figure a comic identity. The Leopard is literal-minded, vain and excitable; the Ethiopian is practical but inclined toward long words; Baviaan speaks in riddling puns. The central wordplay turns on “spots,” first understood as places and then as markings on the Leopard’s skin. Characters often misunderstand figurative language or interpret it too literally, allowing the plot’s transformation to emerge from a joke. Their exchanges use balanced retorts, teasing insults and repeated formulas, producing the rhythm of comic repartee.

Structurally, the narrative follows an etiological folktale pattern: it begins in a mythical time when the world was unfinished, presents an environmental problem, introduces advice in riddling form, stages discovery and transformation, and ends by explaining a familiar natural feature. Cause and effect are intentionally naïve and magical. Animals acquire markings because shadows fall on them, and the Leopard’s spots are made by five black fingertips. The conclusion closes the circle by referring to the proverbial question about changing skin and spots, treating the proverb as garbled evidence that the narrated events once occurred.

The tone remains buoyant, conspiratorial and mock-solemn throughout. Danger and hunting are softened by absurdity, domestic references to breakfasts, dinners and tea, and the cartoon-like resilience of the animals. Capitalisation such as “Quite the Wisest Animal in All South Africa” parodies heroic or scriptural titles, while direct appeals to common sense humorously defend the impossible. Beneath the fantasy lies a simple theme of adaptation: survival depends on matching one’s environment and being willing to change. The prose presents this idea not through realism but through sound play, repetition, visual patterning and the confident illogic of a tale invented to answer a child’s question. Its period voice also includes racial terminology and assumptions characteristic of older colonial children’s literature, elements that now read as dated even though the immediate narrative mode is comic and folkloric.

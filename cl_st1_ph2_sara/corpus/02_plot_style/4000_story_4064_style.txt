The story adopts the style of a nineteenth-century historical and moral narrative, combining chronicle, biography, military history, religious exemplum, and heroic anecdote. Its principal purpose is not merely to recreate sixteenth-century Russia but to draw ethical lessons from historical events. The opening date, “1564,” gives the piece an annalistic frame, while the early passages supply broad geopolitical background before narrowing toward exemplary individuals: Prince Andrej Kourbsky, his servant Vasili Shibanoff, and Archbishop Feeleep. The resulting structure is episodic rather than conventionally dramatic. The siege of Kazan establishes Ivan’s former promise and Kourbsky’s martial stature; the narrative then turns to Ivan’s moral degeneration, Kourbsky’s flight, Vasili’s martyr-like loyalty, and Feeleep’s courageous opposition to tyranny.

The narrator is omniscient, intrusive, authoritative, and openly judgmental. Historical exposition is repeatedly accompanied by moral interpretation, as in comments about what can or cannot justify taking arms against one’s country, the obligations of rulers and clergy, and the spiritual meaning of suffering. The narrator also controls the reader’s emotional response through exclamations and evaluative labels such as “good,” “gentle,” “wicked,” “cruel,” “gallant,” “dauntless,” and “venerable.” Retrospective interventions—“this story has only been told at so much length,” “perhaps it has been lingered over,” and “This has been a horrible story”—make the act of storytelling visible. Such remarks give the voice the quality of a cultivated lecturer or moral historian addressing a general, possibly youthful, audience.

The diction is elevated, formal, and markedly archaic. Words and constructions such as “tidings,” “aught,” “whence,” “thereupon,” “ere yet,” “beheld,” “besought,” “thine,” and “among his dominions” create historical distance and scriptural gravity. Titles, foreign terms, and explanatory glosses—“boyards,” “condottieri,” “Strelitzes,” “Oprichnina,” “muezzins,” and “Moslemin”—lend the narrative an antiquarian texture. The author often explains unfamiliar customs, institutions, siege techniques, and religious architecture, making the prose partly instructional. Period spellings and transliterations such as “Tzar,” “Feeleep,” “Moskwa,” and “Mahometans” further mark the text as belonging to an older English historiographical tradition.

The syntax alternates between long, heavily coordinated explanatory sentences and short dramatic declarations. Expository passages accumulate subordinate clauses, qualifications, and causal links, producing a measured, didactic rhythm. Battle scenes become more immediate through compressed sentences and parallel phrasing: the city is won “step by step, house by house, street by street,” while the armies stand in poised opposition—Tatars on the walls, Russians in the trenches, archers with arrows ready, gunners with lighted matches. Semicolons, dashes, and colons frequently organize elaborate sentences, and exclamation marks intensify moments of lament, admiration, or moral rebuke.

Description is selective but vivid, especially when spectacle supports emotional or symbolic meaning. The Volga meadows spread “like a brilliant green carpet”; copper-clad minarets glow in the sunrise; smoke rolls over the city; gilded armor and streaming plumes embody the brilliance of the victorious cavalry. Sound is equally important: trumpets, drums, hymns, chants, explosions, battle cries, bells, shrieks, and prayers create ceremonial and martial atmosphere. The dawn assault on Kazan is the most cinematic passage, built through silence, suspended expectation, religious observance, sunrise, and sudden thunderous detonation. Similes are generally clear and conventional rather than densely poetic, while metaphor becomes stronger in moral passages: Ivan’s reign is a “long tempestuous day,” his better nature a “sleeping lion,” ecclesiastical office a “heavy freight” entrusted to a “feeble bark,” and goodness shines like diamonds in the “blackest night.”

Dialogue is formal, compressed, and oratorical. Characters speak less as psychologically naturalistic individuals than as representatives of courage, loyalty, faith, or tyranny. Ivan’s command concerning willing and unwilling warriors demonstrates princely resolve; the Tatars’ speeches express collective defiance; Vasili’s few words embody feudal fidelity; and Feeleep’s denunciations resemble sermons or courtroom testimony. Antithesis and parallelism give these speeches a memorable, exemplary quality: earthly power is opposed to divine judgment, military triumph to the “victories of love,” and fear of the Tzar to fear of God. The dialogue often reads like preserved historical quotation, though it is shaped for dramatic and moral effect.

Characterization is strongly ethical and contrastive. Ivan is presented through a fall-from-grace pattern: initially gallant, gifted, religious, generous, and merciful, he becomes suspicious, fanatical, and brutally tyrannical. The narrator allows some complexity by observing that his savagery coexists with political acuteness and concern for national improvement, but his inner life is chiefly explained through heredity, upbringing, illness, bereavement, corrupt advisers, and the loss of his wife’s moderating influence. Kourbsky is a brave but morally compromised noble; Vasili and Feeleep, by contrast, are figures of near-unmixed constancy. Their willingness to endure torture or death transforms them into secular and religious martyrs. The Tatars are also granted courage and tragic dignity during the siege, even though the narrator uses dated, prejudicial language when discussing ethnic and religious groups.

The pacing is broad and uneven by modern fictional standards. Long stretches summarize decades, explain institutions, or assess motives, while a few crucial episodes unfold scenically: the assault on Kazan, Vasili’s delivery of the letter, Feeleep’s confrontation with Ivan in the cathedral, his degradation during the liturgy, and his murder in confinement. Transitions such as “On his way,” “about this time,” “Too soon,” “After a time,” and “Once again” preserve the forward motion of a historical chronicle. Suspense usually arises not from uncertainty about outcomes but from the moral testing of a character whose likely suffering has already been signalled.

The dominant themes are loyalty, conscience, righteous resistance, corrupted power, religious faith, and moral courage under tyranny. Loyalty is treated with nuance: Kourbsky’s allegiance to Ivan and Russia breaks down, while Vasili’s personal allegiance to Kourbsky remains absolute. Feeleep represents a higher loyalty to God and pastoral duty, which obliges him to oppose the sovereign. Christianity supplies both the story’s ethical framework and its final consolation. Ceremonial religion can be corrupted into Ivan’s “foolish fanatic observance,” but genuine faith produces mildness, self-sacrifice, truthfulness, and fearlessness. The conclusion explicitly states the governing design: atrocity forms a dark background against which virtue shines more brilliantly.

The emotional register is grave, admiring, indignant, and elegiac. The narrator mourns fallen nations, betrayed promise, civic suffering, and the transformation of a capable ruler into a monster. At the same time, the prose repeatedly rises into heroic celebration when individuals choose honor or conscience over safety. Violence is described vividly enough to inspire horror but seldom with prolonged physical detail; its function is to test and illuminate moral character. Overall, the style is that of a Christian historical romance or instructive chronicle: expansive in background, ceremonially dramatic in key scenes, explicit in judgment, and designed to preserve examples of courage amid political and spiritual darkness.

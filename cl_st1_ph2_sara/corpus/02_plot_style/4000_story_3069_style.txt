The story has the manner of an Edwardian social comedy: urbane, polished and outwardly decorous, but driven by a coldly mischievous view of human motives. Its principal register is formal upper-class English, enriched by period slang and fashionable references such as “top-hole,” racing meetings, Continental resorts, casinos and expensive food and wine. Elevated phrasing is repeatedly applied to selfish or ridiculous behaviour, creating comic disproportion. Euphemisms such as “good money,” “collective wings” and “no useful purpose would be served” expose the nieces’ greed and social calculation while preserving their veneer of respectability.

The narrative is framed as a conversation between Clovis and the Baroness, with Clovis telling an extended anecdote prompted by the sight of three dejected women in the Park. This structure establishes a knowing, gossip-like intimacy and allows the tale to unfold as an elegant verbal performance rather than as direct dramatic narration. Occasional interruptions from the Baroness regulate the pace, while Clovis’s asides—his clarification of pronouns, his imperfect recollection of a proverb, and his appeals to “you”—give the account a conversational spontaneity. At the same time, the chain of reported confidences and second-hand witnesses playfully mocks the unreliable but socially efficient circulation of gossip.

Dialogue is crisp, poised and epigrammatic. Characters rarely state their real motives openly; instead, those motives emerge through implication, circumlocution and accidental self-revelation. The nieces disguise their concern for an inheritance as moral anxiety over waste and solicitude for their aunt, while the aunt’s apparently innocent remarks gradually reveal independence, appetite and stubbornness. Clovis and the Baroness exchange compact paradoxes and polished insults, as in the comparison between looking depressed and looking one’s age. The best lines often balance clauses symmetrically before ending in an unexpected qualification or reversal.

The dominant comic technique is irony. The nieces seek to expose Roger’s gambling and thereby protect their expected inheritance, but their scheme introduces the aunt herself to gambling and accelerates the dissipation of her fortune. Nearly every stage reverses their intentions: she enjoys the Channel crossing while they suffer, speaks better French, resists their supervision, wins her first bet and eventually dismisses her supposed protectors with cheerful ease. The narration maintains a dry, detached tone, treating emotional and financial disaster as material for aesthetic amusement. Even the aunt’s decline is described without sentimentality; the emphasis remains on the poetic justice inflicted upon the acquisitive nieces.

The humour also depends heavily on figurative comparison. The nieces’ attentions become the multiple wings of a creature from Revelation; their helplessness resembles chickens watching a duck in dangerous water; their loyalty resembles a crew refusing to abandon a foundering treasure ship; and their return becomes a retreat from Moscow whose Moscow is merely “extravagantly over-illuminated.” These analogies are elaborate, unexpected and faintly cruel. They enlarge trivial social predicaments into biblical, military or historical catastrophes, while the bathos of the actual circumstances sharpens the satire.

Sentences are often long and syntactically layered, with subordinate clauses, qualifications and parenthetical remarks that postpone the comic point. The prose is controlled rather than ornate for its own sake: accumulation builds narrative momentum, and the final phrase of a sentence frequently supplies the sting. Short dialogue exchanges then puncture the longer narration. Repetition is used structurally and satirically, particularly in references to “good money,” “collective” behaviour, headaches and protective wings. These recurring expressions become motifs that reveal the nieces’ shared mentality and steadily undermine their stated concern.

Characterisation is economical and behavioural. The three sisters function largely as a collective social type, distinguished less by psychological depth than by degrees of dismay; even Veronique’s individuality rests chiefly on having proposed the disastrous plan. The aunt develops through action, moving from apparently manageable dependent to energetic pleasure-seeker. Roger remains the alleged “rotter” but behaves with comparative generosity and good humour, another reversal of the nieces’ moral classification. Clovis himself is characterised by amused omniscience, verbal precision and indifference to conventional sympathy.

The plot follows a clean comic escalation. An initial social observation leads to background exposition, the nieces formulate their scheme, the first casino visit produces an ominously successful bet, and progressively larger losses destroy their hopes. Each development intensifies the same central irony without diverting into secondary plots. The ending returns neatly to the framing scene and identifies Veronique as the most depressed sister, converting the entire narrative into the explanation of a facial expression. This circular closure delivers a final, understated punch line.

Overall, the style is witty, aphoristic, socially observant and unsentimentally satirical. It treats greed, hypocrisy, respectability and pleasure through elegant indirection, preferring implication to denunciation. The moral logic is exact but not solemn: schemers are punished by the success of their own scheme, and liberation arrives through vice rather than virtue. The result is a compact comedy of manners in which polished language, narrative reversal and precise cruelty are inseparable.

The story is a comic domestic adventure told by an assured third-person narrator who stays close to William’s point of view while retaining an adult awareness of his mistakes. This dual perspective is central to the style: William’s fantasies and deductions are presented with enough sympathy to seem internally reasonable, but the narrator’s dry phrasing exposes their absurdity. The tone is affectionate rather than cruel. William is troublesome, self-dramatising and opportunistic, yet his imaginative energy makes him more appealing than the exasperated adults who judge only the resulting chaos.

The humour relies heavily on irony, incongruity and escalation. Elevated language is repeatedly applied to trivial childish concerns: choosing cheap sweets requires “deep calculations,” shopkeepers are judged through a rigorous system of patronage and boycott, and a dirty bag of confectionery becomes a precious possession. Mock-heroic comparisons, such as William adopting “the air of a Rothschild,” magnify his self-importance. Comic effects also arise from the gap between intention and consequence. William believes he is behaving like a pursued criminal, a rustic lover, a stoical invalid, a romantic mediator and a scientific experimenter; everyone else sees assault, illness, improper interference and vandalism. Each new misunderstanding grows naturally out of something he has watched at the cinema, giving the episode a tightly linked chain of cause and effect.

The narrator’s irony is often compressed into balanced, deadpan statements. Sentences such as the observation that William consumed sweets and garden soil “in fairly equal proportions” report absurdity with formal calm. Similar understatement governs the parodies of the films. Their clichés are anatomised through precise, exaggerated description: criminals behave in ways “guaranteed to attract attention,” a villain is identified by his moustache, emotions demand “gymnastic skill,” and melodramatic suffering becomes so energetic that a relapse is scarcely surprising. Repetition intensifies the satire, especially in phrases like “simple country maiden” and “simple country son of the soil.” The prose thus mocks early screen conventions without interrupting the story’s momentum.

The diction alternates between polished, sometimes ceremonious narration and strongly colloquial speech. The narrative voice uses elaborate vocabulary—“ostentatiously,” “solicitude,” “misconstrued,” “systematic”—to frame childish behaviour with comic grandeur. William’s dialogue, by contrast, is rendered phonetically and idiomatically through forms such as “jus’,” “gotter,” “p’raps,” “kin’ly,” “pertic-ler” and “mornin’.” His speech is repetitive, argumentative and relentlessly explanatory. He rarely accepts correction; instead, he redefines events until he appears innocent or heroic. Adult dialogue is more standard but equally characterful. Mr Brown’s speech consists of explosions, rhetorical questions, repetitions and insults, while Mrs Brown’s mild, qualifying language acts as a domestic counterweight. Ethel and Mr Morgan speak in conventional polite formulas, and their awkward pauses turn social restraint into comedy.

Sentence rhythm varies according to the action. Long, cumulative sentences recreate the breathless excess of films, pursuits, emotional performances and parental complaints. Short sentences and fragments—particularly “Silence” and abrupt exchanges such as “Be quiet!”—control comic timing. Lists are another prominent device. William’s pocket contents, the stages of cinematic action and the father’s catalogue of offences all accumulate detail until the situation becomes ridiculous. Parenthetical qualifications and afterthoughts sharpen the narrator’s wit, while em dashes mark hesitation, interruption, melodramatic emphasis and sudden changes of direction.

Characterisation is achieved primarily through behaviour, speech and recurring verbal habits rather than psychological exposition. William is defined by his solemn concentration, physical appetite, literal imitation and capacity to convert scraps of observation into complete narratives. His imagination is theatrical: he changes his voice, posture and expression to suit each role, but he lacks the social knowledge needed to understand the original scenes. Mr Brown is characterised by bodily discomfort and verbal overreaction; his headache, digestion and injured dignity amplify every disturbance. Mrs Brown remains restrained and practical, while Joan’s credulity and Mr Morgan’s embarrassment allow William’s inventions to flourish.

Structurally, the story begins with a simple enabling incident—the gift of a shilling—then moves through purchase, cinema visit, imitation and domestic reckoning. The cinema programme functions as a set of motifs that are replayed in ordinary life: the crime film produces the chase home, the rustic romance shapes William’s exchange with Joan, the restrained lovers inspire his intervention with Mr Morgan, the painting comedy leads to the damaged door, and the temperance melodrama supplies the final vision of himself as a suffering child. This patterned recurrence gives the apparently episodic narrative strong unity. Transitions are smooth and often triggered by associative thought, so that one remembered image generates the next disaster.

The ending uses circularity and delayed recognition. William reviews the day as a series of noble acts unfairly misunderstood, just as his father reviews the same events as evidence of insanity. Neither possesses the whole explanation, although the reader does. William’s attempted deathbed-style forgiveness repeats the language of the final film and provides a last comic collision between melodrama and family reality. His decision to flee before finishing the line preserves his essential mixture of theatricality and practical instinct.

Overall, the style combines Edwardian or early twentieth-century domestic comedy, social observation, cinematic parody and child-centred farce. Its signature qualities are urbane narrative irony, affectionate treatment of mischief, formal prose set against colloquial dialogue, escalating misunderstandings, carefully timed repetition and a persistent contrast between William’s heroic self-conception and the mundane damage visible to everyone around him.

While spending a summer on Martha’s Vineyard, the narrator befriends Mr. Wigglesworth, a cheerful, simple-minded tombstone carver who has come to the island because its inhabitants import memorial stones for their deeply remembered dead. The narrator visits his workshop daily, observing customers whose requests reveal varied forms of grief, love, vanity, resentment, faith, and remembrance.

Among them are an elderly woman memorializing a lover killed by a whale forty years earlier; a man ordering identical stones for three former wives; a whaling captain preparing a shared monument for himself and his dead wife; a woman whose supposedly drowned husband returns while his epitaph is being carved; a mother and surviving twin choosing a conventional verse for the dead child; a lifelong enemy arranging to be buried beside the man he hated; and a miser commissioning an extravagant monument because no one else will remember him. A sickly young woman even chooses her own stone shortly before dying.

These encounters lead the narrator to reflect on how monuments embody both genuine affection and human self-deception. When Mr. Wigglesworth asks which stone he would choose for himself, the narrator rejects gravestones altogether, arguing that they bind thoughts of the dead to decaying bodies rather than liberated souls. The sculptor is disturbed because this view challenges his life’s work. The narrator leaves their companionship wiser but uncertain, wondering whether sorrow and regret may offer as much consolation as life’s joys.

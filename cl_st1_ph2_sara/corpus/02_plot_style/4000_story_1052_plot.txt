On a stormy New Year’s Eve, a narrator sits by the fire recalling accounts of explorers, mutineers, escaped convicts, and shipwrecks. His thoughts dwell on a cannibalistic convict who escapes twice, the mutiny on the *Bounty*, and the destruction of an East Indiaman whose passengers—including its captain and his daughters—perish when the wreck breaks apart.

He then remembers another shipwreck in which survivors undertake a desperate journey through a hostile wilderness. They make a seven-year-old orphan their sacred responsibility, carrying, feeding, and protecting him despite hunger, illness, wild animals, and repeated deaths within the group. The child eventually dies beside a fading fire, and the devoted steward who cared for him dies soon afterward.

These tales lead the narrator to imagine life’s final journey: death, an exile from which no one returns. He reflects on a dying person tormented by neglected duties, unreturned love, unforgiven injuries, and kindnesses left undone. Recognizing that passing years inevitably carry everyone toward the same voyage, he resolves that one must make amends and act lovingly before it is too late.

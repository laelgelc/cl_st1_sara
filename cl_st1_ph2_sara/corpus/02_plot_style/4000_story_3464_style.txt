The story adopts a nineteenth-century sentimental-reform style, combining domestic realism, moral allegory, patriotic rhetoric, and direct political denunciation. Its governing device is a sharply constructed historical parallel: two domestic “pictures,” one set during the Revolution and the other in 1850, are linked by the recurring image of an altar. In the first, sacrifice for liberty is voluntary, noble, and communal; in the second, a Black man and his family are involuntarily sacrificed to preserve the Union and slavery. This symmetrical structure turns an initially playful metaphor into an accusation of national hypocrisy. Repetition of the words “altar,” “liberty,” “sacrifice,” and “offering” supplies the narrative with symbolic unity, while the contrast between 1776 and 1850 creates an increasingly bitter irony.

The narrative voice is omniscient, conspicuous, and morally authoritative. It moves freely between scenic description, domestic observation, historical explanation, summary, and editorial commentary. At first the narrator is genial and companionable, making humorous asides such as the observation that gathering chips was considered a “wholesome and gracious employment” for children. Later, this geniality gives way to indignation. Rhetorical questions, apostrophe, exclamation, and direct address draw the reader into moral judgment: “Ah, ye who defend this because it is law” openly challenges supporters of the system. The narrator does not aspire to neutrality; facts and scenes are arranged to awaken sympathy, shame, and outrage.

The prose alternates between richly descriptive passages and rapid, dialogue-led scenes. The opening landscape is painterly and sensuous, with snow rendered in crimson, purple, and lilac beneath a “brisk, clear, metallic” winter sky. Nature is frequently personified: the wind is a “roguish fellow,” while kettles sing and flags snap. Domestic scenes depend on precise, tactile objects—blankets, stockings, caps, biscuits, coins, ironing, a china teapot—which make political principles materially immediate. Liberty is not treated as an abstraction alone; it is measured in cold hands, worn clothing, household savings, family separation, and bodily ownership.

Sentence structure varies with purpose. Descriptive and polemical passages often use long, cumulative sentences joined by semicolons, dashes, repeated conjunctions, and catalogues. These accretive constructions allow details to gather emotional force, especially in the slave-market scene, where mothers, children, husbands, wives, politicians, lawyers, and religious men are assembled into one panoramic indictment. Short sentences and fragments punctuate moments of shock or irony: “The sale of a man!” and “for life?—no; but for the life of life—for liberty!” Parallel phrasing, antithesis, strategic repetition, and climactic punctuation give the prose an oratorical, sermon-like cadence.

Dialogue is central to characterization and social texture. Much of it is written in colloquial period speech, with contractions and dialect spellings such as “airth,” “s’pose,” “’spect,” “mas’r,” and “gettin’.” Characters emerge through habitual verbal patterns: Aunt Hitty is bustling, skeptical, practical, and comic; Dick is boastful and imaginatively patriotic; Grace is innocent and tender-hearted; the mothers speak with quiet moral seriousness. The Black family in the second half is represented through affectionate domestic banter, religious devotion, pride in work, and aspirations for education and respectability. This deliberate ordinariness establishes their home as recognizably loving and virtuous before legal violence destroys it.

The tone undergoes a controlled progression. The first section is warm, nostalgic, humorous, and patriotic, though touched by the hardships of Valley Forge. The second initially mirrors that warmth in another modest but happy household, then turns abruptly toward terror, grief, sarcasm, and prophetic anger. The transition from hearthside comedy to arrest, courtroom, and auction block is designed as an emotional shock. Irony becomes especially savage in phrases such as “edifying,” “truly American spectacle,” and “noble maintaining of principle,” where the elevated language of patriotism is applied to brutal acts in order to expose their obscenity.

Sentiment is the story’s principal persuasive method. Children, mothers, domestic labor, prayer, sacrifice, and family affection are used to translate public policy into private suffering. Tears and gestures are carefully staged, but the emotional appeal is reinforced by structural argument: the same nation that revered sacrifice for freedom in 1776 sanctions the sacrifice of freedom in 1850. Biblical diction deepens this argument. The “altar,” the sacrificial victim “bound to the horns,” the “city set on a hill,” the shepherd psalm, and the scattering of families “like the chaff of the threshing floor” frame slavery as both a civic betrayal and a religious sin.

Characterization is broad and emblematic rather than psychologically intricate. Individuals represent moral positions, social roles, or national types: the patriotic household, the innocent child, the self-sacrificing mother, the industrious fugitive, the complacent statesman, and the glib legal functionary. Yet concrete habits and objects keep these figures from becoming entirely abstract. The narrative’s melodramatic polarization—innocent victims against comfortable collaborators—is intentional, serving the reformist aim of making complicity unmistakable.

Overall, the style is vivid, accessible, theatrical, and openly didactic. It blends humor and pathos, domestic detail and public oratory, Christian symbolism and political satire. Its most distinctive effect comes from turning familiar patriotic language against the nation that speaks it: “liberty” begins as an inspiring ideal, is exposed as a cruelly selective fiction, and finally becomes the name of the very altar on which a man’s “heart, his hopes, his manhood” are destroyed.

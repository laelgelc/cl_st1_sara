The story adopts an epistolary, mock-antiquarian form, presenting itself as a respectful letter to the editor from the ceremoniously named “Hermanus Vanderdonk.” Its first-person narrator poses as a diligent local historian compiling “legendary and statistical” particulars, but his solemn confidence repeatedly authenticates absurd events. This tension between scholarly manner and fabulous content is the central stylistic device. References to tradition, relics, archives, historical authorities and eyewitness travellers give the narrative a veneer of documentary reliability, while exaggerated claims, invented miracles and playful analogies expose the account as comic pseudo-history.

The tone is urbane, genial and ceremonially pompous. Formal addresses such as “Sir,” deferential qualifications and elaborate expressions of respect imitate nineteenth-century public correspondence. The narrator treats trivial local customs with epic seriousness: Communipaw becomes the parent of New York, Garret Van Horne is compared with Pelayo, resistance to changing fashions becomes heroic patriotism, and cabbages and oysters acquire sacred genealogies. The humour is predominantly deadpan. Rather than signalling jokes directly, the narrator maintains an air of reverence and factual precision, allowing the disproportion between tone and subject to produce the comedy.

The diction is elevated, antiquarian and deliberately archaic. Latinate words and phrases—“fructification,” “asperity,” “domination,” “reliques,” “stercoraceous,” “mystic signification”—stand beside homely objects such as pipes, breeches, stocking-feet, cabbages and oysters. This mixture creates comic incongruity while evoking an old-fashioned learned essay. Dutch terms and names, including “vrouw,” “Paas,” “Pinxter,” “Hogen Mogens” and “New-Netherlands,” supply local colour and reinforce the imagined cultural continuity of the settlement. Period spellings and typography, such as “New-York,” “every thing,” “strong-hold” and emphatic capitals, further imitate an early nineteenth-century printed text.

Sentences tend to be long, balanced and syntactically elaborate, with multiple subordinate clauses, parenthetical qualifications, semicolons and cumulative lists. The prose often begins with a factual proposition, expands through historical analogy, and culminates in a comic image or inflated conclusion. Repetition is used both rhythmically and satirically, as in the inhabitants’ “smoking and smoking, and watching and watching,” which reduces political deliberation to stagnant habit. Parallel constructions and catalogues convey abundance: clothing, architecture, furniture, customs and relics are itemised as evidence in the narrator’s cultural inventory. Occasional short declarations—“But mark the consequence” or “These miracles were the salvation of Communipaw”—punctuate the expansive prose and sharpen its mock-epic drama.

Imagery is concrete, picturesque and caricatural. Communipaw is rendered through red roofs, weathercocks, apple orchards, cabbage gardens, peaked gables, sunny stoops, pipes, cats and domestic interiors. These warmly observed details create nostalgia for a stable Dutch village, even as that nostalgia is gently mocked. Similes are especially comic and visual: the village beside New York resembles a “squat little dame” with a grenadier son or a hen that has hatched a long-legged turkey. Transformations of Walter the Doubter’s hat into a giant cabbage and Peter Stuyvesant’s shoe into an oyster literalise historical inheritance as grotesque folk mythology.

The narrative structure resembles a local legend embedded within a historical essay. It begins by establishing Communipaw’s neglected significance, describes its preserved customs, explains that preservation through a tale of post-conquest isolation, introduces the miracles that sustain the community, and then carries the conflict into the modern age through satire of Yankee expansion and land speculation. The closing return to the House of the Four Chimneys gives the piece a symbolic centre. Its decay represents cultural erosion, while the narrator’s final blessings parody biblical benediction, patriotic oratory and family chronicle at once.

Characterisation is communal and typological rather than psychologically deep. Dutch burghers are broad, slow, loyal, pipe-smoking and resistant to change; Yankees are mobile, commercial, speculative and aggressively modernising. Individuals are defined through emblematic names and traits—Walter the Doubter, Peter the Headstrong, Oloffe the Dreamer—and function like figures in comic folklore. The satire depends on stereotype, but its targets are distributed: Dutch inertia and credulity are mocked alongside Yankee acquisitiveness, speculative capitalism and architectural pretension.

The story’s thematic style rests on contrasts between old and new, local memory and metropolitan growth, inherited custom and commercial innovation, rootedness and restless speculation. The narrator outwardly celebrates cultural purity, yet the extravagant praise makes such purity appear both charming and absurd. New York’s modernity is similarly double-edged: energetic and expansive, but destructive of historical character. The result is a nostalgic satire rather than a simple elegy. It preserves the atmosphere of the vanished Dutch world by turning that world into affectionate legend.

Overall, the prose is best characterised as mock-heroic, pseudo-historical, folkloric and gently satirical. Its signature features are an unreliable but authoritative antiquarian narrator, sustained deadpan exaggeration, ceremonious diction, long periodic sentences, learned allusion, detailed local colour, comic catalogues, grotesque origin myths and affectionate irony toward both ancestral tradition and modern progress.

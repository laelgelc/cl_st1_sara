The story adopts the style of a traditional didactic animal tale for children, combining pastoral realism with the moral structure of a fable. Its tone is warm, gentle, and reassuring, even when the narrative introduces fear, separation, or parental sorrow. The sheep and lambs are anthropomorphised: they speak, reason, feel guilt, and understand family relationships like human beings, while retaining recognisable animal behaviours such as bleating, frisking, bunting, huddling, and nursing. Practical details about bottle-feeding, sheep shearing, wool, and flock behaviour give the tale an instructional natural-history element.

The narrator is omniscient and openly authoritative. It moves freely between the thoughts and emotions of the brother, his mother, and his sister, while also supplying information unavailable to the animals. The narrative voice frequently interprets events for the reader through general moral observations, such as the idea that impatient people say things they do not mean or that selfishness prevents genuine love. Inclusive phrases such as “we know” create the impression of an adult storyteller guiding a child’s understanding. The closing appeal to keep the brother’s secret addresses the reader directly and ends the lesson with playful intimacy.

The diction is simple, affectionate, and somewhat old-fashioned. Capitalised animal names such as “Lamb,” “Sheep,” and “Wether Sheep” lend the characters the status of types in a moral tale. Endearments and tactile descriptions—“dear woolly mother,” “hard little forehead,” “pinkish-white noses,” and “sad little face”—make the pastoral setting emotionally accessible. Evaluative words such as “selfish,” “naughty,” “good,” “loving,” and “unselfish” state the ethical distinctions plainly, leaving little ambiguity about desirable behaviour. Euphemistic or gentle phrasing keeps distress suitable for young readers.

The prose largely uses clear chronological narration, moderately long compound sentences, and repeated coordinating conjunctions. Repetition is prominent and often mimics childish thought or animal sound: the lambs “bleated and bleated and bleated,” the shears make a “snip-snip-snipping” sound, and anxious questions are repeated. Parallel constructions and incremental lists create a steady oral-storytelling rhythm. Exclamation marks, rhetorical questions, and brief emphatic declarations give the dialogue liveliness, while diminutives and intensifiers such as “little,” “very,” “ever so much,” and “long, long” reproduce the emotional scale of childhood.

Dialogue is direct, uncomplicated, and strongly characterising. The brother’s speech reveals entitlement, impatience, fear, remorse, and finally generosity; the mother speaks with patient moral authority; and the sister’s brief remarks establish her innocence. The mother’s lessons are embedded in affectionate conversation rather than delivered as punishment. Internal conflict is simplified into a contest between personified “selfishness” and “goodness,” making the brother’s moral choice concrete and easy for a child to grasp.

The plot is structured around moral testing and reform. Early episodes establish the brother’s selfish desire to remove his sister, after which his careless words wound his mother. The shearing sequence supplies suspense through limited animal understanding: ordinary farm activity appears ominous because the lambs do not know what the men are doing. This fear gives the brother an experience of possible loss, preparing him to empathise with others. When shearing temporarily prevents the sister from recognising their mother, the earlier temptation returns in a more actionable form. His decision to reunite them constitutes the climax and proves his change through conduct rather than words.

Pacing is initially leisurely and explanatory, slows further during the enclosed, anxious night and the sequential removal of the sheep, then quickens as the lamb recognises the shorn flock and races to help his sister. Foreshadowing is explicit: the account of sheep forgetting one another explains in advance why a brief separation matters, while the brother’s wish to send his sister to the pen anticipates his final temptation. The contrast between the mother’s heavy, dingy fleece and her “new” white appearance also supports a motif of renewal that parallels the brother’s moral improvement.

The central themes are sibling jealousy, maternal love, empathy, repentance, and the enlargement of affection through unselfish action. Love is presented not as something proved by emotional declarations but as something demonstrated through care. The sister embodies instinctive goodness, whereas the brother undergoes a recognisable conversion from self-centred desire to compassionate choice. The ending remains optimistic without claiming that he has become permanently perfect: his admission that goodness “may not last long” gives his reform a modest psychological credibility. Overall, the style is tender, explicitly moralising, suspenseful without being harsh, and carefully shaped for oral reading to young children.

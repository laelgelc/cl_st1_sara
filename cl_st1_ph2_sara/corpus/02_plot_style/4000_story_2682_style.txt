The story uses a close third-person perspective that is almost entirely attached to Mr. Hammond’s consciousness. Its most distinctive technique is free indirect discourse: the narration repeatedly slips into his idiom, assumptions and emotional reflexes without formally announcing that he is thinking. Exclamations such as “By Jove!”, “Hang the children!” and “what a fool” allow his private voice to colour the ostensibly external narration. This produces strong psychological intimacy while preserving enough narrative distance for irony. Readers experience Hammond’s excitement, possessiveness and anxiety from within, yet can also perceive what he cannot: that his ideal of complete marital union is both impossible and oppressive.

The prose alternates between precise visual observation and agitated subjective impression. The opening renders the liner from shore through miniaturising metaphors: passengers become “flies,” a figure on a ladder becomes a “tiny black spider,” and the water resembles a “grey crinkled tablecloth.” Such imagery establishes physical distance while anticipating the deeper emotional distance between husband and wife. Scale is repeatedly manipulated. The ship, hotel room, bed and chair become immense, while Janey appears “small,” “little,” delicate and liable to “fly away.” These contrasts externalise Hammond’s state of mind: the world expands threateningly whenever he feels unable to possess or protect her.

The diction is concrete, sensuous and often domestic. Colours, textures, garments, luggage, tea things, hotel furnishings and small bodily gestures receive close attention. Everyday objects carry psychological weight: Hammond’s watch embodies his impatience and compulsion; his umbrella conducts his nervous energy; the children’s letters become intrusive presences; the dying fire reflects the cooling of his anticipated reunion; and the luggage, suddenly seeming ready to be carried away again, suggests instability and separation. Objects are rarely merely decorative. They become emotional correlatives that disclose fear, desire or alienation without explicit analysis.

Sentence structure is highly responsive to mood. Long, flowing descriptive sentences establish the harbour scene, while Hammond’s excitement generates fragments, repetitions, dashes, parenthetical interruptions and exclamations. Phrases such as “slowly, slowly,” “thank God, thank God,” “yes, yes, yes,” and “never—never once” mimic obsessive thought and emotional pressure. Ellipses and unfinished constructions mark what cannot be comfortably articulated. At the climax, syntax becomes broken and breathless as Hammond struggles to absorb Janey’s revelation; thereafter, the language slows into cold, suspended images. Rhythm therefore functions psychologically, moving from restless anticipation through temporary release to shock and paralysis.

Dialogue is naturalistic but strongly characterising. Hammond speaks rapidly, expansively and repetitively, using jokes, hearty clichés, commands and rhetorical questions. His sociability “ropes in” strangers, but it is also a way of managing anxiety. Janey’s speech is quieter, lighter and more controlled. Her affectionate terms—“darling,” “dear”—do not necessarily convey the intensity Hammond craves; instead, her composure makes her emotionally unreadable to him. The contrast between his noisy need and her restrained manner creates the story’s central tension. Small discrepancies in conversation matter greatly: she wants the children’s letters, to thank the captain and to say goodbye to the doctor, while he continually tries to exclude other people and secure her undivided attention.

The tone combines tenderness, comedy, irony and gathering unease. Hammond is initially comic in his bustling impatience, conspicuous watch-checking, exaggerated generosity and irritation with every interruption. Yet the narration treats him with sympathy rather than ridicule. His possessiveness grows from genuine love and vulnerability, even as it becomes disturbing. The recurrent image of Janey as something precious that might escape reveals both devotion and a desire to absorb her individuality. This emotional doubleness prevents the portrait from becoming either sentimental or satirical.

The narrative is organised around frustrated arrival. The liner appears motionless; docking is delayed; the gangway, farewells, luggage and hotel staff repeatedly postpone privacy. Each apparent threshold—ship to shore, public to private, separation to reunion—fails to produce the complete intimacy Hammond expects. His refrain that they will finally be “alone together” becomes increasingly ironic. Even after the door closes, other people persist through luggage, letters, memories and, ultimately, the dead passenger. The plot is therefore less a sequence of external events than a tightening psychological pattern in which interruption becomes revelation.

Foreshadowing is subtle but pervasive. The doctor’s prolonged absence, Janey’s private farewell to him, her unusual calm, Hammond’s sense that “the danger was over,” and his fear that she is keeping an illness from him all prepare for the final disclosure without making its form predictable. Images of flocking, grasping, shepherding and holding also anticipate his desperate wish to contain Janey. Meanwhile, references to cold, darkness and distance gradually displace the warmth associated with tea, fire and reunion.

The climax depends on understatement. Janey’s statement that the passenger “died in my arms” is delivered in a “small voice,” and the death itself is described through a gentle lifted hand and the phrase “breathed his life away.” The restraint magnifies Hammond’s reaction. For Janey, the event is an act of humane care and has “nothing to do with” their marriage; for Hammond, it constitutes an intolerable intimacy granted to another man. The story does not confirm any romantic attachment. Its force comes from the discrepancy between the objective innocence of the event and the catastrophic meaning Hammond assigns to it.

The ending is abrupt, epiphanic and unresolved. Hammond hides his face in the very bosom that comforted the dying stranger, physically embracing Janey while inwardly recognising an unbridgeable separation. The final assertion that they will “never be alone together again” reverses the entire movement of the story. External reunion exposes internal distance; possession gives way to the knowledge that another person’s experience can never be wholly shared or controlled. Overall, the style is impressionistic, psychologically acute and symbolically economical, using free indirect narration, rhythmic repetition, charged objects and understated revelation to turn an ordinary homecoming into a study of love, jealousy and the irreducible privacy of the self.

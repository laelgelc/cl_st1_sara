The story’s style is modernist, impressionistic, and psychologically intimate. Its third-person narration remains closely bound to Bertha’s consciousness, frequently slipping into free indirect discourse so that narrator and character become almost indistinguishable. Questions, exclamations, repetitions, and self-corrections reproduce thought as it occurs: excited, fragmentary, and unstable. Rather than explaining Bertha’s emotions from a detached position, the prose lets her heightened perceptions determine the shape and rhythm of the narrative. This narrow focalisation also creates dramatic irony, since the reader inhabits Bertha’s confident interpretations while gradually sensing how unreliable they may be.

The syntax alternates between long, flowing accumulations and abrupt emotional bursts. Lists convey the abundance of Bertha’s life and the overflowing quality of her happiness, while sentence fragments and emphatic repetitions—“too happy,” “ardently,” “pear tree”—register moments when feeling exceeds orderly expression. Parentheses, dashes, ellipses, and italic-like verbal emphasis give the prose a spontaneous, breathless movement. Repeated self-interruptions and qualifications reveal a mind continually revising its own metaphors and explanations. The result is lyrical but also conversational, poised between refined narration and the impulsive idiom of private thought.

Sensory imagery is unusually dense. Colour, light, texture, smell, temperature, and bodily sensation carry much of the psychological meaning: silver, jade, purple, red, white, moonlight, fire, fruit, flowers, silk, and cold air recur throughout. Objects are arranged almost painterly, with interiors and people treated as decorative compositions. Similes are vivid and sometimes deliberately fanciful, making dishes appear to float, fire resemble “baby phoenixes,” and clothing turn its wearer into a monkey or a flower. This aesthetic richness reflects Bertha’s tendency to transform ordinary life into a beautiful tableau, but it also exposes the superficiality and fragility of her vision.

The dominant symbol is the flowering pear tree. Perfect, still, luminous, and apparently untouched by decay, it embodies Bertha’s idealised image of herself, her marriage, her sexuality, and her imagined communion with Pearl. Its upward, flame-like form links it to the “fire” in her body, while its silver blossoms connect it visually with Pearl and the moon. Other motifs quietly complicate this perfection. The paired grey and black cats foreshadow the secret relation between Pearl and Harry, and their final correspondence with Pearl and Eddie gives the image retrospective force. Fire, fruit, flowers, and bodily appetite create a network of sensual suggestion that Bertha initially misreads as generalized spiritual bliss.

Dialogue is sharply characterised and socially satirical. The dinner guests speak in fashionable paradoxes, aesthetic poses, exaggerated epigrams, and self-conscious modernisms. Their nicknames, artistic schemes, theatrical projects, and absurd literary remarks produce brittle comedy. Much of their conversation consists of clever performance rather than genuine communication, and its rapid collage-like presentation makes the gathering feel animated yet emotionally hollow. Harry’s clipped, physical, often coarse idiom contrasts with Eddie’s precious morbidity and the Norman Knights’ theatrical eccentricity. Pearl, by contrast, speaks very little; her silence, lowered eyelids, and half-smile allow Bertha to project significance onto her.

The tone moves between rapture, comedy, unease, and cruelty. Bertha’s bliss is treated with genuine lyrical intensity, but the narrative continually places small disturbances beneath it: her exclusion from the nursery, Harry’s impatience, the sinister cats, the guests’ artificiality, and Pearl’s unreadability. Humour often shades into caricature, especially in descriptions of the artistic set, yet the final revelation transforms earlier comic and decorative details into signs of betrayal. The climax is understated and visual rather than melodramatic: Bertha witnesses gestures and silently reads lips and eyelids. This restraint makes the discovery more devastating.

Structurally, the story unfolds over a compressed domestic interval and advances through scenes rather than exposition: arrival home, nursery, preparations, dinner, after-dinner conversation, departure, revelation. The pacing initially follows Bertha’s restless energy, then slows around the shared contemplation of the pear tree, creating a suspended, quasi-mystical centre. Afterward, the departure scene accelerates toward the silent disclosure. The ending returns to the tree, producing a circular form, but its meaning has changed. Its unchanged loveliness offers no explanation or consolation; it becomes an image of the world’s indifference, the persistence of beauty, or the collapse of Bertha’s assumption that beautiful appearances guarantee emotional truth.

Overall, the prose combines lyrical symbolism, close psychological focalisation, social comedy, and dramatic irony. Its signature effect comes from the tension between Bertha’s ecstatic aesthetic interpretation of life and the concealed realities around her. Beauty is not dismissed as false, but it is shown to be ambiguous: capable of expressing desire while also disguising betrayal.

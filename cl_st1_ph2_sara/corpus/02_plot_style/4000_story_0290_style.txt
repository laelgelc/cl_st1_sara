The story uses a framed first-person confession: a largely unobtrusive narrator records another man’s long, intimate account, so that the central voice feels both immediate and mediated. The opening declaration—“I am in love with my wife”—creates the governing tension at once, since its unsolicited repetition suggests anxiety, guilt, and self-persuasion. From there, the narrative proceeds less as a sequence of external events than as an attempt to understand an emotionally contradictory experience. The speaker repeatedly revises, qualifies, or partially retracts what he has just said. Phrases such as “What I am trying to say,” “Do I make myself clear?” and “Am I telling the truth?” turn the act of narration into part of the drama. His difficulty is not factual concealment so much as the inadequacy of straightforward language to express divided impulses.

The diction is plain, conversational, and deliberately unornamented. Common words, direct statements, and frequent repetitions create the sound of spontaneous speech rather than polished literary exposition. Emotional complexity is conveyed through simple vocabulary: “muddled,” “weak,” “happy,” “furious,” “soft and quiet.” Repeated intensifiers—“very,” “all,” “entirely,” “utterly”—give the confession a slightly breathless insistence. The prose also favors loose coordination, with clauses linked by “and,” reproducing the movement of memory as one perception leads into another. Sentences are often long without becoming formally elaborate; their momentum comes from accumulation, self-correction, and reiterated explanation. Shorter declarative sentences appear at moments of decision or revelation, giving them added force.

Repetition is the story’s most distinctive rhetorical device. The protagonist repeatedly asserts his love for his wife, repeatedly claims that the other woman has left his mind, and repeatedly admits that this is only partly true. Images and actions recur as well: trembling hands, sleepless nights, moonlight, walking in the evening, the woman’s eyes, and the movement between nearness and distance. These repetitions do not merely emphasize information; they expose the gap between what the speaker wants to believe and what his memory continues to preserve. The recursive structure makes the confession feel psychologically authentic, as though the speaker must circle around the experience before he can approach its meaning.

The narrative voice combines candor with unreliability. The speaker is unusually willing to reveal humiliating thoughts, yet his interpretation remains unstable and self-protective. He insists on moral distinctions—love for his fiancée versus physical desire for the tobacconist’s wife—but gradually discovers that the illicit encounter altered his understanding of marriage. His uncertainty is preserved rather than resolved by the framing narrator, who offers almost no judgment. This restraint allows irony to emerge naturally: the man’s public elevation as poet, official, and celebrated bridegroom coincides with a private loss of self-command. Praise has placed him metaphorically “on a perch,” while his encounter with an ordinary working woman brings him “down” into embodied life.

Characterization is achieved through selected physical and behavioral details rather than extensive description. The tobacconist’s wife is defined by her small active body, bright grey eyes, cheap torn dress, quiet knock, and decisive movements. She speaks little or not at all, yet her silence gives her an intense presence. The fiancée is characterized chiefly through her letter and her impulsive public declaration that they will be “two human beings.” The confessor, by contrast, is characterized through the restless patterns of his own language: vanity, idealism, shame, sensuality, and the desire to organize experience into a morally acceptable account. Secondary figures remain generalized as crowds, relatives, admirers, and colleagues, reinforcing the contrast between public opinion and private consciousness.

The story’s imagery moves between social elevation and elemental embodiment. At first the protagonist imagines himself floating, riding above crowds, and receiving the gaze of “a hundred thousand pairs of eyes.” Later, his fiancée seems like a distant bird while he becomes a barefoot boy on a dusty road. Most significantly, the memory of the other woman produces an “earthy feeling”: he imagines himself as a seed receiving warm spring rain or as a tree rather than a man. These organic images frame sexuality not simply as temptation but as a return to instinctive, impersonal life. Moonlight and darkness provide a softer recurring atmosphere, linking secrecy, insomnia, erotic memory, and the persistence of the unconscious.

Pacing is controlled through a contrast between summary and prolonged recollection. The first half efficiently establishes the week’s public excitement, while the approach to the Thursday-night meeting becomes increasingly slow and detailed. Small actions—the rattling pennies, the telephone being lifted and replaced, the soft knock, the half-hour wait—acquire suspense because they register hesitation and inevitability at once. The sexual encounter itself is handled elliptically. The prose stops at embrace, darkness, happiness, and strength, avoiding explicit description while making the outcome clear. The narrative then devotes more space to the encounter’s psychological consequence than to the act itself.

Dialogue is embedded within the confession and serves as a series of crystallizing utterances. “I want you,” the fiancée’s written appeal, and “We will be two human beings” each marks a shift in the protagonist’s self-understanding. The dialogue remains simple and emotionally direct, without elaborate verbal performance. The speaker’s constant addresses to his listener create a strong oral quality and make the reader occupy the role of trusted confidant. Questions are generally rhetorical, expressing the speaker’s need for recognition rather than requesting literal answers.

The tonal range is intimate, uneasy, compassionate, and quietly ironic. The story avoids overt condemnation of adultery and instead examines the coexistence of spiritual aspiration, marital devotion, vanity, and bodily desire. Its central paradox is that the protagonist’s betrayal appears to deepen his humility and his capacity to imagine marriage as mutual care rather than paternal guardianship. The ending does not eliminate the contradiction. His claim that the other woman will be “utterly gone” is undermined by the vividness with which he continues to summon her. The final effect is therefore one of unresolved psychological truth: memory may recede from conscious life, but the bodily knowledge it produced remains part of the self.

The story adopts the style of a traditional literary folktale or moral fairy tale, combining a plain rural setting with matter-of-fact supernatural events. Its opening formula, “Not many years ago there lived,” immediately establishes an oral-storytelling quality, while the unspecified New England farm gives the narrative a recognizable but lightly sketched historical setting. The tone is initially sympathetic and sober, emphasizing poverty, honest labor, and the wife’s quiet endurance, but it gradually becomes comic and satirical as sudden wealth inflates the couple’s pride. The conclusion returns to an explicitly didactic register, stating the lesson rather than leaving it implicit.

The narration is third-person omniscient, linear, and authoritative. The narrator freely summarizes the couple’s circumstances, motives, and changing emotions, while occasionally offering wry judgments about their behavior. Descriptions are economical and functional: the barren hillside, thick stones, steep path, cracked teapot, and simple meals establish hardship without prolonged scenic elaboration. Concrete domestic objects—the pail, pump, apron, wood-box, and pump handle—anchor the magical premise in ordinary life. Supernatural elements are presented without elaborate wonder or explanation, creating a characteristic fairy-tale blend of realism and fantasy.

The diction is clear, accessible, and mildly old-fashioned. Expressions such as “scarce anything,” “good wife,” “come by,” “stood in need,” “parson,” and “La sakes!” create a rustic, period flavor. The prose also uses formal connective language—“nevertheless,” “whereupon,” “afterward,” and repeated causal formulations—alongside colloquial dialogue. Characters speak in simple, direct sentences, with rural contractions and idioms distinguishing the farmer and his wife from the more polished pastor and the composed, articulate beetle. The beetle’s unexpectedly formal speech contributes gentle humor and gives him the air of a tiny magical authority.

Sentence structure is generally straightforward, though descriptive and narrative passages often use coordinated clauses joined by “and,” “but,” and “so.” This produces a flowing, cumulative rhythm suited to oral narration. Repetition is prominent: the woman repeatedly journeys to the brook, the beetle repeatedly appears on the flat stone, the husband “dug, and dug, and dug,” and gold pieces emerge “another, and another.” These patterns create predictability, emphasize escalation, and reproduce the rule-of-repetition common to folktales. Dialogue is frequent and carries much of the exposition, especially the explanations concerning magic, fairy gold, rightful ownership, and the insects’ collection of lost coins.

The plot follows a clear fable-like progression: hardship, kindness to a helpless creature, magical reward, moral uncertainty, reassurance, temptation, pride, loss, and final instruction. Suspense arises less from danger than from successive questions: whether the well will yield anything, whether the gold is real, whether it has been stolen, and whether more can be produced. The pastor’s ethical objection briefly complicates the simple reward narrative, while the beetle’s explanation restores the legitimacy of the treasure. The true conflict then shifts inward, from poverty to vanity and lack of moderation. Pacing is brisk, with summary used for labor and travel and fuller scenes reserved for turning points such as the first pumping, the church donation, the robbery, and the final encounter.

Characterization is broad and emblematic rather than psychologically intricate. The woman represents kindness, endurance, and later vanity; the husband is honest, practical, cautious, and equally susceptible to pride. Their transformation is externalized through comic gestures: he tilts his hat, smokes an oversized cigar, and becomes ill, while she “strutted…like a peacock.” The villagers and thieves embody envy, greed, and ridicule, and the pastor functions as the voice of conscience, though his reasoning is gently mocked as overly narrow. The beetle is both fairy benefactor and moral commentator. Calm, literal-minded, and faintly sardonic, he delivers the final corrective: labor remains a possession that cannot be stolen.

The humor is mild, ironic, and often visual. It appears in the disproportion between the beetle’s tiny form and regal status, the couple’s exaggerated display of wealth, the comet-like procession following them home, and the shopkeeper tenderly carrying an unpaid silk dress. The narrator’s phrasing softens harsh events with comic understatement, as when the robbers fail to put the house in order because they are “in a great hurry.” Similes such as “like a peacock” and “like the tail of a comet” are vivid but uncomplicated, matching the story’s popular, child-accessible style.

The thematic emphasis is openly moral. Kindness is rewarded, honest ownership matters, and wealth itself is not condemned; rather, ostentation, pride, extravagance, and failure to exercise moderation lead to ruin. The circulating gold also carries an ironic idea of fortune as temporary: what one person loses another finds, only to lose it again. The final paragraph converts the narrative into a direct lesson, making the work closer to a fable than to modern short fiction. Overall, the style is lucid, whimsical, gently satirical, structurally repetitive, and explicitly instructive, with domestic realism used to make a magical cautionary tale feel immediate and credible.

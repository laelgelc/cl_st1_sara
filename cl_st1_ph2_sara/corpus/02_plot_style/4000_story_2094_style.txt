The story adopts the form of a formal report delivered to an elite institutional audience. Its first-person narrator repeatedly addresses the “gentlemen” of the Academy, creating the surface manner of a learned lecture or testimonial. This ceremonious frame lends the narrative an air of authority and rational order, even though the speaker’s history—an ape transforming himself into a successful human performer—is grotesque and fantastic. The contrast between impossible subject matter and sober presentation is central to the style: absurdity is treated as fact, while trauma, captivity, and radical self-alteration are discussed in the language of professional achievement.

The narrative voice is poised, articulate, self-conscious, and darkly ironic. The speaker has mastered educated human discourse and takes visible pride in his precision, but his fluency also demonstrates the violence of his assimilation. He moves between deference and aggression, addressing the Academy respectfully while mocking human pretensions, journalistic gossip, manners, and ideals of freedom. His composure is periodically broken by flashes of bitterness, especially when discussing his wounds, confinement, and treatment by humans. These eruptions do not overturn the formal register; instead, they sharpen it, giving the voice a mixture of civility, resentment, vanity, and suppressed pain.

The diction ranges from bureaucratic and philosophical language to blunt physical detail. Phrases concerning “development,” “achievement,” “education,” and “knowledge” coexist with references to fleas, wounds, fur, defecation, alcohol, spitting, and sexual gratification. This collision of elevated abstraction with bodily materiality produces much of the story’s black comedy. Human civilization is represented through gestures and habits rather than noble principles: shaking hands, smoking, drinking, wearing trousers, performing, and speaking on command. The apparently refined narrator continually exposes the animal body beneath social convention, while also revealing animality within supposedly civilized conduct.

The syntax is often elaborate, qualified, and recursive. Long sentences accumulate subordinate clauses, corrections, exceptions, and parenthetical remarks, reproducing the movement of a speaker determined to define his meaning exactly. The narrator frequently interrupts himself to refine a word, distinguish one concept from another, or anticipate misunderstanding. Most important is his insistence on the difference between “freedom” and “a way out.” Repetition gives this distinction the force of a philosophical refrain. Shorter declarative sentences—“I survived this period,” “I did not work things out,” or “Only a way out”—punctuate the longer argumentative passages and make moments of necessity or realization feel stark and final.

The storytelling proceeds broadly chronologically, from capture on the Gold Coast through confinement aboard ship, imitation of the sailors, training, and eventual success in the music hall. Yet this chronology is filtered through imperfect and partially inaccessible memory. The narrator openly admits that his earlier ape consciousness cannot be recovered in human language, and that he must rely on the reports of others for parts of his history. This creates an unstable relation between experience and narration: gaining a human voice has enabled him to tell his story, but the same transformation has estranged him from the self who experienced it. The report is therefore both testimony and evidence of an irreparable loss.

Imagery centres on cages, barriers, holes, gates, wounds, and constricted movement. The shrinking gateway back into the past, the bars cutting into the narrator’s flesh, and the crate wall directly before him turn psychological and social restriction into physical space. By contrast, images of movement—galloping, leaping, swinging, beating a path through bushes—are repeatedly qualified or mocked. Even the trapeze artists’ apparent freedom is described as controlled performance. Spatial imagery thus supports the narrator’s governing idea that survival does not require liberation, only an available route away from immediate destruction.

The story’s humour is dry, grotesque, and satirical. Comic effects arise from literal-minded reversals, exaggerated politeness, bodily indignity, and the narrator’s cool evaluation of appalling events. The sailors’ cruelty is narrated alongside their “goodness”; the trainer burns the ape’s fur but is praised for not being angry; the acquisition of an “average education” is presented as an unprecedented feat. Such contradictions are rarely resolved. Instead, the narration normalizes them, forcing the reader to recognize brutality inside ordinary systems of instruction, entertainment, and social advancement.

The tone remains deeply ambivalent. The narrator neither celebrates his transformation nor straightforwardly condemns it. He rejects sentimental ideas of freedom and refuses to present himself simply as a victim, emphasizing discipline, strategy, and achievement. Nevertheless, his success is bleak: he has escaped the literal cage only by internalizing the trainer’s whip, suppressing his former nature, and entering another regime of performance. His relationship with the half-trained female chimpanzee provides a particularly disturbing final image, since her gaze reflects the madness he has learned to conceal. The ending returns to the rhetoric of objective reporting, but this restraint intensifies rather than resolves the emotional unease.

Overall, the style combines institutional formality, philosophical precision, deadpan fantasy, grotesque physical comedy, and understated horror. Its principal method is sustained irony: civilization appears as imitation, education as coercion, professional success as managed captivity, and articulate selfhood as both an escape route and a form of alienation. The narrator’s controlled, highly reasoned language does not merely recount this paradox; it embodies it.

A writer retreats to his country home near Calcutta after becoming the target of widespread public criticism. There he meets a middle-aged religious mendicant who regards him not as an individual but as a vessel through whom she can worship God. She offers him flowers, eats the remnants of his meal despite caste objections, and repeatedly visits him, finding holiness in his presence while criticizing the barriers created by his servants, clothing, books, and social status. Her intense devotion causes the writer to reconsider ordinary things—food, flowers, animals, silence—as manifestations of the divine.

When villagers mock her for venerating a man who is publicly reviled, she remains steadfast, interpreting his humiliation as God’s purifying discipline. She then reveals the tragedy that led her to renounce worldly life. As a young mother, she neglected her little son in favor of social pleasures. One day, while she was swimming, the child followed her to the river, slipped on the steps, and drowned. Overcome by guilt, she sought consolation in the spiritual teacher adored by her gentle, devoted husband.

For five years she worshipped the teacher as a divine presence, bringing peace back into the household. But one morning he encountered her in wet clothes and praised the beauty of her body, exposing a sensual element beneath their supposedly sacred relationship. Feeling that her worship had been corrupted and unable to live falsely with her husband, she announced that the teacher had commanded her to leave the world. Her husband gradually understood the truth behind her words and did not stop her. Having lost her child and abandoned the husband who loved her, she became a wandering devotee determined to seek God only where she could perceive spiritual truth without deception.

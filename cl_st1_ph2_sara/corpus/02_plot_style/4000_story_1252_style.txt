The story is written in a plain, realist mode, with an unobtrusive third-person narrator whose perspective remains closely aligned with Semen Ivanov. The narration supplies broad biographical context, practical detail, and occasional access to Semen’s thoughts without developing a highly individual narrative voice. Its moral outlook is compassionate and serious: poverty, illness, bereavement, institutional injustice, duty, and self-sacrifice are presented directly rather than through irony or elaborate symbolism.

The diction is concrete, accessible, and slightly archaic, reflecting both the story’s historical setting and the character of an older translation. Russian measurements, titles, patronymics, and social formulas—“versts,” “your honor,” “division-master,” “little father”—are retained and sometimes glossed. Railway tools, regulations, wages, land, and maintenance procedures are named with unusual precision. This documentary specificity grounds the narrative in working life and makes the eventual sabotage technically credible. Religious expressions such as “thank God,” “the dear Lord,” “save your soul,” and “God’s blessing” arise naturally from Semen’s consciousness and reinforce his fatalistic but humane worldview.

Sentence structure alternates between cumulative exposition and abrupt dramatic phrasing. The early sections often use long, additive sentences linked by “and,” reproducing the steady accumulation of Semen’s hardships and routines. Repetition is prominent: he “wept, and went on”; the blood is “pouring and pouring”; Semen must “run, run for dear life.” Dashes, exclamations, fragments, and rhetorical questions become increasingly frequent near the climax, converting the initially measured narrative into a breathless representation of panic. Some constructions are formally stiff or non-idiomatic—“became illuminated, as it were, by a thought,” for example—which marks the prose as translated rather than contemporary English.

Characterisation is economical and contrastive. Semen is patient, devout, dutiful, physically broken, and inclined to accept suffering as fate. Vasili is terse, proud, politically conscious, and enraged by exploitation. Their opposed philosophies are expressed chiefly through dialogue: Semen attributes hardship to God’s allotted lot, while Vasili identifies human greed and abuse as its cause. The dialogue is colloquial and socially marked, using repetitions, interruptions, emphatic questions, proverbial formulations, and familiar forms of address. It often functions as moral debate while remaining rooted in practical grievances about wages, fines, cabbages, permission, and official violence.

The pacing is deliberately asymmetrical. A long expository opening recounts years of suffering, migration, and employment, after which the story settles into the repetitive rhythms of railway maintenance. This slow preparation establishes Semen’s conscientiousness, Vasili’s resentment, and the oppressive bureaucracy before the final crisis. Once the rail is displaced, time contracts sharply: distances, the factory whistle, the train schedule, the curve, and the height of the embankment create a precise countdown. The reader is made to experience Semen’s calculations and physical exhaustion almost moment by moment.

Structurally, the story follows a clear moral arc of hardship, conflict, catastrophe, sacrifice, and confession. Earlier details return with functional force: Semen’s military courage anticipates his conduct at the railway; his memorisation of regulations establishes his vigilance; the willow sticks, knife, and handkerchief become the improvised signal; Vasili’s hatred of injustice develops into sabotage, while his residual conscience produces the final rescue and admission. The ending is compressed into a stark reversal. The man who removed the rail becomes the hand that keeps Semen’s flag raised, and the final confession—“Bind me … it was I who cut the rail”—closes the story without explanatory commentary.

Imagery is sparse but strategically intensified. Most description is utilitarian, yet the climax introduces ominous sensory details: the “deathlike quiet,” iron striking iron, trembling rails, the distant whistle, hot blood, black flies before the eyes, and bells in the ears. The railway itself functions as more than a setting. It embodies regulation, hierarchy, industrial power, and danger, but also the shared social order that Semen protects even at the cost of his life. The improvised red flag is the central image, joining bodily sacrifice to professional duty.

Overall, the style combines social realism, melodramatic suspense, Christian moral symbolism, and a didactic folktale-like clarity. Its emotional force comes not from lyrical ornament but from cumulative suffering, sharply opposed moral temperaments, concrete procedural detail, and a climactic act of physical self-sacrifice that restores another man’s conscience.

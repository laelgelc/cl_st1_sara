Express agent Mike Flannery refuses to release two guinea pigs to Mr. Morehouse for the domestic-pet rate of fifty cents, insisting that, because they are called pigs, they must be charged at the higher livestock rate of sixty cents. Morehouse indignantly leaves them at the office and appeals to the express company.

His complaint passes slowly through multiple departments while the company debates whether guinea pigs are pigs, pets, or rabbits. During the bureaucratic delay, the animals reproduce rapidly. Flannery repeatedly reports their growing numbers and mounting food costs, but headquarters orders him not to sell them and to continue caring for them.

A zoologist eventually confirms that guinea pigs are not pigs, so the company rules that the pet rate applies. By then, however, the original pair has multiplied into thousands. When the company finally orders Flannery to collect fifty cents for the original two and deliver the entire population, he discovers that Morehouse has moved away.

Headquarters then directs Flannery to send all the animals to the main office, overwhelming its warehouse with an unending stream of guinea pigs. As Flannery clears out the last of them, he vows that henceforth every animal—pigs, cows, horses, lions, or tigers—will be classified as a pet at the lowest rate, while consoling himself that at least the multiplying animals were not elephants.

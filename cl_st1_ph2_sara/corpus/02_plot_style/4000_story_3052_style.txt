The story uses a polished, urbane comic style built around dry irony, social satire, and an increasingly elaborate reversal of fortune. Its omniscient third-person narrator remains emotionally detached, describing absurd behaviour in the measured language of sober social observation. This contrast between restrained delivery and ridiculous incident is the chief source of humour. The narrative voice frequently slips in aphoristic judgments—such as the observation that losing self-respect matters little once one has gained the world’s esteem—which give the prose a poised, epigrammatic quality.

The diction is formal, precise, and distinctly period-inflected. Words such as “piquant,” “brevet,” “deferred,” “neophyte,” “contrivance,” and “erstwhile” lend the narration an educated, faintly aristocratic tone. Elevated language is repeatedly applied to petty suburban concerns: oversized vegetables, poultry mishaps, commuter gossip, and card games are discussed as though they were affairs of public or diplomatic importance. Proper names and institutional details—newspapers, railway carriages, illustrated papers, the Bois, and the Roumanian Legation—create a convincing social world while also heightening the absurdity through needless specificity.

Sentences are generally balanced and syntactically controlled, often carrying a comic qualification or reversal at the end. The narrator favours antithesis, understatement, mock logic, and deadpan parenthetical explanation. Long sentences accumulate circumstantial detail before arriving at a deflating conclusion, while shorter statements are used for crisp comic impact. Hyperbole appears often, but it is presented with such composure that even mesmerised chickens, gigantic rats, swordstick attacks, and fatal games of patience briefly acquire an air of plausibility.

Dialogue occupies much of the story and drives both plot and characterization. Exchanges are brisk, articulate, and adversarial, with speakers revealing themselves through what they regard as interesting or socially valuable. Gorworth’s remarks are coolly cynical and inventive; Blenkinthrope’s speech shifts from resentful literal-mindedness to confident fabrication; the commuters often respond as a collective “chorus,” making them seem less like individuals than a miniature public governed by appetite for sensation. Repetition and callbacks—especially to the potato, the Seventh Pullet, and the instruction to “Invent something”—bind the episodes together and expose Blenkinthrope’s moral progression.

Characterization is economical and caricatural. Each secondary figure is defined by one socially useful anecdote, eccentric distinction, or comic credential. Stevenham derives authority from an uncle’s dramatic death; Gorworth treats childhood Scriptural knowledge as a licence for unscrupulousness; Smith-Paddon becomes important through his daughter’s tenuous connection to an actress. These compressed portraits satirize a society in which significance is conferred not by character or achievement but by proximity to novelty, celebrity, scandal, or disaster. Blenkinthrope himself is shaped through escalation: first dull and aggrieved, then tempted, then rewarded for lying, and finally punished when truth arrives too late to overcome his reputation.

The plot is tightly constructed as a sequence of comic amplifications. An opening complaint establishes Blenkinthrope’s hunger for distinction; Gorworth supplies the corrupting principle; the invented poultry story brings immediate success; later inventions become more ornate and less credible; and the genuine domestic tragedy completes the ironic design. The ending returns Blenkinthrope to the trivial subjects with which he began, producing a circular structure. Yet his final obscurity is more painful because he has experienced public attention and lost it. The story’s central reversal is exact: while his life is uneventful, invention makes him famous; when something truly sensational happens, his inventions ensure that nobody believes him.

The humour is predominantly satirical rather than sympathetic. It targets gossip, commuter hierarchy, popular journalism, celebrity culture, credulity, competitive misfortune, and the human desire to convert private suffering into social capital. Even bereavement is filtered through imagined headlines. The narrative avoids overt moralizing, but its irony exposes the corrosive effects of attention-seeking and the public’s preference for entertaining falsehood over ordinary truth. At the same time, Blenkinthrope’s punishment is disproportionate enough to retain a streak of black comedy: his wife’s death matters to him both as a genuine loss and as the long-awaited sensational event that should have redeemed his colourless existence.

The overall tone is witty, unsentimental, and mischievously cruel. Pathos appears only briefly and is quickly displaced by vanity, journalistic calculation, or social embarrassment. Violence and death are treated with comic decorum rather than emotional intensity, creating a characteristic blend of elegance and morbidity. The style depends on incongruity: grave language frames trivial matters, grotesque events are narrated calmly, and moral decline is described as though it were a practical social education. The result is a compact tale with a strong anecdotal surface, an epigrammatic narrative voice, and a neatly engineered final irony.

During a spring journey down the Volga, the frail, deeply religious Mitia helps steer his father Silan’s raft alongside the hired laborer Sergei. Sergei relentlessly mocks Mitia because Silan is openly having an affair with Mitia’s wife, Marka. Mitia explains that he never wanted the marriage and refused to live with Marka as her husband, believing intimacy without love would violate his conscience. Feeling rejected and ruined, Marka turned to the vigorous, domineering Silan.

Mitia insists that true law lies within the soul and dreams of escaping his corrupt family to join a persecuted Christian brotherhood in the Caucasus. Sergei initially treats the scandal as a joke and blames Mitia’s weakness for creating it, but his taunts eventually drive Mitia into a sobbing collapse. Mitia begs to be left alone, warning that the torment may make him throw himself into the river. Sergei finally grasps the depth of his suffering and becomes uneasy.

At the front of the raft, Silan and Marka embrace and discuss their situation. Silan admits their relationship is sinful but claims their love justifies the risk. He regrets arranging Marka’s marriage to Mitia shortly before his own wife died, since otherwise he could have married her lawfully. Bitterly contemptuous of his son’s weakness, Silan imagines that Mitia’s death would free them to marry, move far away, and atone through good works. Marka prays that the sickly Mitia will die soon. As dawn clears the clouds and brightens the river, the raft continues downstream, with Mitia still trapped between his spiritual convictions, public humiliation, and his father’s overpowering will.

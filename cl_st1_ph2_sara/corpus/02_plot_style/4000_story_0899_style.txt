The story uses a brisk, comic-sentimental style characteristic of early twentieth-century American magazine fiction. Its tone moves fluidly from affectionate caricature and broad urban humour to suspense, pathos and heroic affirmation. The narrator treats Peter Mullaney with amused tenderness, exaggerating his small stature, fierce determination and reverence for police work without undermining his dignity. Comic hyperbole is pervasive: muscles resemble “hard-boiled eggs,” Peter lacks the required height by “the distance between a bumble-bee’s eyes,” and his happiness or misery is repeatedly rendered through extravagantly physical images. This buoyant exaggeration makes the eventual violence and emotional climax more affecting.

The narration is third-person omniscient, strongly authorial and evaluative, with frequent access to Peter’s thoughts and emotions. It often pauses to supply background, explain institutional practices or make wry generalisations about life, ambition and bureaucracy. Characterisation is economical and emphatic, built from recurring physical and verbal signatures: Peter is small, freckled, red-haired and intensely earnest; Kondorman is rigid, scientific and rule-bound; Gaffney is bulky, seasoned, genial and morally practical. These figures initially verge on types, but each is given emotional depth through decisive action. The central contrast is between abstract qualification and demonstrated character—encyclopedic knowledge versus courage—and nearly every descriptive and comic detail reinforces that opposition.

The prose alternates between long, elaborately cumulative sentences and short, forceful statements. Lists are a major comic device, especially when cataloguing the bewildering range of facts and regulations Peter must learn. Parallel constructions and repeated infinitives convey the scale of his ambition, while rhythmic repetition—most notably “One, two, three, bend,” “A cop never runs away when needed,” and “the Tropic of Capricorn”—provides structural unity. The recurring geographical question begins as bureaucratic absurdity, becomes a symbol of humiliation, and is finally transformed into the vehicle of the closing joke and emotional vindication.

Dialogue carries much of the story’s energy. Kondorman speaks in clipped, official declarations; Peter’s speech is direct, pleading and earnest; Gaffney’s is expansive, anecdotal and proverbial. Eye dialect heavily marks the Irish-American characters through spellings such as “Pether,” “whin,” “prisint,” and “foorce.” This creates strong vocal differentiation and supplies much of the humour, though it also reflects the ethnic stereotyping and now-dated conventions of its period. The dialogue frequently stages conflicts between written regulations and lived wisdom, with Gaffney acting as the spokesman for the story’s practical moral code.

The narrative is tightly plotted around reversal and delayed recognition. An opening scene of joyful physical training leads into an extended account of Peter’s lifelong ambition, narrow admission and academic weakness. The failed examination appears to close off his goal, but his final patrol creates a practical test more important than the official one. The burglary sequence sharply changes the tempo: sentences become shorter, verbs more violent, sensory details more immediate, and physical action is narrated through flashes, blows, falls and bodily pain. Peter’s internal hesitation is compressed into a stark moral choice before the action accelerates into melodramatic combat.

Emotion is presented openly rather than obliquely. Tears, clenched fists, husky voices, drooping shoulders and symbolic objects externalise feeling. The uniform, brass buttons, shoes and especially the shield function as recurring emblems of identity and belonging. The story willingly embraces melodrama—the dying mentor, the unarmed hero facing two gunmen, the hospital-bed recognition scene—but balances it with humour and restraint at key moments. Its ending combines sentiment with a punch line: Kondorman’s absurd declaration that the Tropic of Capricorn must be in the Bronx completes his transformation from inflexible bureaucrat to humane judge while preserving his characteristic forcefulness.

Overall, the style is vivid, accessible, highly plotted and morally explicit. It relies on caricature, repetition, dialect, comic lists, physical imagery and a clean dramatic arc. The governing effect is affectionate and populist: formal education and regulations have value, but courage, loyalty and steadfastness are presented as the truest measures of professional worth.

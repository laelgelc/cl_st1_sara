The story adopts the manner of an early twentieth-century literary fairy tale: whimsical, morally purposeful, and gently satirical. Its fantasy world is presented with cheerful certainty rather than elaborate explanation. Knooks, fairies, and ryls are treated as familiar parts of the universe, while magical acts occur instantly and without technical rules. The central conceit—stuffed birds restored to life by a supernatural guardian—combines childlike wonder with social commentary about fashion, commerce, and the exploitation of animals.

The narration is third-person omniscient and strongly authorial. The narrator explains motives, supplies background information, summarizes events, and occasionally addresses the reader directly through phrases such as “you could scarcely imagine it” and the concluding “having read this story, you will understand.” This creates the intimate tone of an adult storyteller speaking to children. The narrator also uses knowing generalizations about mortals, fashions, and immortal beings, lending the tale the air of a playful fable whose world has its own settled truths.

The prose is clear, chronological, and episodic. Paragraphs usually advance one action or one stage of Popopo’s reasoning at a time: he becomes bored, visits the city, frees the birds, attempts to repair the milliner’s loss with mice, consults the birds, seeks advice from the king, and finally changes fashion itself. Transitions such as “Finally,” “Therefore,” “After a time,” “The following morning,” and “Yet” make the causal sequence explicit. The pacing is brisk, with little scenic description beyond what is needed to establish contrast, comedy, or plot. Repetition gives the narrative a cumulative structure, especially as each attempted solution produces a new complication.

Sentence structure is generally accessible but more formal and expansive than that of contemporary children’s fiction. The prose mixes short declarative statements with balanced, multi-clause sentences featuring semicolons, parenthetical qualifications, and explanatory conjunctions. Inverted or old-fashioned constructions—such as “such was not the case,” “were they obliged,” and “that is certain”—contribute to the period flavor. Double hyphens function as dashes, while occasional exclamations and direct questions add animation. The source also contains a likely typographical error in “jumpped,” which is not otherwise characteristic of the style.

The diction is polished, storybook-like, and mildly archaic. Words and phrases such as “dainty,” “wearisome,” “halloaed,” “wares,” “headgear,” “straightway,” “gayly,” “befuddled,” and “his majesty” evoke an older register without making the story difficult to follow. Commercial language—“barter and exchange,” “goods and merchandise,” “property,” “business,” and “ruined”—is deliberately juxtaposed with the vocabulary of enchantment, nature, and immortality. This contrast supports the story’s satire of human institutions from the perspective of an innocent outsider.

Characterization is economical and functional. Popopo is defined by boredom, benevolence, innocence, and a persistent sense of responsibility. He is powerful enough to animate stuffed birds and alter the press, yet ignorant of ordinary human customs; this disparity generates most of the humor. His mistakes do not arise from malice but from taking appearances literally and applying supernatural ethics to social conventions. The milliner and her sister are broad comic figures, reacting with sobs, shrieks, leaps, and fainting, while the birds become articulate advocates for their own freedom. The king of the knooks serves as the conventional wise ruler who identifies both the lesson and the practical solution.

Dialogue is direct, theatrical, and argument-driven. Speakers usually state their feelings or positions plainly, and tags often add emotional coloring: “angrily,” “gravely,” “solemnly,” or “sternly.” The birds’ debate with Popopo forms the ethical center of the story. Their rhetorical questions and sharp analogies expose the absurdity of treating living creatures as decorations, particularly when the linnet asks whether Popopo himself would accept being worn on a hat. Individual birds receive only slight differentiation, but their collective voice is lively, assertive, and morally persuasive.

The humor is gentle and ironic. It depends on reversals, literal-minded magic, exaggerated reactions, and the mismatch between Popopo’s intentions and human responses. His belief that mice would make attractive hat ornaments, and that enforced immobility would improve their morals, is comic because it is perfectly logical from his unworldly perspective. The tale also satirizes fashion as arbitrary and socially coercive: people accept whatever newspapers and magazines declare stylish, scarcely questioning the “mandate of fashion.” The eventual solution does not restore the old arrangement but manipulates the same system of conformity for a humane end.

Thematically, the story favors compassion, freedom, and responsibility over property rights and commercial custom. It criticizes the use of birds in millinery while acknowledging that individual people may depend economically on an unjust fashion. Popopo’s dilemma is therefore resolved not by punishing the milliner or returning the birds, but by changing the wider social convention that created the conflict. The moral is embedded in the plot rather than delivered as a solemn maxim, although the king explicitly warns against thoughtless interference and insists that those who cause trouble must remedy it.

The ending uses a mock-explanatory flourish typical of fanciful children’s tales. It connects the magical events to an everyday mystery—why hunters sometimes miss birds—and offers an intentionally absurd explanation as though it were established fact. This closing address reinforces the narrator’s conspiratorial relationship with the reader and leaves the story on a playful rather than preachy note. Overall, the style is lucid, episodic, warmly didactic, lightly absurd, and socially observant, with fairy-tale simplicity carrying a pointed critique of fashion and cruelty.

The story uses a first-person retrospective voice that remains closely aligned with the narrator’s adolescent consciousness. The narration is observant, wry, and emotionally restrained: pain is rarely declared outright and instead emerges through practical details, private fantasies, and the discrepancy between what the narrator says and what he thinks. His self-created “religion” of precise calm provides a key to the voice. Even when describing abandonment, foreclosure, danger, or resentment, he tends to intellectualise, joke, or narrativise his experience. Short admissions such as “My father: gone for months” and “I felt strong. He noticed me. I felt headless” puncture the more expansive passages with compressed emotional force.

The structure is braided and nonlinear, alternating among the narrator’s present life at the home improvement store, memories of his father’s behaviour, and a childhood Anansi tale performed by the father. Transitions often occur without explanation, requiring the reader to infer the thematic relationship between strands. The folktale functions as more than an inset story: Lion’s appetite, arrogance, and impatience echo the father, while Anansi’s resourcefulness reflects the narrator’s need to survive through intelligence, imagination, and self-control. The embedded tale also preserves the father at his most affectionate and creative, complicating any simple judgment of him as merely neglectful. As the story advances, the folktale moves toward Anansi’s victory while the realistic plot moves toward the father’s return, creating parallel forms of suspense with sharply different kinds of resolution.

The prose combines conversational naturalism with sudden lyric compression. Sentences frequently begin plainly and accumulate clauses, qualifications, or imaginative extensions, mimicking the narrator thinking his way around feelings he cannot address directly. These longer passages are counterbalanced by fragments and brief declarative sentences. Colons are used to create abrupt equivalences or revelations, as in “My father: gone for months,” while repetition gives emotional moments a restrained rhythm. The final exchange—“Dad,” followed by the narrator’s inability to say more—uses withheld speech as its central effect. Internal statements such as “There’s food in the fridge because of me” reveal the confrontation that cannot occur aloud.

The diction moves fluidly between colloquial teenage speech, workplace terminology, comic-book and film references, and elevated figurative language. Technical objects—pallet jacks, bolt cutters, nylon vests, forklifts, washing machines, paint cans—give the workplace scenes tactile credibility. At the same time, the narrator recasts ordinary labour through heroic and mythic frameworks: the unload crew becomes “the Specialists,” trailers become villains and caves, and Cato becomes Hercules. This mock-epic register is humorous but also psychologically protective. It grants dignity and adventure to exhausting, low-paid work while exposing how badly the narrator needs an alternative story about his circumstances.

Humour is pervasive but defensive rather than frivolous. It appears in the imagined action-movie roles of the workers, Cato’s repeated “Hercules,” the attractiveness rankings, Reese’s crude encouragement, and the elaborate fantasy of discovering baby Cristie behind the appliances. These comic passages humanise the characters and delay direct confrontation with the narrator’s fear. They also tend to darken as they unfold. The fantasy of finding a baby, for example, begins whimsically but becomes a vision of shared, distant fatherhood, connecting the narrator’s longing for care with his own forced assumption of parental responsibility.

Dialogue is concise, idiomatic, and strongly differentiated by character. The father speaks with authority, impatience, and casual assumptions about obedience; the mother answers pain with composed resignation; Cato’s language is playful and fraternal; Reese mixes vulgarity, prejudice, encouragement, and awkward affection. The Anansi sections use a more performative oral cadence, including repetition, direct address, exclamations, and phrases such as “You will see.” The references to English and Twi, Ghana, Anansi, and the Togo Mountains establish a diasporic cultural background without turning the narration into exposition. Folklore is presented as a living family practice embodied through the father’s voices, hands, and movements.

Characterisation relies heavily on gesture, work habits, and contradiction. The father is both the imaginative storyteller who makes his children “stars” and the man who repeatedly leaves them waiting. The mother’s calm simultaneously comforts, wounds, and teaches the narrator the emotional discipline he later adopts. Cato is both a fallen school “god” and a generous coworker who urges the narrator not to become trapped. Reese is rendered with moral ambiguity: he may be racist, yet he is also dependable, encouraging, and proud of the narrator. Rather than resolving these contradictions, the story allows affection and distrust, admiration and disappointment, to coexist.

Imagery repeatedly centres on mouths, stomachs, weight, containment, and unloading. Lion swallows the rabbit children; trucks have “guts” and “mouths”; trailers hold tightly packed objects; washing machines pin Cato down; anguish sits like lead in the narrator’s stomach. These motifs connect consumption and physical burden to familial and economic pressure. The unloading job becomes an extended metaphor for the narrator’s emotional condition: he digs through packed, dangerous interiors hoping to find something meaningful at the end, only to reach “a tall slab of nothing.” Hands are similarly important. The father’s hands become Lion and Anansi, workers handle dangerous loads, and the father’s returning hand rests on the narrator’s shoulder in a gesture that is affectionate but inadequate.

Pacing alternates between summary and vivid scene. Months of labour or abandonment may be compressed into a sentence, while accidents, remembered humiliations, and moments with the father are slowed into concrete action and dialogue. The repeated returns to the shipment truck create a cyclical, oppressive rhythm: school, work, sleep, dread, and another truck. Against this repetition, the father’s return arrives suddenly and almost anticlimactically, in a paint aisle on a day when there is no truck. The ordinary setting prevents cathartic melodrama and keeps the emotional emphasis on the narrator’s paralysis.

The dominant themes are abandonment, premature adulthood, economic precarity, masculinity, storytelling, and the unstable boundary between care and harm. Fatherhood appears in multiple forms: biological, failed, imagined, fraternal, and improvised. The narrator tries to parent his sister, imagines the workers becoming secret fathers to an abandoned baby, regards Cato as a brother, and receives from Reese the affirmation his own father has not provided. The story also examines survival through narrative. The father uses folklore to enchant his children; the narrator uses movies, superheroes, jokes, and fantasies to make labour and fear bearable. Yet these invented frameworks do not erase reality, and the story repeatedly exposes the emptiness behind them.

The ending is deliberately unresolved and emotionally ambivalent. The narrator has imagined his father gone forever and has proved his own competence, but the father’s return instantly destabilises that hard-won identity. His thoughts contain accusation, pride, gratitude, and bewilderment, while his spoken responses remain minimal and compliant. The final “Yes, I do” answers the practical question of whether he needs a ride, but it also captures his continuing dependence on the person who abandoned him. The style avoids explicit reconciliation or condemnation, closing instead on the tension between injury and attachment.

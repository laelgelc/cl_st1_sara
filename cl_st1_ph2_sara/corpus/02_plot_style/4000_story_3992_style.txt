The story uses a polished comic-realistic style shaped by an urbane, intrusive narrator. The narration moves freely between panoramic descriptions of London, close observation of social types, and the private emotions of individual characters. The narrator frequently addresses or includes the reader—“you or I”—and offers mock-authoritative explanations, creating the impression of a witty raconteur guiding an audience through the action. Omniscience is flexible: the narrative enters Plimmer’s jealousy and insecurity, Ellen’s combative thought process, and Alf Brooks’s cowardly self-justification, often exposing the discrepancy between how characters see themselves and how they actually behave.

The prevailing tone is genial, ironic, and lightly satirical, with tenderness gradually emerging beneath the comedy. Elevated language is repeatedly applied to mundane subjects for comic effect. Battersea’s blocks of flats become “cliffs,” their residents “cliff-dwellers,” and a milkman rattling his cans is compared to Apollo driving his chariot. Domestic exchanges over eggs and bacon are presented as a miniature “Romeo and Juliet” drama, while Plimmer’s frustration is described in the language of tragedy, warfare, and volcanic eruption. This mock-heroic treatment magnifies ordinary life without becoming cruel; even ridiculous characters are rendered with a measure of sympathy.

The prose relies heavily on contrast and antithesis. Nature is set against Intellect, Brain against Crime, turbulent Whitechapel against placid Battersea, and Plimmer’s intimidating exterior against his vulnerable, loyal nature. Comic lists sharpen these contrasts: the artistic residents “assault and batter nothing but pianos,” “steal nothing but ideas,” and “murder nobody except Chopin and Beethoven.” Such parallel constructions, escalating repetitions, and reversals give the narration rhythmic precision. Short emphatic fragments—“He brooded,” “Yet here was Alf Brooks,” “Playfulness, after this, was at a discount”—regularly interrupt longer, more elaborate sentences, controlling pace and delivering punchlines.

The vocabulary ranges from formal and literary to colloquial and slangy. Narratorial phrases such as “the repose which stamps the caste of Vere de Vere” coexist with “blighters,” “pinched,” “hop it,” and “gift of the gab.” This mixture is central to the humour: high cultural references, bureaucratic language, and grand abstractions are brought into contact with working-class courtship, tradesmen’s calls, household disputes, and police routine. Capitalisation also supplies ironic emphasis, as in “Nature,” “Intellect,” “Brain,” “Crime,” “All Right,” and “Force,” turning ordinary categories into mock-philosophical principles.

Dialogue is lively, economical, and strongly differentiated by class and personality. Non-standard pronunciation and grammar—“pleeceman,” “I ’adn’t but borrowed it,” “if you had have done”—give Ellen and the tradespeople an audible social identity, while Plimmer’s bluntness reflects emotional repression. His repeated “Hop it” carries more feeling than an eloquent declaration would, because the gruff phrase disguises an act of self-sacrifice. Alf’s clipped exclamations and his refusal to acknowledge Ellen reveal his cowardice immediately. Dialogue often functions as a contest: characters tease, provoke, evade, and test one another, and emotional turning points arrive through unexpectedly direct replies, especially Plimmer’s simple “Yes” when accused of jealousy.

Characterisation is caricatural in outline but humane in development. Figures are introduced through vivid physical or social shorthand: Plimmer is red-faced, large-footed, and broken-nosed; Alf is scented, curled, brightly dressed, and socially vain; the mistress is sharp-faced; her husband is bald, nervous, and nearly invisible. Similes frequently make people resemble animals or objects—Plimmer looks like an angry gorilla, a puzzled bear, or a block of wood—yet these comic images coexist with genuine moral seriousness. Plimmer’s ugliness becomes less important as his courage and compassion emerge, while Alf’s beauty is exposed as superficial.

The story is tightly structured around reversal. Plimmer initially welcomes Battersea’s peace, then longs for action; when action comes, it threatens the woman he loves. He envies Alf’s romantic success, but Alf’s betrayal creates the possibility of Ellen’s change of heart. Plimmer first tries to arrest Ellen impersonally, then offers to sacrifice his career to free her; Ellen, in turn, refuses freedom because she will not let him suffer for her. The comic rivalry therefore develops into a test of moral character. Recurrent motifs—the pavement Plimmer kicks, his policeman’s “wooden” mask, his broken nose, and the contrast between public duty and private feeling—bind the episodes together.

Pacing alternates between leisurely comic exposition and concentrated dramatic scenes. The opening spends time establishing the neighbourhood as a social ecosystem, after which successive encounters intensify the emotional conflict. Asterisks mark clear scene changes, giving the narrative an episodic, almost theatrical quality. Background details such as sunshine, children, a cat, a barking dog, and a woman singing create ironic calm around moments of distress. Near the climax these ambient sounds cease, making Plimmer and Ellen seem “curiously alone” and briefly shifting the register from social comedy to intimate romance.

Overall, the style combines Edwardian urban comedy, mock-heroic narration, class-conscious dialogue, and sentimental romance. Its humour comes from incongruity, exaggeration, repetition, verbal elevation, and affectionate exposure of vanity. Its emotional effect depends on allowing a stock comic policeman to reveal unexpected nobility. The final exchange remains understated and character-driven: rather than completing the romance with an explicit declaration, it uses Ellen’s question about whether he is called “Eddie or Ted” to signal intimacy, acceptance, and a changed future.

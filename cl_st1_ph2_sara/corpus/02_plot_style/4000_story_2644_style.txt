The story uses a retrospective first-person confessional voice, framed by the narrator’s confinement in an asylum. Jervas presents himself as lucid, educated, and unfairly disbelieved, yet his evasions, obsessive habits, archaic speech, and supernatural claims continually undermine his reliability. This tension between reasoned testimony and apparent monomania is the narrative’s central stylistic mechanism. Direct appeals to the reader and references to physicians, attendants, relatives, and witnesses give the account the appearance of a defence or deposition, while strategically withheld details—“things I need not name” and experiences he “must never recall”—preserve uncertainty and dread.

The prose is ornate, formal, and deliberately antiquated. Its vocabulary draws heavily on Gothic, funerary, theological, and antiquarian registers: “sepulcher,” “charnel-house,” “inurned,” “declivity,” “bacchanalian,” and “corporeal tenement.” Archaic forms such as “betwixt,” “whilst,” “aye,” and “’tis” reinforce the narrator’s affinity with earlier centuries and become increasingly significant as his personality appears to merge with that of Jervas Hyde. The language is elevated even when describing physical action, lending the narrative the quality of an old memoir or supernatural case history. Occasional spelling and punctuation irregularities do little to alter the strongly controlled, literary tone.

Sentences are generally long, periodic, and clause-heavy, with frequent semicolons, subordinate phrases, balanced constructions, and qualifying asides. The narrator often begins from abstraction or reflection before arriving at a concrete event. This creates a measured, ceremonious rhythm and makes his reasoning sound persuasive, though it also suggests compulsive self-justification. Repetition and parallelism are prominent, especially in formulations such as “seen and felt,” “reading, thinking, and dreaming,” or “things that are not, or are no longer, living.” Shorter declarative sentences are reserved for revelations and transitions, giving them additional force.

Description is sensuous but oppressive. The natural world is rendered through dense accumulations of colour, texture, smell, light, and moisture: moss, damp granite, tangled briars, waning moonlight, rank swamp, whitish dust, and “surging seas of moist verdure.” Landscapes are rarely neutral; they appear sentient, sacred, or malign. Trees possess “presiding dryads,” the tomb beckons, the forest has a “hideous soul,” and the ruined mansion has a “presiding demon.” This personification turns setting into an active psychological and supernatural force. Recurrent contrasts between light and darkness, warmth and chill, revelry and decay, or luxuriant vegetation and buried death produce a dreamlike Gothic atmosphere.

The narrative develops slowly through cumulative obsession. The tomb is introduced early, but entry is delayed across childhood, adolescence, genealogical discovery, nocturnal wandering, and repeated vigils. Each episode deepens the narrator’s identification with the dead before the story accelerates into the vault visits, the transformation of his manner, the phantom revel, the lightning strike, and his institutionalisation. The final paragraphs then slow again to present competing rational and supernatural explanations. This structure resembles a tightening spiral: each return to the tomb adds historical knowledge, sensory intensity, and psychological dependence.

Exposition is extensive and often antiquarian. Family history, local legend, genealogy, old books, burial customs, dialects, clothing, and eighteenth-century literary culture all contribute to an impression of documentary depth. Proper names and specific dates lend credibility to events that remain unverifiable. The embedded drinking song temporarily shifts the style into jaunty rhymed verse, using colloquial contractions, comic blasphemy, and carpe diem sentiment. Its robust conviviality contrasts sharply with the narrator’s earlier isolation and provides stylistic evidence of his apparent possession by an eighteenth-century personality.

Dialogue is scarce and usually reported rather than dramatised. Other characters function mainly as witnesses who interpret Jervas’s behaviour from the outside. Their practical observations—the untouched padlock, his sleep outside the vault, the origins of his historical knowledge—form a rational counter-narrative. Because the story largely withholds their direct voices, however, the reader remains enclosed within Jervas’s consciousness. This limited perspective sustains ambiguity rather than resolving it.

The dominant tone combines melancholy, morbidity, awe, and intellectual hauteur. Jervas treats ordinary humanity as unimaginative and spiritually limited, while regarding his own abnormal sensitivity as privileged perception. Fear is often displaced by fascination: odors repel yet bewitch, death feels familiar, and confinement appears more hellish than the tomb. Moments of flamboyant decadence and black humour briefly interrupt the solemnity, especially during the spectral banquet and drinking song, but these ultimately intensify the contrast between sensual life and inevitable burial.

The story’s supernatural effects depend less on explicit monsters than on uncertainty about identity, memory, and perception. Dreams, ancestral inheritance, reincarnation, possession, atavism, and madness remain overlapping possibilities. The climactic resemblance between Jervas and the miniature marked “J. H.” supplies suggestive physical evidence, while the empty coffin bearing the name “Jervas” provides the final validating detail. The ending therefore uses delayed corroboration rather than full explanation: it confirms that the narrator’s obsession has an objective counterpart without proving that his experiences occurred exactly as described.

Overall, the style is antiquarian Gothic filtered through psychological horror. Its key features are an unreliable but rhetorically sophisticated narrator, elaborate archaic diction, long syntactically layered sentences, luxuriant descriptions of decay and landscape, gradual escalation, strategic withholding, and a concluding twist that preserves the boundary between delusion and supernatural truth.

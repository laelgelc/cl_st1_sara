The story uses a plainspoken bush-yarn style, combining realistic detail with escalating farce. Its third-person narrator is genial, knowing and intermittently intrusive, offering broad observations such as the inevitability of a “rich reef” supposedly existing nearby and reporting later communal legends through phrases like “Bushmen say.” This creates the atmosphere of an anecdote retold and embellished over time. The narrative voice treats the characters with affectionate irony: their ingenuity, stubbornness and foolishness are exposed without malice, while the dog’s innocent enthusiasm turns human panic into comedy.

The diction is strongly rooted in Australian bush life. Mining, fishing and camp vocabulary—“shaft,” “reef,” “baling,” “time-fuse,” “billy,” “claim,” “scrub” and “shanty”—gives the tale occupational and regional authenticity. Colloquialisms, contractions and dialect spellings such as “foller,” “blanky,” “’em” and the final drawn-out “Da-a-ve” reproduce informal speech and a nasal bush cadence. Similes are concrete, local and comic: the dog’s tail swings “like a stock-whip,” Jim climbs “like a native bear,” and the exploded yellow dog is described through an absurd sequence involving a horse, fire, dust, a barrow and a fence. Even pain is rendered through idiosyncratic folk description, as in Dave’s claim that toothache has a “burred edge.”

Sentence structure shifts with the action. Early passages use long, methodical sentences and accumulative lists to explain mining techniques and the cartridge’s construction. This painstaking procedural detail both establishes credibility and delays the joke, while each extra layer of canvas, paper, wire, wax and tallow increases the reader’s anticipation. Once the fuse is lit, the prose accelerates through coordinated clauses, rapid changes of subject, repeated verbs of pursuit and breathless catalogues of shouting, dodging, climbing and hiding. Short statements—“Dave got an idea,” “And the dog followed Andy,” “It all took but a very few moments”—serve as comic pivots. Dashes, exclamation marks, interruptions and the suspended sentence immediately before the explosion sharpen timing and suspense.

The humour depends chiefly on escalation, repetition and dramatic irony. The reader understands the danger while the retriever interprets the chase as a game, and every attempt to escape merely transfers the bomb to another victim. Repeated retrieving becomes the central comic mechanism: the dog fetches the cartridge after Dave throws it away, lays it beneath Jim’s tree and follows whichever fleeing man appears most playful. Human behaviour mirrors the dog’s irrationality, since the men cannot explain why they keep following one another instead of scattering. Understatement intensifies the absurdity, especially in the dry assurance that the cartridge “had been excellently well made” immediately after detonation.

Characterisation is economical and behavioural. Dave is the elaborate theorist, Andy the patient craftsman who makes Dave’s ideas dangerously effective, and Jim the sceptic who nevertheless becomes trapped in the consequences. Tommy is described through recurring physical motifs—his grin, slobber and lashing tail—and remains amiable and oblivious throughout, making him both the engine and the innocent hero of the farce. Minor figures and animals are drawn in swift caricature, especially the bad-tempered publican, the hysterical household and the vicious yellow dog. Repetition of distinctive traits gives the ensemble clarity amid the frantic action.

Structurally, the story proceeds from exposition to preparation, ignition, extended chase, explosion, aftermath and communal punchline. The opening’s documentary realism grounds an increasingly improbable chain of events, while earlier details about the dog’s retrieving habit foreshadow the central disaster. The climax is briefly withheld by a dash and followed not by graphic immediacy but by extravagant reports of displaced buildings, fleeing horses and singed dogs. The final return to camp restores ordinary life—more chops are cooked and Jim is helped from the hole—before the closing taunt converts the incident into enduring bush folklore.

Overall, the style blends social realism, oral storytelling, deadpan understatement, affectionate caricature and slapstick. Beneath the comedy lies a portrait of bush community life in which hardship is met through improvisation, ridicule and storytelling. Reputation outlasts danger: Dave’s failed fishing scheme becomes a permanent joke, and the leisurely final call from passing Bushmen supplies a circular, memorable ending.

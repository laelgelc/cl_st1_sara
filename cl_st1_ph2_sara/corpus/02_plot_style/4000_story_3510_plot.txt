An older narrator recalls the frightening, gloomy, and often coarse pictures and exhibitions of his childhood, contrasting them with the abundant, wholesome illustrated books enjoyed by Victorian children. He surveys the development of English pictorial humor from savage caricaturists such as Gillray and Rowlandson through George Cruikshank, whose work helped make comic art suitable for families.

The discussion then turns to *Punch* and its leading illustrator, John Leech. The narrator praises Leech’s humane, observant drawings of contemporary British life—families, children, servants, dandies, sportsmen, domestic interiors, fashions, and landscapes. He argues that Leech’s authentic details, gentle satire, humor, beauty, and kindness create a valuable social record while allowing parents and children to laugh together.

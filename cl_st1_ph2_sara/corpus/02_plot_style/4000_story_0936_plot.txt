An American traveler arrives in London feeling helpless and anxious about his luggage, but is surprised by the unfailingly courteous, disciplined porter and cabman. Riding through the dark, rainy city, he contrasts London’s subdued order with New York’s relentless noise. He marvels at cab horses deliberately sliding down slick streets, then watches bystanders—including a well-dressed young man—ritually scold a cabman before helping raise his fallen horse.

At his hotel, the traveler encounters a weary clerk and an elderly, extraordinarily slow lift operator. The next day he observes London’s buses, cabmen, pedestrians, and police. He concludes that the city’s calm efficiency comes not from individual brilliance but from collective training and obedience: traffic flows because police and citizens participate in the same disciplined system. Along the way, he digresses into a comic Western tale about a top hat provoking gunfights and reflects on how aggressive advertisements overwhelm necessary public signs. His initial confusion gradually becomes fascination with London’s quiet, artificial civility and social order.

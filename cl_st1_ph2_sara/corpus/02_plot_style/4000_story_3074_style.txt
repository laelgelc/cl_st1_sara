The story adopts an ornate, late-Victorian or Edwardian literary style, combining a children’s fantasy with Gothic supernaturalism, domestic comedy, and restrained moral seriousness. Its setting is rendered through period-specific social and material detail—box pews, crinolines, Geneva bands, hassocks, catechisms, servants, and patent medicines—which establishes a formal, hierarchical adult world against which the child’s imaginative freedom is defined. The diction is elevated and occasionally archaic, especially in descriptions of the church, where heraldic terms such as “argent,” “sable,” and “gules” mingle with ecclesiastical and architectural vocabulary. This richness is offset by plain, emotionally direct statements such as “He must be very tired,” allowing the prose to move naturally between adult sophistication and a child’s compassionate logic.

The narration is third-person but closely aligned with Ernest’s consciousness. The narrator repeatedly enters his imaginative world, treating fantasy not as a decorative invention but as a credible mode of perception. Free indirect discourse and childlike reasoning blur the boundary between narration and thought: marble may be alive because Lot’s wife could become salt, punishment is objectionable precisely because it is punishment, and adults “never saw the things that mattered.” At intervals the narrator addresses the reader directly—“This did not surprise him as much as it would surprise you” and “Did you ever take Gregory’s powder?”—creating the intimate, conspiratorial manner of an oral storyteller. These interventions align the narrator with the child rather than the adults and invite the reader to accept the supernatural on emotional grounds.

The syntax is highly varied. Long, elaborately subordinated sentences accumulate visual, historical, and associative detail, particularly in the opening church description, where one image unfolds into another through analogy and digression. Short sentences then provide comic or emotional emphasis: “All the same, marble had moved”; “He must be very tired”; “The aunts!” Parentheses, dashes, semicolons, and interrupted clauses reproduce the movement of thought, including hesitation, qualification, and sudden realization. Repetition is used incantatorily in Ernest’s appeals—“You must come, you must”—and more subtly in recurring contrasts between the child who is marble and “the child who was not marble.” This repetition gives the supernatural relationship the simplicity and gravity of a fairy tale.

Imagery is concrete, sensuous, and strongly visual. The church is transformed by Ernest’s imagination into woodland, ship, playground, prison, and sanctuary. Marble, stone, dust, red fabric, purple storm clouds, black shadows, and golden evening light form a carefully controlled palette. Architectural objects are animated: canopies “brooded,” silence becomes “insistent,” and the church eventually breaks “like a slice of cake.” Similes frequently translate unfamiliar or frightening things into forms available to a child’s experience, while other comparisons carry ironic adult commentary. Thunder and lightning operate both atmospherically and structurally, recurring at moments when the ordinary world becomes permeable to the supernatural.

The tone shifts fluidly among whimsical, satirical, eerie, tender, and tragic registers. The aunts are treated with affectionate mockery rather than cruelty. Their kindness is real but suffocating, and their language of propriety, health, justice, and Providence exposes the limitations of adult interpretation. Comic observations about ruined bonnets, raspberry jam, safe rebellion, and “grown-up crinolined” kindness prevent the fantasy from becoming solemn too early. The Gothic tension develops gradually through small ambiguities—the marble hands seem to move, a familiar child appears in the shrubbery, dreams overlap with waking life—before becoming explicit in the deserted church. Even then, horror remains subordinated to moral choice.

Dialogue is concise and socially differentiated. Ernest’s speech is candid, repetitive, and emotionally transparent; the marble child speaks with calm, grave simplicity; the aunts use corrective, euphemistic, and self-justifying language. In the final anecdotal frame, Aunt Emmeline’s polished reminiscence contrasts with Mrs. Meadows’s dialect. This contrast dramatizes competing explanations: respectable adults reduce the event to illness, shock, naughtiness, and delusion, while the socially less authoritative witness recognizes an angelic intervention. The aunt’s conversation is also gently satirized through the “sympathetic friend,” whose formulaic questions and impatience expose the ritualized nature of the oft-repeated family story.

Structurally, the narrative progresses through a series of increasingly substantial encounters. First Ernest imagines movement; then he sees the child outside the church; then the child joins him in a possibly dreamed or half-dreamed experience; finally the marble figure speaks and embraces him in waking reality. This escalation preserves uncertainty while steadily strengthening the supernatural claim. The climax hinges not on escape or spectacle but on Ernest’s ethical decision to relinquish the companionship he most desires in order to save others. His choice resolves the central conflict between imaginative love and moral duty, while the marble child’s declaration that its true home is in Ernest’s heart converts physical loss into inward permanence.

The ending uses a double perspective. Aunt Emmeline’s retrospective account rationalizes and sentimentalizes the catastrophe, whereas the marble finger provides a compact physical proof that resists her explanation. The closing sentence explicitly distinguishes adult reminiscence from Ernest’s memory, affirming the tale itself as the truer record. Overall, the style is that of a sophisticated literary fairy tale: richly descriptive yet accessible, humorous about adult blindness, deeply sympathetic to childhood loneliness, and willing to leave magic poised between psychological experience, religious miracle, and objective fact.

The story uses an elevated, formal, early-twentieth-century prose style, combining society romance, exotic adventure, and a final revelation shaped like a mystery-story twist. Its atmosphere is established through dense visual description: the firelit library, yellow London fog, monumental architecture, antique furniture, rubies, ivory, vellum, and the dried desert flower create a world of wealth, melancholy, and latent mystery. Settings are not merely decorative but symbolic. The oppressive Park Lane house reflects the heroine’s sense of entrapment, while the distant Gobi represents danger, idealism, and self-transcending purpose.

The narration is third-person and largely focalized through Miss Carstair, moving between external observation and close access to her emotional reasoning. Her dilemma is rendered less through casual introspection than through solemn, generalized statements about marriage, sacrifice, destiny, and romantic idealism. The narrator sometimes broadens her private crisis into aphoristic commentary, especially when describing marriage as the cutting of every thread except the one binding a woman to her mate. This gives the narrative a ceremonious, almost philosophical authority, though its assumptions about gender, nationality, and “East” and “West” are strongly marked by its period.

The diction is ornate and emphatic. Abstract nouns such as “idealism,” “sacrifice,” “romance,” “conception,” “impulse,” and “extremity” recur, often reinforced by intensifiers including “great,” “immense,” “incredible,” “profound,” and “entirely.” Repetition is a major rhetorical device: phrases such as “immense idealism,” “great dreamer,” “profoundly puzzled,” and “extreme care” elevate people and actions toward the legendary. Concrete luxury objects are described with similarly heightened precision—“pigeon-blood rubies,” “ancient crude gold work,” and the “arabesque of silver and tortoise-shell”—so material objects acquire moral and symbolic weight.

Sentence structure alternates between short dramatic declarations and long, carefully articulated periods. Brief paragraphs such as “What should she do?” or “We were fatally misled” function as emphatic beats. Longer sentences accumulate qualifications, parallel clauses, and explanatory phrases, often using semicolons, dashes, and repeated conjunctions. The prose regularly delays its main point, creating an impression of grave deliberation. This pattern is especially pronounced in Dr. Tsan-Sgam’s speech, whose elaborate syntax is presented as the product of mentally translating through several languages. His dialogue sounds precise, ceremonious, and cautiously constructed, with each sentence likened to a mosaic or delicate mechanism.

The story favors painterly tableaux and controlled physical gestures over rapid action. Miss Carstair huddles before the fire, stands with her fingers linked behind her back, touches the flower as though caressing it, and twists the necklace around her hands. Dr. Tsan-Sgam bows, stoops, pauses, passes a hand over his face, and settles awkwardly into a chair. These gestures externalize inward states while preserving social restraint. Emotion is generally held back until it breaks through in tears, exclamations, or sudden movement, producing a melodramatic but disciplined tone.

Imagery is grand, remote, and often quasi-biblical or cosmic. London’s rumble becomes “the distant roar of a world turning in some stellar space”; the heroine’s father appears like “the cloud in the Hebraic Myth”; and his determination is imagined as capable of passing a flaming sword at the entrance to the Gobi. Similes frequently monumentalize their subjects, turning architecture into a mausoleum, age into the slow erosion of a resistant substance, and speech into machinery operating in hidden spaces. Such comparisons make psychological and conversational moments feel mythic.

The narrative is structured around paired symbols and mistaken interpretations. The dried yellow flower signifies Major Carstair’s genuine sacrifice in seeking relief from human pain, while the ruby necklace initially seems to signify Lord Eckhart’s equally magnificent devotion. Miss Carstair’s error lies in reading both objects through the same romantic code. The visitor’s account confirms her father’s idealism but gradually destabilizes her view of Eckhart. Suspense develops through withheld information, repeated cryptic statements—“We had a fear”—and ambiguities surrounding payment, ownership, and sacrifice.

Dialogue is used primarily for revelation rather than everyday social exchange. Characters speak in polished, thematic statements, and questions often open the next layer of exposition. Dr. Tsan-Sgam’s long narrative supplies the adventure backstory, while Miss Carstair’s interruptions clarify its emotional and moral significance. Their conversation becomes a gradual interrogation of appearances: what treasure means, what sacrifice means, and whether gratitude can be mistaken for love. The climactic question, “What sacrifice?”, abruptly punctures the heroine’s romantic construction.

Pacing is slow and cumulative. The opening lingers over room, objects, and emotional context; the middle expands into an extended embedded account of the father’s expedition and medical service; the ending then accelerates through shorter exchanges. The final line delivers a concise reversal: the necklace was not purchased through Lord Eckhart’s ruin but was a gift from the Dalai Lama entrusted to him for delivery. Ending immediately after this disclosure preserves the shock and leaves its consequences unstated.

Overall, the style is luxuriant, solemn, romantic, and suspenseful. It treats private feeling as a matter of destiny and ethical grandeur, surrounds plot information with atmospheric description, and builds toward an ironic exposure of false appearances. To imitate it, one would use formal diction, richly symbolic objects, long reflective passages, ceremonious dialogue, repeated abstract intensifiers, exotic or monumental imagery, and a final economical revelation that overturns the emotional premise established by the preceding narrative.

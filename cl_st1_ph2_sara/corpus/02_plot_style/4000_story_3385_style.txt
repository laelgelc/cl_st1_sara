The story has the profile of a late nineteenth-century comic suspense tale, combining domestic realism, frontier adventure, and a reversal of conventional gender expectations. Its central conceit is established in the opening sentence—a woman who has habitually searched for an imaginary man beneath her bed finally finds one—and the narrative develops this irony by turning a familiar comic fear into a genuine threat. The tone moves between menace and amusement, but comedy remains the governing mode: the burglar is dangerous, yet his encounter with Mrs. Laughton becomes an increasingly absurd contest between physical force and verbal ingenuity.

The narration is third-person and largely aligned with Mrs. Laughton’s consciousness, though an intrusive first-person narrator occasionally appears in phrases such as “this night of which I speak” and “As I said.” This storyteller-like presence gives the piece an oral, anecdotal quality. The narrator is affectionate but gently patronising toward the heroine, repeatedly describing her through diminutives and decorative comparisons: she is a “pretty little person,” a “fairy-queen,” a “rain-lily,” and a “wren.” Such language reflects period conventions of femininity while also sharpening the central irony, since the supposedly fragile woman proves mentally stronger than the armed man towering over her.

The prose alternates between long, elaborately coordinated sentences and short, emphatic questions or declarations. The long sentences are especially prominent during Mrs. Laughton’s initial panic. Clauses accumulate rapidly as she imagines disgrace, financial ruin, death, and harm to the whole enterprise, reproducing the speed and excess of frightened thought. Rhetorical questions—“What was she to do?,” “Scream?,” “Run?”—fragment this flow and make her paralysis immediate. The narrator explicitly compresses psychological time, insisting that a great sequence of thoughts occurs “in the fraction of a second.” Once the confrontation begins, dialogue takes over, shortening the narrative units and accelerating the pace.

Diction is a mixture of elevated literary language, sentimental description, colloquial speech, and frontier slang. The narration uses formal or ornate expressions such as “coward par eminence,” “perfunctory,” and “all but annihilated by terror,” while the intruder speaks in rough dialect: “None of your lip,” “I don’t want no fifteen dollars,” and “What kind of a dumb fool be you?” Mrs. Laughton’s speech occupies a comic middle ground. It is grammatically more refined, but breathless, digressive, and full of apparently irrelevant domestic details about ribbons, sweets, lace, drinks, powder, and tweezers. This contrast in registers helps distinguish class, education, gender, and temperament while making conversation itself the scene of conflict.

Dialogue is the story’s principal dramatic device. Mrs. Laughton survives by controlling its rhythm: she misunderstands deliberately, changes subjects, complains about manners, appeals to sentiment, offers unsolicited moral advice, and overwhelms the burglar with chatter. Her repeated insistence that she is a coward disguises the courage involved in maintaining this performance. Much of the humour comes from incongruity. She treats an armed robber like an awkward visitor, objects to his dirty hands touching her lace, encourages him to swear after he hurts his head, and suggests that he reform, acquire land, and start a family. Her politeness is both socially comic and strategically aggressive.

Characterisation depends on contradiction. Mrs. Laughton is physically weak, genuinely terrified, and socially coded as helpless, yet she is observant, quick-thinking, and capable of improvisation under pressure. Her courage is not represented as fearlessness; it consists in acting while terrified and postponing collapse until the danger has passed. The burglar, by contrast, has weapons and bodily strength but is impatient, literal-minded, superstitious, and vulnerable to surprise. His inability to decide whether she is foolish or “bluffing” becomes the key to her power over him. The story therefore reverses the apparent hierarchy between them without transforming the heroine into a conventional action figure.

The structure is tightly organised around escalation and reversal. The opening supplies the threat; the extended interior monologue establishes the stakes; the rolling away of the bed initiates the confrontation; and successive verbal feints delay the burglar’s search. Physical action is used sparingly but decisively. The falling desk lid creates a slapstick interruption and allows Mrs. Laughton to conceal the money, while the invented Apache provides the final bluff that drives the intruder away. The conclusion delivers a comic aftershock: once Tom returns and she revives, her first question preserves the fiction she created and reveals how fully she inhabited it.

Imagery repeatedly juxtaposes vulnerability and confinement with open frontier space. Indoors, there are beds, drawers, closets, valances, locked doors, and hidden money; outdoors lies the moonlit prairie, remote works, desperadoes, Apaches, and escape. Animal and miniature imagery makes Mrs. Laughton seem delicate—a snail, chicken, hen-sparrow, wren—while mechanical imagery conveys extreme physical strain: Tom’s heart beats “like a trip-hammer,” and the robber’s eyes resemble “augers turning to live coals.” Domestic objects become tactical instruments, so the feminine interior is not merely a place of vulnerability but a terrain the heroine knows how to manipulate.

The humour is affectionate, ironic, and occasionally satirical. It targets masculine assumptions about female foolishness, sentimental stereotypes of delicate women, the melodramatic mythology of the frontier, and the burglar’s expectation that violence will produce immediate obedience. At the same time, the story retains period attitudes in its diminutive treatment of the heroine and its use of the Apache as a frightening racialised figure, even though that figure is imaginary within the plot. The resulting style is theatrical and highly performative: suspense is sustained through threatened violence, but resolution comes through language, social acting, and psychological misdirection rather than combat.

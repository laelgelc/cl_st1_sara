The story is written as a first-person social reminiscence, with an observant, worldly narrator who participates in events while also standing slightly apart from them. His voice is urbane, conversational, and self-aware. He openly admits his own mischievousness, prejudices, and mistaken judgments, which makes him engaging without presenting him as infallible. The retrospective viewpoint allows early comic impressions to be quietly revised by later events: people initially treated as shallow social types acquire emotional depth, while the narrator’s amused detachment gives way to sympathy.

The dominant tone is one of dry, polished irony. Much of the humour comes from understatement, incongruous comparison, mock solemnity, and epigrammatic social criticism. Hunting becomes an elaborate operation for killing “twelve-and-sixpence worth of poultry”; Central Africa resembles Kew Gardens; China is reduced to a “cross between a willow-pattern plate and a New York slum.” Society is described through similarly compressed paradoxes, as when Gerty goes “to Kensington for her religion and to Mayfair for her morals.” Such lines imitate the clever, dismissive speech of fashionable circles while also exposing their superficiality. The humour is frequently self-directed: the narrator calls himself “persevering” where his nurse had called him “wearing,” and acknowledges that conventional phrases come to him without “the trouble of composition.”

Dialogue is central to the style. The opening relies on short exchanges in which the narrator’s aggressive cheerfulness meets Billy’s clipped, languid replies. Elisions such as “Mornin’” and “‘Fraid so” establish fashionable affectation and social pose, while Billy’s longer responses reveal his intelligence through weary, aphoristic wit. Later dialogue becomes warmer, simpler, and less performative as love and illness strip the characters of their public manners. This change is especially marked in the domestic scenes, where phrases such as “I would rather stop with you, dear” contrast with the brittle cleverness of the Club. Speech therefore functions not only as characterization but as a measure of sincerity.

The diction mixes late-Victorian upper-class vocabulary with colloquial idiom and journalistic social observation. Clubs, broughams, “At Homes,” Mayfair dressmakers, fashionable theatres, titles, and named cultural figures create a densely specific metropolitan world. Slang such as “got mashed,” “lucky dog,” and “by Jove” gives the narration period immediacy. Social labels and full honorific names are used humorously: the expansion of “Blasé Billy” into “The Hon. William Cecil Wychwood Stanley Drayton” emphasizes the absurd weight of rank and convention. Parenthetical qualifications, mock definitions, and knowing asides reinforce the sense of a narrator speaking to an audience familiar with the codes he describes.

Sentence structure alternates between brisk comic statements and long, carefully balanced periods. The longer sentences accumulate parallel details, often building toward a satirical climax. Lists are particularly characteristic: Billy knows women’s pasts, men’s futures, the origins of crests, the prices of baronetcies, and the houses in which soap or political honesty would be socially inappropriate. These catalogues produce rhythm, abundance, and comic escalation. Antithesis is equally important, as in the contrast between saying foolish things and doing wise ones, or between being “a good wife but bad company.” The prose often turns on a final clause that revises or punctures everything preceding it.

Characterization begins through caricature and social taxonomy. Billy is introduced as the blasé man of fashion, Gerty as the perfectly dressed and perfectly derivative society woman, and the captain as a conventional smoking-room cynic. The narrator summarizes people through sharp formulations rather than detailed psychological analysis. Yet the narrative gradually undermines these formulas. Billy’s boredom conceals an unsuspected capacity for devotion, and Gerty’s apparent vanity conceals loyalty, grief, and moral independence. Their transformation is not achieved by abandoning their established traits: fashion, performance, and social appearance remain central to them, but those traits are redirected by love. Gerty’s elaborate gowns become costumes in a private act of mercy rather than instruments of public display.

The plot has a clear tonal movement from comedy of manners to domestic pathos. Its first portion consists largely of witty sketches and anecdotes, creating a leisurely portrait of fashionable London. The love story then introduces gentle farce: hidden kisses, deliberately forgotten gloves, sentimental theatre-going, and lovers caught embracing in the dusk. Illness darkens the same motif of theatricality. Gerty must pretend to attend society functions, dressing for imaginary audiences so that her dying husband will not feel guilty. The story’s central irony is that a woman formerly defined by social performance becomes most sincere while acting a false part.

The narration handles this transition without entirely surrendering its comic restraint. Even at its most emotional, it avoids prolonged lyrical lament. Pathos arises from concrete visual contrasts: the glittering evening dress and haggard face, the diamonds seen in a darkened room, and the grieving woman slipping “thief-like” through her own door. The narrator’s decision to turn toward the window rather than describe an overt emotional response preserves decorum and makes the feeling more effective. Humour also persists in Gerty’s remark that Billy was never much of a judge of art, allowing tenderness and irony to coexist.

Social satire supplies the story’s broader thematic framework. Fashionable society is governed by appearances, rehearsed opinions, invitations, reputation, and fear of seeming “odd.” Its members pride themselves on sophistication yet interpret visible behaviour crudely. Seeing Gerty dressed for supposed entertainments, they conclude that she is heartlessly deserting her dying husband. Their judgment is plausible from the outside, but wholly false. This dramatic irony exposes both the cruelty of gossip and the limits of social knowledge. The repeated distinction between public and secret life culminates in Gerty’s refusal to defend herself because explanation would make private love into public material.

The ending is concise and epigrammatic. Gerty’s question, “Does it matter very much what they think?”, reverses everything previously established about her. The narrator’s final observation emphasizes the irony by identifying her through the very social title and maiden identity she has transcended. Rather than explicitly stating a moral, the story closes on astonishment at inward change. Its overall style combines club-room wit, social caricature, theatrical plotting, sentimental feeling, and restrained moral insight, using irony first to diminish its characters and then, more significantly, to reveal how inadequate such diminishment has been.

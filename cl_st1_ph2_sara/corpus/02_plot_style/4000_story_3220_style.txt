The story adopts a mock-serious, satirical style in which an ostensibly reasonable narrator applies absurd logic with unwavering confidence. Its comedy depends on the contrast between elevated exposition and trivial subject matter: a petty rivalry between provincial newspaper editors is narrated as though it were an epic conflict involving wisdom, honour, chivalry and public catastrophe. The narrator is intrusive, digressive and knowingly unreliable, repeatedly qualifying details with phrases such as “as nearly as I can recollect” while claiming to have “shown” conclusions that rest on transparent non sequiturs. This voice creates sustained verbal irony, flattering Bullet-head as “wise,” “great” and “infallible” while steadily exposing his vanity, irritability and obstinacy.

The diction ranges deliberately across registers. Latinate and ceremonious language—“collateral proof,” “philippic,” “legitimate object,” “decidedly enunciated”—sits beside slang, oaths, newspaper jargon and comic dialect. Grandiloquent descriptions inflate mundane acts, while printers and apprentices speak in heavily phonetic vernacular: “there beant an o,” “paragrab,” “wery,” and “consekvence.” The dialect is caricatural rather than realistic, serving to differentiate social roles and intensify the farce. Editorial prose is itself parodied through bloated formality, excessive parenthetical clarification, repeated pronouns and affected classical allusions. Names and titles are similarly comic: “Touch-and-go Bullet-head,” “The Tea-Pot,” and the unwieldy “Alexander-the-Great-o-nopolis” turn nomenclature into a source of ridicule.

Sentences are often long, cumulative and syntactically overburdened, with clauses interrupted by dashes, parentheses, qualifications and self-corrections. Short exclamations then puncture this elaboration: “What!”, “Perish so vile a thought!”, or “So much for Buckingham!” Repetition is central to the rhythm, particularly the recursive use of names, titles and pronouns. The narrator frequently delays a simple point through circumlocution, creating comic suspense and mimicking the self-importance of editorial argument. Punctuation is conspicuous and theatrical: dashes, exclamation marks, quotation marks, italics-by-implication and abrupt interruptions reproduce agitation, oral emphasis and typographical bustle.

The humour is overwhelmingly linguistic. It progresses from logical fallacy and editorial invective to letter-based wordplay, lipogrammatic parody and typographical corruption. The repeated “O” becomes simultaneously a vowel, an exclamation, a circular shape, a sign of debt in the pun on “owes,” and the organising principle of Bullet-head’s extravagantly “O-wy” attack. When every “o” is replaced with “x,” the printed page itself becomes the climax: typography ceases to be a neutral vehicle and becomes the comic event. The closing sequence extends the device through a rapid series of puns—“X-ellent,” “X-entric,” “X-press,” “X-traordinary”—culminating in mathematical and alcoholic meanings of X. Sound effects are equally important, with rhyme, internal rhyme, alliteration, animal noises and accumulative insults giving the O-filled paragraph the quality of a taunting comic chant.

Structurally, the story follows escalating cause and effect. A new editor arrives, initiates a feud, receives a stylistic insult, overreacts by composing an extreme demonstration, and is defeated by a mechanical accident engineered through the print shop. Each stage magnifies the previous absurdity. Expository passages establish the mock-heroic frame; quoted newspaper articles dramatise the rivalry; print-shop dialogue shifts the comedy into low farce; and the corrupted article provides a visual set piece. The ending disperses the joke through multiple townspeople’s interpretations rather than resolving the editor’s disappearance, allowing the verbal conceit to proliferate after the plot has effectively concluded.

Characterisation is broad and caricatural. Bullet-head is defined by wounded authorship, inflated dignity and a refusal to retreat; Smith functions as the cool provocateur whose pun triggers the disaster; Bob is a mischievous, literal-minded apprentice whose practical solution unknowingly destroys the editor’s performance. The community behaves as a volatile comic mob, ready to interpret typographical nonsense as treason and to punish someone before understanding it. These figures are less psychologically developed individuals than embodiments of vanity, rivalry, credulity and professional incompetence.

The overall tone is exuberant, mischievous and mercilessly burlesque. Its principal targets are editorial egotism, combative journalism, provincial sensationalism, pedantry and the tendency to mistake verbal display for intellectual substance. Beneath the farce lies a fascination with the material conditions of writing: type cases, missing letters, compositors, copy deadlines and substitutions all determine what readers finally receive. The story’s distinctive style therefore combines mock scholarship, tall-tale exaggeration, theatrical narration, grotesque dialect, typographical experimentation and densely layered punning, making language both the instrument and the object of the satire.

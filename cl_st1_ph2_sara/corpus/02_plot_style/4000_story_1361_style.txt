The story’s style is comic-realist, theatrical, and gently satirical, combining an expansive nineteenth-century narrative manner with sharply differentiated vernacular dialogue. Its central stylistic energy comes from the contrast between Colonel Starbottle’s grandiose conception of himself and the comparatively mundane, even absurd, circumstances around him. Legal practice, romantic grievance, Southern chivalry, temperance, duelling, and public rhetoric are all treated as forms of performance. The comedy is affectionate rather than wholly destructive: the Colonel is vain, bombastic, impulsive, and professionally dubious, but he is also courageous, courteous, loyal, and genuinely moved by his own ideals.

The narrator is an urbane, conspicuously present storyteller who alternates between omniscient access, social observation, and direct personal commentary. Phrases such as “I prefer not to speak” and “I am not sure” give the narration the air of an amused raconteur rather than an invisible recorder. This voice often adopts mock-legal or mock-historical gravity, reporting ridiculous developments with elaborate seriousness. It also slips freely into the Colonel’s perspective, exposing his rationalisations while preserving some sympathy for him. Irony commonly arises from the distance between what the Colonel believes, what other characters infer, and what the narrator allows the reader to understand. The crowd’s certainty that a woman’s note must be a challenge, for example, satirises the heroic mythology surrounding him.

The prose is syntactically elaborate, with long, balanced sentences, semicolons, parenthetical qualifications, and accumulative clauses. Descriptions frequently build through triads and parallel structures, as in the initial division of the Colonel’s triumph into personality, oratory, and legal function. The sentences mimic both forensic argument and after-dinner storytelling: they postpone conclusions, insert comic qualifications, and arrive at understated punch lines. Dashes are especially prominent. In narration they create qualifications or comic turns; in dialogue they represent hesitation, interruption, self-correction, and performative pauses. Exclamation marks, rhetorical questions, and italic-like verbal emphasis contribute to the story’s heightened oral quality.

The diction moves between elevated, Latinate formality and colloquial American speech. The narrator and the Colonel favour words and phrases such as “oratorical abilities,” “patriotic exaltation,” “documentary evidence,” “sacred precincts,” and “usages of advocacy.” Zaidee and her mother speak in compressed regional vernacular—“maw,” “darter,” “kin,” “reckon,” “ketch,” “nary,” “goin’,” and “suthing.” Their grammar and pronunciation are represented phonetically but remain highly readable. This linguistic opposition is a major source of humour. The women repeatedly puncture the Colonel’s elaborate circumlocutions with blunt practical demands, especially their insistence on “damages.” Zaidee’s plain language is not evidence of intellectual simplicity: her penetrating looks and economical replies repeatedly reveal that she understands more than the verbose Colonel does.

Dialogue is the principal instrument of characterisation. Starbottle’s speech consists of fragments from a larger imagined oration, full of “er,” ceremonial titles, euphemisms, and self-dramatising pauses. Even ordinary conversation becomes a public address. His language is also evasive: he calls a duel “personal satisfaction,” money “compensation,” and a professional conflict “an affair of some delicacy.” His recurrent “ha!” functions almost like a verbal flourish or stage gesture. Hotchkiss’s speech is terse, impatient, moralistic, and defensive, while Mrs. Hooker’s is practical and transactional. Zaidee begins in a “monotonous” recital but acquires force through timing, understatement, and sudden shifts into enthusiasm. Her brief answers—“Hymn-books,” “I ate it,” and “He just quit”—serve as deadpan comic beats after the Colonel’s elaborate questions.

Character description is visual, gestural, and emblematic. The Colonel is repeatedly assembled out of costume and props: blue frock coat, spotless shirt frill, white trousers, varnished boots, Byron collar, Panama hat, gold watch, whiskey bottle, and sword-stick. These details make him seem both vividly corporeal and historically obsolete. His florid face, breathlessness, perspiration, and “fat but white and well-kept hand” undercut his heroic pose without entirely cancelling it. Recurrent acts of buttoning, bowing, strutting, coughing, waving his hand, and adjusting his clothes give him a repertoire of theatrical stage business. His antiquated dress corresponds to his antiquated code of honour, so external detail continuously reinforces moral and psychological character.

Zaidee is constructed through a deliberate contrast between apparent innocence and unexplained knowledge. Her plain clothes, downcast face, parasol, blue ribbons, and childlike figure suggest demure rustic youth, but her eyes are described as possessing adult penetration and “prescience.” The narration repeatedly withholds a precise explanation of what those eyes express, instead recording their destabilising effect on the Colonel. This gives her a faintly enigmatic, almost bewitching quality. Her parasol is also used as stage business: she traces patterns, punctuates words with it, and readdresses it whenever she wishes to retreat into demureness. The story therefore makes gesture as meaningful as speech.

The humour relies on incongruity, repetition, reversal, and understatement. Lofty ideals are applied to trivial or compromising situations: the language of sacred psalmody becomes a clandestine courtship code, a peppermint lozenge becomes romantic evidence, and a breach-of-promise action is treated as though it ought to be settled by bloodshed. Repeated formulas acquire comic force, particularly “That’s what maw said,” the Colonel’s apologetic “er,” and the escalating distinction between marriage, damages, and personal satisfaction. Objects often provide punch lines: the eaten lozenge eliminates evidence; the walking stick becomes a concealed sword; the bullet-marked watch and dead partner’s name turn the Colonel’s office into a museum of his own legend.

The satire targets several overlapping social codes. Legal advocacy is shown as emotional manipulation rather than disciplined reasoning: the Colonel wins by invoking the American eagle, Thomas Jefferson, and political outrage in a dispute about a ditch company. Southern chivalry is portrayed as noble in aspiration but absurdly maladapted to civil litigation. Masculine honour converts every insult into a possible duel, while supposed reverence for women coexists with vanity and romantic self-deception. Religious austerity is also treated sceptically through Hotchkiss, whose rigid public identity makes the alleged courtship both unlikely and comically scandalous. Yet the story avoids reducing these systems to simple hypocrisy; it is interested in how people sincerely inhabit theatrical social roles.

Pacing alternates between broad narrative summary and extended scenic dialogue. The opening rapidly establishes the Colonel’s public reputation, after which the narrative slows for the interviews with Mrs. Hooker, Zaidee, and Hotchkiss. These scenes unfold almost like a stage comedy, with entrances, exits, props, physical reactions, pauses, and reversals. Backstory is supplied economically between scenes, particularly through the Colonel’s inquiries and the account of his office arrangements. Suspense is not primarily about factual danger but about interpretation: whether Zaidee’s claim is true, what Hotchkiss fears, what the Colonel’s motives are, and how a seemingly hopeless case might be transformed by rhetoric.

The setting is evoked through institutions and manners more than through landscape. Courtroom, saloon, law office, church, crossroads, and ranch form a compact social world in which reputation travels quickly. References to the State of California, Missouri, Kentucky, Georgia, New Orleans, slavery, duelling, and antebellum Southern etiquette place the story within a culturally mixed American frontier environment. The language preserves historically specific attitudes, including romanticised references to the Colonel’s former enslaved servant and to a social order already described as “antique in the republic.” This historical distance is central to the tone: Starbottle appears as a surviving embodiment of an older code operating in a more commercial, legalistic society.

Overall, the style is richly oral, episodic, and character-centred. It blends regional colour, social comedy, courtroom satire, romantic ambiguity, and affectionate caricature. Its signature technique is to place inflated rhetoric beside plain fact, then allow neither wholly to defeat the other. The Colonel’s imagination continually falsifies or embellishes reality, but it also gives reality vitality, drama, and moral colour. The result is a prose style at once ceremonious and mischievous, in which speech, clothing, gesture, and social ritual reveal character more powerfully than direct psychological explanation.

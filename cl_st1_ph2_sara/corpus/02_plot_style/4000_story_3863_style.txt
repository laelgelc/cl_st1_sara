The story uses a first-person confessional voice that is intimate, self-conscious and deliberately unreliable. The narrator addresses the reader directly, repeatedly pausing to explain, qualify or correct himself, so that the account feels like an urgent personal testimony written during a crisis. His claim that he is losing his identity shapes the narration itself: the prose gradually acquires the theatrical mannerisms he condemns. This creates a strong comic discrepancy between his continued awareness of his absurdity and his inability to behave—or eventually write—otherwise.

The prevailing tone is mock-serious, combining psychological alarm with farce. The narrator describes a ridiculous occupational “infection” in the language of illness, possession and personal extinction, treating exaggerated stage behaviour as though it were a genuine degenerative disease. Elevated expressions such as “to my own destruction,” “under a curse” and “obliterated” sit beside mundane details about razors, tickets, tailoring and office cigars. This collision between melodramatic intensity and ordinary circumstances generates much of the humour. The comedy remains tinged with pathos, however, because the narrator loses his fiancée, his social ease and his sense of self.

The diction mixes late-Victorian literary formality with journalistic briskness, theatrical jargon and colloquial asides. Words such as “histrionic,” “flamboyant,” “ineffably,” “susceptibility” and “indisposition” lend the narration an educated, slightly pompous register, while clipped remarks such as “Virgin soil,” “Dear Boy” and “Did you think it was a treat?” introduce conversational energy. References to contemporary writers, periodicals, actors, museums and theatrical conventions place the story in a recognisably metropolitan literary world. Biblical and Shakespearean allusions are used partly for cultural texture and partly for comic inflation.

Sentence structure is highly variable. Long, cumulative sentences catalogue theatrical gestures and emotions, often building through parallel phrases: actors glare, smite their chests, fling out their fingers, laugh, fall and die. These accumulations reproduce the excess they satirise. Elsewhere the prose breaks into fragments, abrupt exclamations, questions, dashes, ellipses and parenthetical remarks. The punctuation gives the voice a performed quality, indicating hesitations, sudden poses and emotional changes. Italics mimic vocal emphasis or “vocal italics,” while repeated words and syllabic distortions—such as “Pit—y me!”—parody stage delivery. The narrator explicitly notices this stylistic transformation, making the prose metatextual: its form becomes evidence of the condition being described.

Dialogue is fast, economical and strongly characterising. Barnaby speaks in terse commands, interruptions and evasions, giving him an overwhelming comic authority; the narrator’s objections are repeatedly cut off before they can develop. Delia’s plain, restrained speech contrasts with the narrator’s increasingly ornate performance. Her unemotional directness exposes his artificiality more effectively than any explicit authorial judgment. Much of the dialogue is arranged as rapid exchange, producing the timing of stage comedy even while the story mocks theatrical convention.

The narrative follows a clear retrospective progression from an opening declaration of crisis, through an account of the narrator’s former personality, to his appointment as dramatic critic, the first symptoms of imitation, the collapse of his engagement and the prospect of complete transformation. Early hints—his borrowed stutter, yielding disposition and susceptibility to impressions—prepare the central development. Repetition reinforces the causal chain: he repeatedly stresses that he was quiet, inexperienced and unusually impressionable. The ending is circular rather than conclusive. He still wishes to resign, but Barnaby’s evasions ensure that the process will continue, leaving him suspended between resistance and surrender.

A central technique is sustained ironic inversion. The dramatic critic knows nothing about theatre; his ignorance is treated by his editor as a qualification; repeated exposure makes him less capable of ordinary human expression; and the only place where his unnatural behaviour might be accepted is the stage itself. The story also parodies professional criticism, journalistic rhetoric, melodrama and anxieties about social imitation. Its governing conceit—that style and behaviour are contagious—operates both as comic fantasy and as a satirical observation about identity. Overall, the style is urbane, digressive, allusive and theatrically animated, with a controlled blend of deadpan narration, verbal caricature, self-reflexive irony and escalating absurdity.

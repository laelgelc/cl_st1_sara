The story adopts an elevated, archaic, folkloric mode that resembles a medieval Irish legend retold with the polish of a literary fairy tale. Its language combines concrete physical detail with mythic allusion, placing ordinary discomforts—damp fuel, mouldy bread, dirty water, fleas—within a world populated imaginatively by Druids, merrows, saints, demons, legendary heroes and supernatural rulers. Irish place-names, genealogies and named figures from native tradition give the narrative a strongly local, antiquarian texture. The diction is formal and deliberately old-fashioned, using constructions such as “thereupon,” “mayhap,” “lest,” “bade,” “unto,” and “well-nigh,” together with ceremonial epithets including “the Swift, Wild Horse,” “Aengus the Subtle-hearted,” and “White-breasted Deirdre.”

The narration is third-person and broadly omniscient, but remains close to Cumhal’s sensations, fears and imaginative associations. Character is established through emblematic description rather than psychological analysis: Cumhal’s thin hair, pale face, parti-coloured doublet, pointed shoes and bulging wallet identify him immediately as a wandering performer, while the abbot’s nightcap, tonsure and concern for his reputation reduce ecclesiastical authority to a grotesquely comic image. Figures often function as types—the gleeman, the abbot, the lay brother, the friars, the beggars—yet distinctive speech and visual details prevent them from becoming wholly abstract.

The syntax is strongly cumulative. Long sentences link actions and perceptions through repeated coordinating conjunctions, especially “and,” producing the cadence of oral storytelling or scriptural chronicle. Lists, parallel clauses and repeated formulas control the rhythm: the defective sods, bread, water, foot-water and blanket are first experienced, then catalogued in complaint, then reported again to the abbot. This patterned repetition gives the prose both comic momentum and an air of inevitability. Transitions such as “Then,” “And,” “So,” and “Another half-mile on the way” make the action feel sequential, ritualised and inexorably directed toward the final crucifixion.

Dialogue is theatrical, rhetorical and highly stylised. Cumhal speaks in curses, apostrophes, extravagant comparisons and self-defining metaphors, while the religious characters use solemn logic to justify cruelty. Their bureaucratic calm creates much of the story’s black comedy: the abbot worries not about injustice but about the possibility that a well-made curse will circulate among children, spinning girls and robbers. Repetition within dialogue sharpens the satire, particularly when the same groups and legendary names recur in incantatory lists. Speech frequently resembles public performance rather than private conversation, fitting a world in which poetry has social power and reputation can be damaged by memorable rhyme.

The tone moves between grotesque comedy, lyrical wonder, moral satire and stark tragedy. Early scenes amplify petty misery to comic proportions, but their exaggeration prepares for institutional violence. The friars’ cruelty is presented in a flat, almost administrative manner, making it more disturbing. Their piety is inseparable from self-interest, fear of mockery and hostility toward imaginative freedom. Irony is pervasive: the supposed guardians of charity offer foul hospitality; the lay brother who condemns immoral tales knows them by heart; the young friars enjoy Cumhal’s tricks, jokes and songs before punishing him for awakening their suppressed desires; and the beggars, themselves marginalised, abandon another outcast.

Imagery frequently draws on landscape, weather, animals and water. Souls are compared to wind, faces to a flooded lake, hair to an island in a pond, and beauty to a bursting dawn. Such similes are expansive and geographically rooted, often turning emotion into a feature of the Irish landscape. Birds and wolves form a recurring ominous pattern. The story opens with Cumhal imagining their assault upon crucified bodies and ends with that fear literally fulfilled, giving the narrative a circular, prophetic structure. The grass-barnacles overhead briefly offer a contrasting image of freedom, migration and the “ungovernable sea,” suggesting the liberated existence for which his wandering soul longs.

The plot is episodic and processional. Cumhal arrives at the abbey, protests its hospitality, is condemned, and is led by stages toward execution. His successive performances—juggling, jesting and singing—delay the journey while displaying the range of his art. Each performance momentarily captivates the younger friars, then provokes shame and violence, revealing art’s ability to release forbidden delight, memory and longing. The three-part sequence has the rhythm of folklore, while the construction and carrying of his own cross invoke Christian passion imagery in an intentionally subversive way. Cumhal, condemned by religious authorities, comes to resemble the sacred victim whose faith those authorities profess.

The central stylistic contrast is between enclosure and wandering. The monastery represents roofs, walls, rules, order and coercion; Cumhal represents roads, sea-edges, wind, appetite, song and unstable inspiration. His declaration that his soul is “like the wind among the salley gardens” condenses the story’s ideal of artistic identity: mobile, unruly, receptive and impossible to domesticate. His final speech about the “towered city” and the ideal woman in his heart raises his poverty into visionary exile. Material deprivation is reinterpreted as the suffering produced by a disparity between the coarse visible world and the splendour imagined within.

Overall, the prose is lyrical but not delicate, symbolic but grounded in bodily discomfort and violence. It uses archaic cadence, folkloric repetition, lavish naming, rhetorical dialogue and nature imagery to create a mythic satire of institutional religion and social conformity. Its characteristic emotional effect comes from combining whimsical extravagance with remorseless brutality: art is charming, ridiculous and socially disruptive, yet also the source of truth, freedom and spiritual dignity. The closing question—an outcast appealing to other outcasts—ends the tale not with resolution but with bitter paradox, completing its movement from comic complaint to universal abandonment.

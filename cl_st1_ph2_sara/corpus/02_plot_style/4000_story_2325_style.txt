The story uses a first-person, retrospective narrator whose voice is informal, observant, and implicated in the prejudices of the working men around him. He speaks as one member of a close-knit itinerant community rather than as an authoritative outsider, frequently shifting from “I” to the collective “we.” This communal perspective is central to the effect: O’Briar is initially described through camp gossip, suspicion, and contradictory judgments, while the narrator remains largely unaware of how unfair those judgments are. Mitchell, by contrast, functions as the sharper observer, and the Oracle supplies a laconic moral authority.

The diction is colloquial and strongly marked by Australian working-class speech. Occupational and regional terms such as “swag,” “graft,” “shouted,” “yarn,” “job,” and “T’othersiders” establish the labouring milieu without explanatory exposition. Dialogue is rendered phonetically and idiomatically, especially in the Irish-inflected speech of the constable and the imagined O’Connor family: “Av coorse,” “dacent,” and “ould woman.” Contractions, slang, dry understatement, and expressions such as “I’m dashed if I can” give the narration an oral, anecdotal quality. Capitalisation and quotation marks are occasionally used for comic or thematic emphasis, as in “Case” and “ALF THINKS ALOUD.”

The prose is generally plain, economical, and conversational, but it often develops through long, coordinated sentences that accumulate qualifications and comic reversals. Parallel constructions expose the irrationality of group opinion: if O’Briar works less, he is a loafer; if he works harder, he is contemptible for that too. Parenthetical comments and interruptions reproduce the rhythm of someone telling a story aloud, while clipped exchanges accelerate scenes and make punch lines land cleanly. Repetition is also important, particularly the Oracle’s “I’ve heerd of such cases before,” which begins as a claim of worldly knowledge and ends as a restrained acknowledgment of human privacy.

The narrative is structured as a comic mystery. Early references to O’Briar’s mimicry and ventriloquism quietly foreshadow the solution, while the reported voices in his tent encourage both the characters and the reader to infer a clandestine affair. Suspense grows through successive overheard episodes, the constable’s warning, and the camp’s concern for its respectability. The extended scene outside the tent delays revelation by presenting an apparently complete domestic drama in dialogue alone. The visual discovery of O’Briar lying by himself then creates the central surprise, after which Mitchell’s blunt explanation retrospectively reorganises every preceding detail.

Humour arises chiefly from dramatic irony, social satire, and deadpan delivery. The men consider themselves loyal and respectable, yet they spy, gossip, moralise, and repeatedly misread a vulnerable companion. Mitchell’s nudges, the narrator’s kicks, and their whispered interpretations provide low physical comedy, while the Oracle’s strategic deafness quietly mocks officious moral concern. The humour is affectionate rather than cruel, but it exposes the group’s readiness to manufacture a scandal from incomplete evidence.

Characterisation is indirect and economical. O’Briar’s hidden emotional life emerges not through conventional confession but through his ventriloquised fantasy of reunion, acceptance, and marriage. His imagined conversation reveals loneliness, thrift, disappointment, and hope beneath the “vacant grin” assigned to him by others. Mitchell is defined by patience, scepticism, and understated sympathy; the Oracle by fatalism, generosity, and an instinctive respect for boundaries. The narrator is comparatively obtuse, serving as both witness and comic foil.

The tone moves from matey amusement and suspicion to tenderness and discretion. Beneath the anecdotal surface, the story examines loneliness, masculine reserve, class fellowship, and the danger of judging a person whose inner life is inaccessible. Its final emotional effect depends on restraint: no explicit sentimental explanation is offered, and O’Briar never learns that his private hopes have been overheard. The farewell, Mitchell’s earnest handshake, and the Oracle’s final remark convert the comic revelation into a quiet affirmation of sympathy, dignity, and the right to keep one’s deepest feelings private.

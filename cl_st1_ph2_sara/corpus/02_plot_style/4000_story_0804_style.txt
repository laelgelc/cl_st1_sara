The story uses an omniscient third-person narrator whose perspective stays close to Father Brown while retaining the freedom to describe other characters, supply background, and make broad observations about human nature. The narrative voice is confident, urbane, and conspicuously interpretive. It does not merely record appearances but turns them into judgments, paradoxes, and miniature essays: Father Brown is described as “made of two men,” rooms are brighter than the morning outside, and apparent madness becomes evidence of sanity. This habit of framing people and events through contradiction is central to the style.

The prose is elaborate, rhythmically varied, and often built from long, balanced sentences containing semicolons, dashes, parentheses, qualifications, and parallel clauses. Extended descriptive passages alternate with short declarative lines that sharpen a revelation or joke. Repetition is frequently used for cadence and emphasis, as in successive formulations of what a character saw, heard, or feared. The syntax often imitates a mind testing possibilities: observations are introduced, revised, compared, and then provisionally explained. Parenthetical remarks allow the narrator to insert irony, moral commentary, or comic correction without breaking the flow.

The diction blends formal, literary English with concrete domestic detail and occasional colloquial speech. Abstract terms such as “reflection,” “morbidity,” “communion,” and “consequences” coexist with pyjamas, cruet-stands, dustbins, mustard, pepper, and soda-water. This collision between the metaphysical and the mundane is characteristic. Exotic or Gothic vocabulary appears in Cray’s account of India—“idol,” “hieroglyphic,” “curse,” “temple,” “tortured”—but is ultimately displaced by ordinary mechanical explanations involving razors, gas, boomerangs, emetics, and table condiments.

Description is visual, figurative, and strongly caricatural. Mist, light, trees, and houses emerge in stages, producing an atmosphere of revelation before the mystery has even begun. Similes are vivid and sometimes deliberately incongruous: trees appear drawn first in chalk and then charcoal; a hat resembles an inappropriate halo; sorrow erodes a face as wind erodes a Greek temple. Characters are rendered through memorable physical contrasts—bald against black-haired, broad against lean, scarlet pyjamas against white, comic solidity against nervous elegance. Faces, clothes, posture, and gesture carry psychological and moral implications. The descriptions often approach Dickensian grotesque, but the exaggeration remains affectionate rather than purely satirical.

The tone continually shifts among mystery, comedy, Gothic dread, social satire, and moral seriousness. Suspense is repeatedly punctured by puns and bathos, especially around the burglar’s sneeze, the revolver being “a thing to be sneezed at,” and the portable cruet-stand. Yet the humour does not destroy the danger; instead, comic objects become crucial evidence and even instruments of rescue. The result is a distinctive tonal doubleness in which absurdity and mortal peril occupy the same scene.

Dialogue is sharply individualized and performs much of the characterization. Putnam speaks with hearty, soothing common sense; Cray is feverish, abrupt, and melodramatic; Audrey is practical and severe; Oman is polished and guarded; Father Brown is mild, literal, teasing, and unexpectedly authoritative. Brown’s speech often works through understated questions, homely analogies, theological allusions, or apparently irrelevant jokes. His climactic command—“Drink that!”—is effective because it violently interrupts his usual diffidence and garrulity. Dialogue also conceals motives: characters discuss burglary, madness, food, and church while the true conflict of jealousy and attempted murder remains unstated.

The plot follows a classical clue-based detective structure. It opens with an anomalous sensory event, expands into a domestic mystery, introduces a supernatural explanation, and then supplies a chain of apparently unrelated past incidents. Clues are planted conspicuously but disguised by comedy or social detail: the peculiar echo, the sneeze, the dustbin, the missing cruet, the poison book, the doctor’s backward glances, the boomerangs, and Father Brown’s refusal to leave. The story delays interpretation by allowing the reader to share Cray’s fear of an occult conspiracy, then reverses that interpretation through material causes. The final solution is delivered rapidly after a dramatic intervention, with each supernatural “curse” translated into a practical weapon.

Pacing depends on alternation. Slow atmospheric description and leisurely social observation establish the setting; Cray’s embedded narrative lengthens and darkens the middle; the lunch scene converts accumulated clues into immediate danger; and the ending contracts into concise explanation. Digressions about cookery, clothing, religious practice, and manners seem ornamental but often conceal functional evidence. The narration therefore rewards attention to details that initially look comic or incidental.

Father Brown’s detective method is psychological and moral as much as forensic. He notices physical evidence, but his decisive insight comes from understanding desire: Cray wants to be proved wrong, Oman’s uneasiness reflects conscience, and Putnam’s geniality masks possessive jealousy. The story distrusts crude oppositions between sanity and madness, East and West, superstition and rationality, appearance and reality. In particular, it exposes the racialized fantasy of an exotic Eastern curse as camouflage for “the trick of a white man.” Evil is not remote, occult, or culturally alien; it is domestic, familiar, and motivated by ordinary passion.

Religious imagery and language are woven into the prose without turning it into overt sermonizing. Mass, church bells, fasting, mustard-seed, anointing oil, vinegar, and the Crucifixion all appear within the mechanics of the mystery. Sacred references coexist with jokes, and commonplace objects acquire symbolic as well as practical significance. Father Brown’s humility is similarly double-edged: his shabby, absent-minded manner makes him socially negligible, while his spiritual and psychological attentiveness gives him authority.

Overall, the style is best characterized as a literate comic-Gothic detective mode: ornate but controlled prose, paradoxical commentary, grotesque portraiture, atmospheric symbolism, witty dialogue, fair-play clue planting, and a moralized rational solution. Its signature effect comes from making the ordinary look mysterious and the mysterious turn out to be ordinary, without suggesting that ordinary human motives are therefore simple or harmless.

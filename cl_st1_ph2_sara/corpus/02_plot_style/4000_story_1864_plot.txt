In 1629, the silent, solemn, and chronically indecisive Wouter Van Twiller becomes governor of New Amsterdam. His tranquil administration reflects his sedentary habits: he spends most of his time eating, sleeping, smoking, and doubting, earning a reputation for wisdom through his gravity and reluctance to express opinions.

Soon after taking office, Van Twiller hears a financial dispute between Wandle Schoonhoven and Barent Bleecker. Unable or unwilling to decipher their account books, he weighs them, counts their pages, and declares the accounts balanced because the books are equally thick and heavy. He orders both men to give each other receipts and makes the constable pay the costs. The townspeople celebrate the ruling as wise and equitable, and its absurd outcome discourages all further lawsuits. It remains the only decisive judgment Van Twiller ever makes.

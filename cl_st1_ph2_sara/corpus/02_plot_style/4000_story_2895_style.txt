The story adopts a nineteenth-century literary travel-sketch style, combining maritime observation, natural history, memoir, philosophical meditation, and Gothic allegory. Its structure is episodic: numbered “Sketches” move from a panoramic account of the Encantadas to focused studies of tortoises, Rock Rodondo, sea birds, and the surrounding geography. Each section is introduced by archaic verse epigraphs, chiefly in a Spenserian manner, which establish an atmosphere of enchantment, peril, deformity, and moral trial. The sketches proceed associatively rather than through a continuous plot, shifting between description, anecdote, historical explanation, sailors’ lore, and imaginative reverie.

The narrative voice is learned, experienced, and theatrically conversational. The first-person speaker presents himself as both mariner and reflective essayist, grounding the account in personal recollection while repeatedly enlarging observed facts into myths or metaphysical symbols. He addresses the reader directly—“Take five-and-twenty heaps of cinders,” “let us ascend,” “observe another thing”—and occasionally apostrophizes animals or landscapes. This creates a guided-tour effect, though the guide is deliberately eccentric: authoritative nautical detail coexists with superstition, fantasy, comic exaggeration, and admissions of optical or imaginative delusion. The narrator often moves from empirical certainty into speculation through phrases such as “one may call it,” “one fancies,” “seems,” and “I can hardly resist the feeling.”

The diction is elevated, archaizing, and highly Latinate, mixed with concrete nautical and natural-historical vocabulary. Words such as “desolateness,” “uninhabitableness,” “antediluvian,” “cadaverous,” “Plutonian,” and “venerableness” lend the prose a monumental, antiquarian weight, while terms including “lee-way,” “spermaceti,” “calipee,” “main-royal-man,” and “trade-wind” provide technical specificity. Biblical, classical, Hindu, literary, and historical references appear densely and without much explanatory mediation: Lazarus, Babylon, Idumea, Tartarus, Job, Milton, Brahmins, Roman coliseums, and Daniel Boone all become points of comparison. The result is encyclopedic and allusive, presenting the islands as a meeting place of geology, scripture, legend, and seafaring experience.

Sentence structure is typically long, cumulative, and periodic. Clauses are layered through semicolons, colons, dashes, parenthetical qualifications, and repeated conjunctions, allowing descriptions to expand by accretion. Balanced contrasts and parallel constructions are especially prominent: the forests have seasonal change, but the islands do not; the tortoise is “both black and bright”; the penguin “stumps,” “sculls,” and “flops.” The prose also uses short exclamations and questions to punctuate these elaborate passages—“Sail ho!” “Pelicans.” “But ropes now, and let us ascend.”—producing sudden changes of pace and a sense of oral performance. Repetition, catalogues, and triadic phrasing add rhetorical force, as in the inventories of reptiles, birds, geological forms, and distant islands.

Imagery is profuse, visual, tactile, and often grotesque. The Encantadas are rendered through images of ash, cinders, furnaces, graves, corpses, cracked gourds, wreckage, and vitrified rock. Nature is rarely neutral: it appears ruined, punitive, or malignly enchanted. The islands resemble a world after “a penal conflagration,” their rocks belong to a “fallen” creation, and their tortoises seem condemned to hopeless labor. Personification intensifies this moral atmosphere: the islands cry out like a tormented spirit, currents behave capriciously, and the landscape appears to bear a curse. Similes characteristically leap between remote fields of experience, comparing tortoise shells to battered shields and ruined coliseums, Rock Rodondo to a bell tower and an enchanted frigate, birds to monks, senators, bandits, angels, and architectural ornaments.

The dominant tone is solemn, uncanny, and apocalyptic, but it is continually complicated by irony and grotesque humor. The narrator can describe tortoises as primordial world-bearers and, in the next movement, recount eating them and turning their shells into tableware. Penguins become failures of Nature; wicked sea officers are imagined reincarnated as tortoises; geographic immensity is reduced to “a boundless watery Kentucky.” Such comic reversals prevent the elevated rhetoric from becoming uniformly grave. The governing tonal principle is doubleness: sublimity and absurdity, beauty and ugliness, sympathy and cruelty, scientific curiosity and superstition remain simultaneously active.

The prose repeatedly transforms physical characteristics into moral or metaphysical emblems. Geological changelessness signifies damnation and hopelessness; baffling currents suggest unreality and enchantment; the tortoise embodies age, endurance, imprisonment, and futile determination. Its “drudging impulse to straightforwardness in a belittered world” turns animal behavior into a compact allegory of human persistence amid obstruction. Yet the story resists simple conclusions. The bright underside and black back of the tortoise become an explicit lesson against one-sided judgment, and moments of beauty—golden light, silver bird calls, brilliantly colored fish—emerge within the prevailing desolation.

Descriptions commonly follow a pattern of initial analogy, detailed observation, imaginative magnification, and reflective conclusion. A rock first resembles a sail, then becomes a tower crowded with birds, then an “aviary of Ocean,” and finally a vantage point from which geography becomes cosmology. Objects are rarely left at their literal scale: three tortoises expand into ancient cities or supports of the universe, while a remote sea stack becomes a cathedral, fortress, lighthouse, and celestial observatory. This habit of metamorphic comparison is central to the style, making perception itself seem enchanted.

The setting functions less as realistic background than as an active symbolic presence. Silence, drought, sterility, repetition, and isolation give the islands a postlapsarian character, as though they preserve the aftermath of cosmic punishment. Against this, the teeming birds and fish around Rock Rodondo introduce a paradoxical abundance. The natural world is therefore both dead and crowded with life, static and violently restless, factual and fabulous. Overall, the style is baroque, digressive, allusive, and symbolically charged: a hybrid of travel narrative and prose poem in which exact seafaring knowledge is continually converted into Gothic spectacle, comic fable, and metaphysical inquiry.

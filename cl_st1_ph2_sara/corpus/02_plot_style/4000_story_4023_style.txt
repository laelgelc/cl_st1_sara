The story adopts the manner of an orally transmitted Irish folktale, combining plain communal storytelling with lyrical, supernatural intensity. Its narrator speaks with the authority of tradition rather than personal observation, using formulas such as “one time,” “it is likely,” “as far as she could hear,” and “whether it was that time or another time.” These phrases create the impression of a tale preserved through repeated retelling, in which uncertainty is part of the legend rather than a flaw in the account. The narration remains largely external and omniscient, but moves readily into Hanrahan’s perceptions when the story darkens, allowing ordinary events to shade into vision, dream, and enchantment without any firm boundary between them.

The prose is shaped by Irish-inflected English. Its diction is mostly simple and concrete, but its grammar and idiom often preserve Gaelic speech patterns: “A welcome before you,” “he had a drop of drink taken,” “the like of it,” “let on to be stirring the fire,” and “there is no man in the house must dance with her but myself.” Repeated coordinators—especially “and,” “but,” and “then”—produce long, cumulative sentences that resemble spoken narration. Actions are commonly linked in sequence rather than subordinated or analytically explained. This paratactic movement gives the story a steady, incantatory rhythm and makes even extraordinary occurrences sound matter-of-fact.

Dialogue occupies a large portion of the narrative and serves both dramatic and communal functions. Characters reveal themselves through direct speech: the father is hospitable, the mother suspicious and practical, the young men mocking and competitive, Oona receptive but largely silent, and Hanrahan proud, seductive, volatile, and visionary. The household speaks collectively through gossip, warning, teasing, and proverbial knowledge. Speech is frequently repetitive, with phrases reiterated for emphasis, as in “he was weak, he was weak” and “let him die, let him die, let him die.” Such repetition evokes oral performance while heightening the ominous, ritual quality of the supernatural voices.

The tonal range moves from warm social realism to romantic enchantment and finally to desolation and nightmare. The opening presents a recognizable rural gathering of whiskey, cards, dancing, neighbours, courtship, and household work. Within this setting, Hanrahan’s presence introduces instability. His poetic speech transforms Oona’s attraction into a mythic destiny, comparing lovers to the sun and moon and to dancers who continue after everyone else has tired. The embedded song intensifies this effect through an image of an immortal country filled with perpetual blossom, overflowing beer, precious woods, music, and dancing queens. Yet the story continually undercuts Hanrahan’s grandeur with physical details—his ragged coat, broken boots, exposed grey socks, need for drink, and wounded vanity. The resulting style balances admiration for the poet’s imaginative power with irony about his weakness and social failure.

Imagery is rooted in western Irish landscape and folklore: bogs, bare hills, woods, strands, sea mist, plovers, thatched stacks, and the otherworldly Sidhe. Natural details frequently carry emotional or symbolic weight. The plover’s cry becomes an echo of ancient grief; firelight reveals Oona’s beauty; the sea mist produces spectral shapes; and the rope made from hay develops into an image of entrapment, sorrow, and cosmic doom. The closing vision expands rapidly from a household trick into grotesque mythology: the rope becomes a water-worm that coils around Hanrahan, then around the earth and sky, with the stars reduced to gleams upon its skin. This escalation from the domestic to the cosmic is characteristic of the story’s symbolic method.

The narrative is episodic but carefully patterned. Hanrahan enters because he cannot resist music and company, gains control of the room through speech and song, and is expelled through a practical deception that exploits his vanity. The twisting of the rope functions as the central structural and symbolic reversal. At first it is an ordinary task and a comic device; afterward it becomes the emblem of his humiliation, his accumulated sorrows, and the supernatural fate tightening around him. The lively interior of the house contrasts with the silent road and exposed strand outside, while human warmth, drink, music, and female attention contrast with cold, isolation, and death.

Poetry is presented as both revered and dangerous. Hanrahan’s status as “a poet of the Gael” grants him hospitality and protects him from physical expulsion because his curse is believed capable of damaging crops and cattle. His words can enchant Oona, silence mockery, and make an imagined country seem close at hand. At the same time, poetry isolates him from ordinary life: his visions encourage pride, longing, and dissatisfaction, while his pursuit of ideal love leaves him unable to find comfort among mortal women. The style therefore treats the poet as a liminal figure—socially degraded yet spiritually powerful, comic in circumstance yet tragic in destiny.

Overall, the prose is deliberately archaic, rhythmic, folkloric, and symbolically charged. Its characteristic effects come from oral-storytelling formulas, Gaelicized syntax, cumulative coordination, direct dialogue, strategic repetition, embedded song, mythological allusion, and the seamless mingling of rural realism with supernatural vision. The dominant mood is one of melancholy enchantment: beauty and desire briefly promise escape from age, poverty, and death, but that promise collapses into exclusion, loneliness, and an inescapable otherworldly judgment.

The story is written as a retrospective first-person narrative, with Ursula recounting events from the limited but emotionally candid perspective of her eighteen-year-old self. Her voice is lucid, personable, and gently self-mocking. She openly admits her romantic foolishness, misery, and indecision, while the retrospective framing lends the account enough distance for irony. The narration frequently moves between plain factual summary and more immediate emotional disclosure, creating the impression of an intimate anecdote told after its happy resolution.

The dominant tone is warm, comic, and affectionate, though it incorporates genuine romantic anxiety. Much of the humour arises from contrasts: urban expectations meet rural practicality, youthful idealism meets Aunt Philippa’s cynicism, and solemn subjects such as marriage, death, religion, and scandal are treated with blunt domestic common sense. The comedy is rarely cruel. Even Aunt Philippa’s harsh judgments ultimately conceal loyalty, disappointed love, and a strong protective instinct. This late revelation turns her from a comic eccentric into a sympathetic figure without undermining the humour.

The prose uses clear, accessible diction with occasional lyrical touches in descriptions of Prince Edward Island. Images such as the “grassy, elm-shaded country road,” its “ribbon of buttercups,” and the “pretty, seaward-sloping valley” establish a pastoral, idyllic background. These graceful scenic passages are balanced by concrete domestic details: the buggy, spinning wheel, lemon pie, patchwork cat, print wrappers, wedding cake, and kitchen fire. Setting is therefore rendered through homely objects and daily routines rather than elaborate atmospheric description. The vivid green farmhouse, oversized lettering, colourful clothing, and multicoloured cat also contribute to the story’s bright, picturesque quality.

Sentence structure is generally straightforward and conversational. The narrator often uses coordinated clauses, emphatic repetitions, dashes, and short declarative sentences to reproduce the rhythms of speech and thought: “I could not do what Mark asked—I could not.” Parenthetical qualifications and afterthoughts create a confiding tone. Longer sentences are used for exposition and comic accumulation, especially when cataloguing local histories, while short sentences sharpen emotional turns or punch lines. Paragraphing is frequent, keeping the narrative brisk and allowing dialogue to dominate many scenes.

Dialogue is the principal engine of characterization and comedy. Aunt Philippa speaks in a strongly marked rural vernacular, using forms such as “ain’t,” “writ,” “sot,” “give,” “a-took,” and “mebbe.” Her speech is blunt, proverbial, judgmental, and rich in comic understatement. Ursula’s language is more educated and polished, and her grave questions or flippant epigrams serve as prompts for Aunt Philippa’s sharper replies. Their exchanges often follow a set comic rhythm: Ursula offers a reasonable or idealistic observation, Aunt Philippa counters it with an anecdote, and the anecdote ends in a sardonic verdict.

Repetition is the story’s most conspicuous stylistic device. Aunt Philippa’s refrain, “That’s the men for you,” functions as a catchphrase, structural marker, and recurring punch line. Each repetition gathers new comic force as it is attached to increasingly grotesque examples of male folly. The refrain also establishes her apparent philosophy, making her eventual support for Ursula’s marriage both surprising and emotionally persuasive. Near the end, the narrator’s phrase “And that was Aunt Philippa for you” affectionately reverses the formula, defining Philippa herself through her contradictory mixture of severity and generosity.

Characterization relies on caricature softened by revealing detail. Aunt Philippa is introduced visually through her “determined mouth,” “piercing grey eyes,” unmistakable family nose, and clashing clothes. Her forceful personality is then developed through speech, practical actions, prejudices, and anecdotes. She appears severe, intrusive, anti-romantic, sectarian, and suspicious of men, yet she is also competent, independent, hospitable, shrewd, and emotionally perceptive. Ursula is characterized through her reactions to Philippa: amusement, resistance, discouragement, amazement, and eventual gratitude. Mark remains comparatively lightly drawn because the real emotional relationship in the story is between Ursula and her aunt.

The narrative structure is linear and tightly shaped around reversal. It opens with enforced exile and a romantic quarrel, shifts into an episodic middle composed of country life and Aunt Philippa’s cautionary tales, then introduces urgency through Mark’s letter and proposed departure. The apparent obstacle—Philippa’s hostility to men and marriage—becomes the solution when she offers to host the wedding. A second, smaller obstacle arises when the Presbyterian minister leaves, forcing Philippa to accept the Methodist minister she despises. The kitchen fire interrupts the wedding at precisely the moment of greatest ceremonial solemnity, producing a final burst of physical comedy. The last exchange supplies both the emotional explanation and the closing joke.

Pacing alternates between summary and scene. Ursula’s first month on the island is compressed into a few paragraphs, while the buggy ride, verandah conversations, reading of Mark’s letter, and wedding are dramatized at length. The episodic anecdotes about neighbours momentarily suspend the central plot, but they are stylistically purposeful: they build Philippa’s worldview, create a communal rural setting, and gradually intensify Ursula’s doubts. Once the letter arrives, the pace accelerates through the three-week wedding preparations.

Irony operates throughout. The adults send Ursula away partly to separate her from Mark, but the visit enables their marriage. Philippa professes contempt for men while working energetically to secure one for her niece. She rejects romantic elopement because she once refused to elope herself and has regretted it ever since. Her rigid denominational prejudice collapses under practical necessity, and the despised Methodist minister performs the marriage competently. Even the final confession is immediately qualified: had Philippa eloped, she supposes she would have regretted that as well. This closing ambiguity preserves her comic identity and presents human choice as inherently imperfect.

The thematic treatment is light but coherent. The story explores the conflict between romance and respectability, youthful desire and parental authority, prejudice and practical kindness, and professed belief versus hidden experience. It suggests that cynicism may be disappointed idealism in disguise and that genuine wisdom is less about abstract “sense” than about recognizing what another person truly needs. Its overall style is that of a humorous domestic romance: conversational, picturesque, character-driven, gently satirical, and resolved through coincidence, reversal, and affectionate comic revelation.

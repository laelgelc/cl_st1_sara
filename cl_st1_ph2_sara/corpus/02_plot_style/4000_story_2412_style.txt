The story adopts a layered pseudo-historical style that combines dystopian speculation, oral folk narrative, political parable, and documentary fiction. Editorial notes placed before and after the central tale frame it as a recovered historical artifact. Their scholarly, retrospective language—complete with dates, legal references, archival claims, and publication history—creates an appearance of authenticity while establishing a vast chronology. This frame also produces dramatic irony: institutions in the remote future study as history a slave society that is itself still centuries ahead of the implied original audience.

Within the frame, the dominant voice is that of an itinerant working-class storyteller addressing an illicit gathering. The repeated vocatives “my brothers” and “brothers,” together with commands and invitations such as “Listen” and “Have you got that?”, make the narration sound spoken rather than written. The speaker continually acknowledges his listeners’ ignorance, anticipates their reactions, asks rhetorical questions, and draws comparisons with their own lives. This creates the cadence of a political sermon or agitator’s speech, in which storytelling, education, and revolutionary persuasion are inseparable.

The diction is deliberately accessible and emphatic. Concrete terms such as “slave,” “master,” “arm,” “blood,” “books,” and “pen” recur insistently, reducing the social order to stark moral oppositions. The narrator uses emotionally loaded epithets—“hell-hound,” “accursed,” “Bloody Vange”—and repeatedly asserts the truth of his account through phrases such as “This is fact,” “I know,” and “I have read it with my own eyes.” These assertions characterize an audience denied access to literacy and turn documentary knowledge into a source of forbidden authority. Formal or archaic constructions, including “wherein,” “thereafter,” “as you shall see,” and “if you will but study,” mingle with colloquial expressions such as “I guess,” “take my word for it,” and “a bit of biliousness.” The result is a voice at once popular, biblical, didactic, and historically distanced.

Sentence structure alternates between accumulating historical exposition and short declarations designed for oral emphasis. Long sentences carry genealogies, institutional details, and chains of cause and effect, while compact statements—“He was a slave,” “It is history,” “They were well beaten”—land like verdicts. Repetition and syntactic parallelism are central to the rhythm. Names are repeated as they change—“Vange, Bill Vange, Sly Vange”—and ideological formulas recur, especially “masters and slaves,” “read and write,” and “with my own eyes.” Parenthetical interruptions, direct questions, dashes, and delayed qualifications imitate a speaker adjusting his tale to a live audience.

The narrative is digressive but carefully controlled. It begins by promising “a tale of an arm,” then postpones that central incident through an extended history of the Vanderwater dynasty. This apparent detour establishes that the ruling family originated among the enslaved and therefore undermines claims of natural class superiority. The narrator explicitly returns to his starting point before recounting the industrial grievance, the severed arm, and the confrontation in the palace. The structure moves from broad historical indictment to one intensely physical symbol, allowing a systemic argument to culminate in a grotesque and memorable image.

Contrast governs much of the descriptive method. The factory called “Hell’s Bottom” is set against Vanderwater’s palace; starvation wages against luxury; workers’ bodily injuries against the oligarch’s trivial headache; filthy sleeping quarters against a bedchamber resembling Paradise; and the white severed arm against blood-red strawberries “like precious jewels.” These juxtapositions expose exploitation through sensory and material detail rather than abstract theory alone. The story’s most striking scene uses controlled grotesquerie: the arm is washed, arranged among expensive fruit, and made to clutch a written petition. Body and text become a single message, converting suffering that the master can ignore into an object he must see and read.

Irony is pervasive and often bitter. The workers possess legal rights but “have no access to the law”; an emergency fund financed from their meagre wages is stolen by overseers; literacy is criminalized because it might reveal the ruling class’s origins; and Roger Vanderwater considers his minor discomfort comparable to catastrophic mutilation. The narrator’s courteous explanations frequently sharpen the satire, as when he observes that the arm was “well washed, of course.” Capitalization in “THOUGHT” provides an unusually blunt oral stress, while euphemistic phrases such as “regular slave rights” expose the emptiness of official language.

Characterization is largely emblematic rather than psychologically intricate. Individuals represent positions within the class system: Roger Vanderwater is the insulated but intermittently paternal oligarch; Clancy and Munster embody delegated corruption; Bloody Vange represents collaboration and social betrayal; Tom Dixon’s mutilated body represents industrial sacrifice; and the literate slave embodies courage, testimony, and educational resistance. Yet brief dialogue gives the climactic figures moral force. The reply “I have been sorry all my life that I was born” is terse, unsentimental, and devastating, reversing the master’s threat by showing that oppression has already exceeded any punishment he can impose.

The tone shifts among mock scholarship, indignation, pathos, macabre spectacle, and revolutionary hope. Violence is described directly but without elaborate sensationalism, which makes it feel ordinary within the social system and therefore more disturbing. The narrator openly moralizes and does not seek ideological neutrality. His tale has a practical purpose: to recruit listeners into literacy and solidarity. Even the temporary act of justice at the end is qualified—“for a time only”—preventing the resolution from becoming reassuring. Reform depends on the character of a particular master and is therefore unstable; lasting change requires collective knowledge.

Overall, the style is propagandistic in the deliberate, literary sense: it uses a gripping oral tale to teach a political lesson. Its principal techniques are archival framing, direct audience address, repetition, rhetorical questioning, stark class vocabulary, historical exemplum, grotesque symbolism, and ironic contrast. The printed word functions both as subject and as formal power: records expose the masters, the petition forces recognition, the storyteller’s literacy preserves suppressed memory, and the editorial apparatus ensures the tale’s survival. The story’s style thus enacts its central claim that narrative and literacy can transform private suffering into historical consciousness and collective resistance.

The story has a distinctly modernist, impressionistic style, built around the unstable activity of perception rather than a conventionally plotted sequence of events. Its apparent action is slight—a train journey and the observation of a fellow passenger—but this narrow setting opens into an elaborate drama of speculation. External details act as prompts from which the narrator invents whole lives, motives, relationships and crises. The resulting narrative repeatedly expands from the immediate scene into fantasy and then contracts when some mundane fact interrupts it. This creates a fluid boundary between observation, imagination and reality.

The first-person narrator is intensely self-conscious, intrusive and unreliable. Rather than simply describing the woman, the narrator mentally addresses her, interprets her gestures, supplies her with a name and history, and imagines her domestic surroundings and moral burdens. These conjectures are often presented with momentary certainty, only to be revised, abandoned or contradicted. The eventual discovery that the woman’s circumstances are quite different from those imagined exposes the presumptuousness of the narrator’s construction. Unreliability is therefore not treated merely as an error but as the story’s central method: another person remains fundamentally unknowable, yet the attempt to know them produces imaginative richness.

The narrative voice moves rapidly among reported observation, internal monologue, rhetorical commentary, imaginary dialogue and scenes rendered as though directly witnessed. Pronouns shift accordingly. The observed woman is alternately “she,” “you,” “Minnie” and an emblem of humanity, while the narrator moves between “I,” the communal “we” and generalized reflections on “one.” These shifts collapse distance and dramatize the narrator’s changing degree of identification with the stranger. Free-associative transitions allow a physical twitch, a blemish on glass, a newspaper item or the price of eggs to generate extended chains of thought.

Syntax is highly elastic. Long, cumulative sentences reproduce the rush and swirl of consciousness, gathering images, qualifications, questions and digressions through commas, semicolons, dashes and parentheses. These are interrupted by fragments, exclamations and clipped imperatives such as imagined commands to “skip,” “look” or “face it.” The punctuation often performs the mind’s movement: dashes mark abrupt redirections, ellipses indicate omitted or dissolving material, parentheses admit second thoughts, and square brackets foreground the narrator’s active editing of the invented story. Missing or compressed conjunctions intensify speed, while repetitions and self-corrections convey a consciousness thinking faster than orderly prose can contain.

The story is openly metafictional. The narrator comments on how stories are made, worries about whether invented details are plausible, rejects decorative possibilities that do not fit the social setting, and complains that life imposes less satisfying material than fiction desires. Characters are proposed, named, developed and dismissed before the reader’s eyes. The imagined commercial traveller is assembled from bodily, social and domestic details, while alternative characters and images are sacrificed because they would be aesthetically attractive but factually unsuitable. Composition itself becomes part of the action, exposing fiction as a mixture of scrutiny, prejudice, desire, convention and improvisation.

The diction ranges from abstract and philosophical to sharply concrete and domestic. Large concepts—life, destiny, God, sin, immortality and the soul—are continually juxtaposed with eggs, gloves, muffins, newspapers, curtains, cruets and public transport. This contrast produces both comedy and pathos. Spiritual anguish is never allowed to remain purely sublime; it is tethered to prices, meals, clothing and household routines. Conversely, ordinary objects acquire symbolic force. The spot on the window becomes a possible stain of guilt, the itch between the shoulders becomes embodied conscience, the newspaper becomes a shield against human contact, and fragments of eggshell become a broken map that the narrator longs to reconstruct.

Imagery is abundant, mobile and often metamorphic. Faces become symbols, newspapers become armour, a tired head resembles an exhausted spinning top, roofs resemble caterpillar spines, and consciousness takes the form of moths, butterflies, hawks, waves, webs and rushing water. Images rarely remain static; they develop through motion, transformation and association. Water imagery in particular conveys the narrator’s mental state: thoughts swirl, surge, flood, deposit sediment and carry the self onward. Flight imagery expresses the elusive inner life that escapes whenever another person seems almost graspable. Such figurative movement gives the prose a lyrical, almost incantatory rhythm.

The tone oscillates among satire, sympathy, exhilaration, irritation, dread and wonder. The narrator can be cruelly comic in imagining the woman’s social awkwardness and bodily discomfort, yet also feels profound compassion for her presumed loneliness. Bourgeois interiors and social habits are rendered with amused precision, while grandiose interpretations are repeatedly punctured by practical facts. This tonal instability prevents any single judgment from settling. Even mockery can suddenly become tenderness, and bathos can lead back into metaphysical awe.

Dialogue is sparse in the actual encounter but plentiful in imagined form. Much of it is invented by the narrator to animate hypothetical scenes, and it tends to be socially revealing: formulaic politeness, family rebukes and banal remarks convey class, habit and emotional constraint. The real exchange near the end is conspicuously ordinary, and its plainness overturns the elaborate drama previously constructed. Speech therefore functions both as characterization and as a corrective to imaginative projection.

Rhythmically, the prose alternates between acceleration and suspension. Lists and accumulations imitate the train’s forward motion and the mind’s compulsive elaboration, while repeated commands to pause, look or return briefly arrest the flow. Recurring phrases and motifs—the twitch, the spot, the eggs, the act of looking—create cohesion despite the digressive structure. Exclamations and rhetorical questions give the narration the energy of an internal performance, as though thought were being improvised aloud.

The ending transforms epistemological defeat into renewed wonder. Once the imagined biography collapses, the narrator briefly experiences reality as “bare,” but uncertainty soon becomes a source of reverence rather than frustration. The unknown woman and her son merge into the larger flow of anonymous urban life. The final style is expansive, apostrophic and ecstatic, moving through luminous visual fragments and repeated direct address. The story thus ends not by solving the stranger but by celebrating her unknowability and the inexhaustible mystery of other lives.

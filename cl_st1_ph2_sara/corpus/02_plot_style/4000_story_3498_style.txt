The story adopts a lyrical, elevated mode of romantic tragedy, blending courtly narrative, devotional symbolism and the cadence of oral legend. Its prose is formal and slightly archaic, using expressions such as “out of ken,” “sire,” “thy,” and “to thee,” alongside culturally specific terms including “vina,” “pandits,” and “MSS.” The setting is presented less through concrete historical detail than through emblematic features—the throne, screened balcony, golden anklets, royal hall, moonlit landscape and flower-filled death chamber—which give the tale the timeless atmosphere of a fable or poetic romance.

The narration is third-person and broadly omniscient, with privileged access to Shekhar’s dreams, fears and self-doubt. Although the narrator sometimes states events directly, the voice also offers aphoristic commentary: “Thus truth and falsehood mingle in life” and “to what God builds man adds his own decoration.” Such interventions lend the storyteller philosophical authority and turn individual events into reflections on art, love, illusion and human judgement. The narrative remains emotionally aligned with Shekhar, while Pundarik is viewed externally and satirically through his pride, theatrical gestures and ostentatious learning.

Diction is sensuous, musical and richly figurative. Sound is especially important: poems rise through the court, anklets tinkle from afar, the flute fills the landscape, Pundarik’s voice crashes like sea-breakers, and Shekhar’s voice ascends “like a quivering flame of fire.” Visual motifs of veils, shadows, lamps, flowers, moonlight and screened spaces reinforce the tension between what is imagined and what is seen. Fragrance and touch also contribute to the atmosphere through spring flowers, perfume, breeze and the delicacy of imagined feet. Images often fuse the human with the cosmic or sacred: Ajita becomes a distant ruling planet, love resembles divine mercy, popular devotion rises like incense, and poetry turns landscape and emotion into music.

The syntax alternates between short, emphatic statements and long, flowing descriptive sentences. Brief openings such as “She was the Princess Ajita” or “The trumpet sounded” give scenes a ceremonial clarity, while cumulative clauses create rhapsodic momentum during poetic performances. Repetition and parallelism are prominent: the recurring balcony shadow and anklet bells bind the story together, while sequences such as “from fields and groves, from the shady lanes and lonely roads” imitate the diffusion of music across the world. Exclamations, rhetorical questions and direct apostrophe heighten the theatrical and devotional quality of the prose.

Dialogue is concise, formal and performative rather than naturalistic. Characters speak in epigrams, challenges and declarations: the bee-and-flower exchange establishes playful courtly wit, Pundarik repeatedly asks what is superior to words, and Ajita’s final speech delivers both judgement and benediction. Public speech functions as contest, whereas private speech becomes confession or revelation. The contrast between the two poets is therefore also stylistic. Pundarik represents volume, analysis, etymology, quotation and intellectual display; Shekhar represents simplicity, melody, emotional truth and intuitive communion. The story’s own lush and sympathetic language clearly endorses Shekhar’s conception of poetry.

Structurally, the narrative moves from idyllic repetition into public conflict and then private catastrophe. The opening establishes a stable pattern of recitation, distant anklet music and Manjari’s visits. Pundarik’s arrival abruptly introduces a contest plot. The two days of competition are arranged as paired contrasts between feeling and erudition, after which the pace accelerates through defeat, manuscript burning, poisoning and belated recognition. Foreshadowing is persistent: Ajita’s hidden presence, Shekhar’s worship of an unseen image, the altar and fire imagery, and his association of love with unattainable distance all anticipate a union achieved only at death.

The emotional register combines tenderness, wonder, irony, humiliation and fatal pathos. Gentle humour surrounds Manjari and the court’s teasing, but this warmth gives way to anxiety and then despair. The ending is deliberately melodramatic yet restrained in execution: flowers, lamps, poison, perfume and anklet bells converge in a ritualised death scene. Ajita’s arrival resolves the central uncertainty—she truly was the hidden listener—but the recognition comes too late to alter events. The final crowning transforms Shekhar’s death into artistic vindication.

At its thematic centre is the opposition between living poetry and sterile verbal mastery. The story distinguishes words that merely demonstrate ingenuity from words animated by love, communal memory and spiritual experience. It also explores the instability of public taste: the crowd is moved deeply by Shekhar, then persuaded that his simplicity is inadequacy. Hidden female presence functions as muse, audience and ultimate judge, while the motifs of veiling and misrecognition suggest that truth may remain invisible to official authority. Overall, the style is ornate, rhythmic, symbolic and emotionally direct, shaped to resemble the very kind of poetry the story defends: simple in its governing conflict, but resonant through music, imagery and spiritual longing.

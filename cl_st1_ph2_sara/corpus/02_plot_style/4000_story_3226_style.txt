The story is written as a period mystery in a formal, early-twentieth-century literary register, though its action is set in antebellum Virginia. Its governing mode is the classical detection tale: an apparently straightforward crime is reconstructed through physical anomalies, close questioning, scientific knowledge, and a sequence of reversals. The opening reference to Sir Henry Marquis gives the narrative a framed, recounted quality, after which an unnamed first-person narrator relates an episode dominated by his father, Pendleton. The narrator remains largely unobtrusive, serving as a respectful witness whose retrospective comments guide the reader’s moral response without competing with the principal investigator.

The prose combines material exactness with an atmosphere of cultivated wealth and latent disorder. The library is established through long windows, formal gardens, mahogany drawers, jade, ivory, bronze, and Japanese crystal. Such objects are not merely decorative: the room’s refined furnishings evoke Peyton Marshall’s character as “a traveler, a man of letters, a dreamer,” while the crystal later becomes the instrument of detection. This conversion of ornament into evidence is characteristic of the story’s construction. Description often proceeds by inventory, using balanced clauses and carefully selected exotic materials to create a stately, old-world environment before introducing the visual disruption of the ransacked drawers.

Sentence structure alternates between short, declarative statements and expansive, cumulative passages. Brief sentences—“It was searched for Peyton Marshall’s will”—give interrogations clarity and force, while longer sentences carry descriptions, accusations, and philosophical reflections. Semicolons are frequent, producing a measured, deliberative rhythm and allowing successive observations to accumulate without losing their logical relation. Dashes and parenthetical interruptions reproduce shifts in thought or emphasis. Repetition is also prominent, especially in questioning: “What business…? What business…?” and “I see…” create rhetorical pressure. At climactic moments, syntax becomes more visual and emphatic, as in the enlarged description of the altered numeral, where the forged mark is compared to “a black lath tacked across two uprights.”

Dialogue supplies most of the action and characterization. Each principal figure has a distinct verbal manner. Pendleton speaks in a cold, controlled, aphoristic style, asking deceptively simple questions and occasionally expanding into moral or metaphysical reflections. Gosford uses legalistic qualifications, elaborate denials, and patronizing forms of address; his verbosity and technical phrasing suggest both professional polish and evasiveness. Lewis speaks as a conventional lawyer, committed to statutory validity even when disturbed by its consequences. Young Marshall’s language is emotionally direct and founded on filial faith. Gaeki speaks least, but his blunt observations, foreign expletives, and dry final remark give him comic force and practical authority. Dialogue tags are generally plain, but they are supplemented by precise gestures—twisting a seal ring, arranging writing materials, fingering bottles, or putting one’s fingers together “with a judicial air.”

Characterization is strongly physiognomic and typological. Faces, posture, complexion, and movement disclose moral or social identity: the son is fair, delicate, generous, and vulnerable; Gosford is pink-faced, pale-eyed, dapper, and hard-mouthed; Gaeki is jerky, shaggy, eccentric, and formidable; Pendleton’s large shoulders and level voice convey restrained power. These portraits rely on the conventions of older popular fiction, in which outward appearance often reflects inward character. National and social types are similarly pronounced, particularly in the repeated designation of Gosford as “the Englishman” and in the antebellum references to servants and enslaved Black people, whose portrayal reflects the historical assumptions and racial vocabulary of the setting and period rather than a modern perspective.

The pacing is architectural. The narrative first establishes the violated room and suspicion of Gosford, then introduces the son’s concealed will, apparently resolving the burglary while exposing a moral and legal dilemma. It next reveals the altered bequest through magnification, allows Gosford to escape that accusation with a plausible explanation, and finally closes the trap through Gaeki’s chemical test. Each solution therefore produces a deeper problem. The story withholds crucial significance rather than crucial objects: the crystal, Gosford’s writing equipment, Gaeki’s bottles, and the differing inks are all visible before their functions become clear. This gives the ending the retrospectively satisfying logic expected of fair-play detection.

The narrative repeatedly sets law against justice. Lewis defines crime as a violation of law, whereas Pendleton calls it a violation of justice—“something behind the law.” Young Marshall has technically committed theft, yet the prose treats his conduct sympathetically because it springs from loyalty and an attempt to prevent fraud. Gosford, by contrast, has manipulated legal forms to conceal a moral and material crime. The investigation thus proceeds on two levels: the will must be shown not only suspicious but forensically fraudulent, since moral certainty alone cannot defeat its legal validity. Pendleton provides ethical and intuitive insight, while Gaeki supplies the empirical proof that makes justice actionable.

The tone is grave, assured, and faintly theatrical, relieved by dry humor. Pendleton’s humiliation of Gosford over the open door, Lewis’s concealed laughter, the mock “crystal-gazing,” and Gaeki’s complaint about losing “a measure of good acid” temper the solemnity. Irony is central: Gosford mistakes Pendleton’s use of the crystal for superstition, although it is functioning as a lens; he relies on modern commercial ink, only for that modernity to expose him; and the apparent criminal, young Marshall, is morally innocent beside the man invoking criminal proceedings. The climax is staged almost like courtroom drama, with the characters gathered around the will and successive experts interpreting the same physical mark.

Figurative language draws heavily on religion, craftsmanship, and the supernatural. Crime is “the devil’s work”; fabricated events are false pieces fitted into the “natural sequence”; criminals are workmen in “the devil’s shop.” Pendleton’s investigative method is expressed through images of joints, serrated contacts, and imperfect workmanship, suggesting that reality forms a coherent structure into which fraud can never fit perfectly. The crystal-gazing conceit extends this imagery by turning scientific observation into mock prophecy. These metaphors elevate detection from clever puzzle-solving into a moral inquiry governed by providential order.

The style is therefore best described as formal, rhetorical, object-rich, and plot-disciplined. It favors lucid exposition, sharply differentiated voices, moral polarization, and tangible forensic clues. Its principal stylistic strength is the integration of setting, character, and evidence: nearly every conspicuous object or mannerism contributes to the eventual solution. Occasional textual irregularities—such as inconsistent spellings of Lewis’s name, misplaced commas, “its’,” “find defiance,” and “is” where “as” is intended—appear to be transcription or source-text errors rather than deliberate features of the narrative voice.

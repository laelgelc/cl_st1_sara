The story adopts the form of a first-person travel sketch, combining documentary observation with reflective essay, social satire, picturesque description, and occasional Gothic fantasy. Its narrator moves through a succession of American locations—Burlington, Rochester, and a nighttime stop on Lake Erie—recording landscapes, buildings, crowds, commerce, and local customs. Plot is secondary to perception: each section is organized around a place or spectacle, then develops through accumulated visual detail, personal commentary, and a concluding image or moral reflection. The episodic Roman-numeral divisions reinforce the impression of extracts from a travel journal, while the duplicated sequence in the supplied text appears to be a transmission or assembly feature rather than a deliberate narrative device.

The narrative voice is cultivated, observant, self-conscious, and gently ironic. The narrator writes as both eyewitness and interpreter, frequently shifting from concrete description to analogy, judgment, or speculation. He can sound confidently superior when assessing inland sailors, urban manners, or immigrant populations, but he also turns the irony upon himself, as when he calls himself “wise in theory” yet “practically as great a fool as Sam.” This self-mockery softens, without removing, the voice’s moral and social authority. The perspective is distinctly period-bound: its depictions of Irish immigrants, women, Black hotel workers, and social classes rely on ethnic, racial, gendered, and class stereotypes characteristic of nineteenth-century travel writing.

The diction is formal and literary but animated by colloquial labels and concrete occupational vocabulary. Elevated phrases such as “unprofitable sublimity,” “posthumous renown,” and “the immense void of night” coexist with expressions like “old salt,” “fresh-water fellows,” and “sprung up like a mushroom.” Nautical, commercial, architectural, and civic terms give the observations material specificity: skiffs, sloops, schooners, wharves, mill-dams, ox-teams, lottery offices, court-houses, and packet boats. The vocabulary often raises ordinary scenes into a grander register, only to puncture that grandeur with humor. Rochester’s waterfall possesses “diminished pomp” because practical citizens have diverted its water, while Sam Patch “alighted in the other world,” a ceremonious euphemism for a fatal plunge.

Sentences tend to be long, periodic, and syntactically layered, with subordinate clauses, parenthetical qualifications, semicolons, and cumulative lists. The prose often delays its central point while refining or complicating an impression. Dashes introduce witty afterthoughts, reversals, or editorial asides, and semicolons coordinate densely packed observations. Enumeration is a major rhythmic device, especially in crowd scenes: pedestrians, horsemen, coaches, wagons, and ox-teams become a verbal equivalent of urban congestion. Participial sequences—“hurrying, trotting, rattling, and rumbling”—increase pace and supply an audible texture. Shorter sentences and rhetorical questions interrupt these expansive constructions at moments of emphasis, particularly in the moral meditation on Sam Patch.

Description is strongly pictorial. Scenes are composed in terms of foreground and distance, color, movement, illumination, and contrast, as though they were landscape paintings or theatrical tableaux. Burlington is rendered through blue peaks, a green island, white houses, brick stores, shining tin roofs, and a silvery lake. Rochester is filtered through a gray, transparent evening and the twinkling lights of an emergent city. The final night scene depends almost entirely on chiaroscuro: bodies repeatedly emerge from darkness, cross a lurid field of firelight, and disappear again. Light does not merely reveal the setting; it transforms commonplace labor into spectacle and makes the ordinary verge upon the supernatural.

Figurative language is frequent, lucid, and often witty. Personification makes mountains gaze over one another’s shoulders, a steamboat leave “smoky breath,” and national effigies kiss inside a mixed pocket. Similes move between the homely and the sublime: Rochester grows “like a mushroom,” an inland sailor resembles a true seaman only as much as a lake fish resembles a Newfoundland cod, and fame spreads like perfume from fruit. Biblical, classical, and literary allusions—“all the perfumes of Arabia,” Mammon, miracles, final conflagration—give local American scenes a broader cultural resonance. These allusions are commonly used with playful disproportion, magnifying mundane smells, lotteries, fires, and civic activity into moral or apocalyptic emblems.

The tone changes fluidly across the sections. Burlington is bright, curious, comic, and socially satirical. Rochester begins in subdued sublimity, turns elegiac and philosophical around the legend of Sam Patch, then becomes energetic and almost cacophonous in its depiction of urban growth. The Lake Erie episode is darker, more concentrated, and overtly Gothic. Yet a consistent imaginative procedure unites them: observation supplies the physical scene, fancy transforms it, and reflection extracts a symbolic or moral meaning. Even the supernatural suggestion remains grounded in acknowledged perception; the narrator knows that light and shadow are distorting laborers, but willingly entertains the image of devils tending their own flames.

The pacing follows the narrator’s gaze rather than a conventional dramatic arc. Broad landscapes give way to close inspection; static description yields to moving crowds; an anecdote prompts meditation; and a practical delay in travel becomes an opportunity for visual reverie. Transitional phrases such as “On closer inspection,” “Our next movement,” “I confess, however,” “Thus musing,” and “In short” openly guide the reader through this process. There is virtually no dialogue. Human figures are viewed externally and often grouped into social types, so characterization is achieved through clothing, posture, occupation, motion, and the narrator’s interpretive comparisons rather than through speech or psychological depth.

A central stylistic tension lies between utility and imagination. Natural grandeur is reduced by canals and mills, yet the resulting commercial city has its own vitality. An obscure stunt performer acquires legendary permanence through empty fame. Anonymous laborers become infernal figures under the influence of firelight. Borders and categories repeatedly blur: lake ports imitate seaports, Canadian and American money mingle, wilderness gives way to an “instantaneous city,” and realistic reportage shades into legend or supernatural tale. The overall style is therefore picturesque, essayistic, rhetorically polished, and morally speculative, balancing close empirical detail with irony, symbolism, and a persistent desire to discover romance in the rapidly modernizing American landscape.

A narrator describes a good-hearted but relentlessly tedious acquaintance whose calm manner, endless anecdotes, and inflated sense of personal importance exhaust everyone around him. The man turns ordinary conversations into elaborate accounts of his travels, claiming to have discovered an unknown Italian masterpiece and an incomparable Swiss valley. He cites his supposedly influential brother in the East as an authority on foreign affairs and insists that he has witnessed numerous singular events, including meeting William IV, seeing Parliament just before it burned, and experiencing unprecedented voyages, speeds, performances, and spectacles.

Any mention of health prompts a detailed history of his own illness and the many mistaken doctors who treated him before an obscure physician cured his indigestion with mutton chops and sherry. He also traffics in secret information, hints at mysteries he cannot reveal, prolongs pointless arguments, and repeatedly offers useless advice. Gifted at recognizing others like himself, he eagerly joins fellow bores in interminable debates and later praises them as exceptionally intelligent. The narrator closes with relief that the bore has never paid him that compliment.

The story uses close third-person narration with a mobile, selectively omniscient viewpoint. It moves between Hilda, Emma, the father, the clergyman, and other family members, briefly entering each consciousness while preserving a cool narrative distance. Interior states are usually rendered through free indirect discourse or concise authorial interpretation rather than extended monologue. The narrator frequently exposes contradictions between outward behaviour and inward feeling: humility conceals brutality, obedience proves more disturbing than open conflict, religious tenderness coexists with blindness, and Emma can hate the baby as a symbol while loving its physical presence “like fire in her blood.” Characterisation depends on these psychological oppositions.

The prose combines social realism with lyrical, symbolic description. Domestic and industrial details are concrete and plentiful: cakes, flour sacks, bacon, coal, ointment, bread and butter, tinned salmon, a china bowl, pit dirt, and the ritual spread of high tea. Such objects establish a materially specific working-class environment and encode status, shame, and aspiration. Against this plain physical world, the narration introduces heightened natural images. Hilda resembles “a black swan of mournful, disreputable plumage,” the moon is “like a flamingo flying low,” and the father imagines the child as “a willow tree beside the waters.” Nature offers fleeting beauty and imaginative release, but it does not resolve the household’s emotional confinement.

The diction shifts markedly according to speaker and narrative function. The main narration is literate, precise, and sometimes elevated, using words such as “saturnine,” “impervious,” “solemnity,” and “locomotor ataxy.” Dialogue, by contrast, records regional working-class speech through contractions, altered spelling, and dialect forms such as “nöwt,” “tha,” “wor,” “canna,” and “childt.” This contrast dramatises divisions of education, class, and self-presentation within the family. Hilda’s rapid, over-elaborate speech reveals nervousness and social self-consciousness; the baker’s clipped replies communicate hostility; Emma and Bertha speak in blunt commands; the clergyman uses soft, sonorous religious formulas; and the father’s loose dialect expands into passionate, repetitive prayer.

Sentence rhythm is highly expressive. Descriptive passages often use long, cumulative sentences that gather physical detail and emotional qualification. Short declarative sentences interrupt them at moments of hostility or exposure: “He had evidently caught that word. He stood waiting.” Parallel clauses and repeated conjunctions reproduce the oppressive accumulation of family feeling. The father’s prayer relies on anaphora, direct address, Biblical cadence, and recursive self-accusation—“Lord,” “Father,” “because of me”—creating an emotional climax that is simultaneously moving, humiliating, and coercive. Dashes, semicolons, ellipses, and abrupt paragraph breaks mimic hesitation, interruption, nervous acceleration, or lapses of thought.

Figurative language is bodily and kinetic. Lips “flutter,” words crowd “like a flock of sheep at a gate,” shame flushes across the neck, love burns in the blood, and damaged authority operates like a “lodestone.” Characters are repeatedly described through posture, movement, voice, skin, hands, mouths, and physical imbalance. Illness and bodily deterioration are central stylistic motifs: Hilda’s heart disease, the father’s staggering frame, the exposed breast, the sucking infant, and the grime of the miner’s body make emotional relations tangible. Even abstract power is presented as something physically imposed: the father has been “like a stone upon” his children, leaving them as “plants under a stone.”

The tone is compassionate but unsentimental, mingling pity, irony, discomfort, and occasional cruelty. The narrator understands the characters’ suffering without idealising them. Descriptions can be harsh—“heavy, brutal cast,” “big and ugly,” “slavering old fool”—yet these judgements are complicated by glimpses of vulnerability and thwarted affection. Irony often arises from failed communication: the baker does not follow Hilda’s nervous wit, the clergyman hears but cannot understand the father’s language, and the family participates in a ceremony whose members experience radically different meanings. Religious language is treated neither as simple consolation nor as mere hypocrisy; it becomes a vehicle for authentic confession that also burdens and embarrasses its listeners.

The story’s structure is linear and tightly concentrated around a domestic ritual. It begins with Hilda’s socially conspicuous deviation from routine, follows her humiliating errand, broadens into the strained household, and culminates in the christening and the father’s prayer. The baker’s bag and Laurie’s taunting “baker’s man” rhyme connect the opening errand to the concealed paternity, giving an ordinary object symbolic force. The narrative closes not with reconciliation but with an interpretive summary of the father’s lingering domination, followed by his lyrical praise of the daisies and his daughters’ sullen recoil. This ending crystallises the story’s governing tension: beauty, love, faith, and vitality remain present, but every expression of them has been distorted by shame, paternal authority, and emotional bondage.

The story is an early twentieth-century adventure yarn framed as an intimate, late-night conversation. A minimally characterised first-person narrator, Sir Henry, listens while Barclay recounts the history of the explorer Charlie Tavor and his apparent discovery of a treasure. This frame gives the narrative the immediacy of oral storytelling while allowing Barclay to dominate as the principal voice. His repeated pauses, movements before the fire, cigarettes, gestures and changes of expression punctuate the long monologue and keep the hotel room present even as the tale ranges across Mongolia, Arabia, New York and England.

The prose is strongly colloquial, masculine and performative. Barclay speaks in American slang and commercial idiom—“faked dope,” “down and out,” “bucked,” “straight stuff,” “four-flushing”—and habitually interprets both morality and adventure through betting, bargaining and business. Formal or specialist terms such as “obiter dicta,” geographical coordinates and historical theories are set beside punchy vernacular expressions. This mixture makes him sound worldly, forceful and self-educated rather than scholarly. Period-specific ethnic language and stereotypes, including an explicit racial slur, mark the story’s historical milieu and its colonial adventure tradition.

Sentence rhythms alternate between expansive explanations and abrupt emphatic statements. Long passages accumulate geographical, historical and logistical detail, often through parallel clauses and lists; these are interrupted by fragments such as “Barclay paused,” “It hooked him,” or “No, he didn’t get it.” Ellipses, dashes, exclamation marks and repeated pauses imitate spontaneous speech, hesitation and dramatic timing. Contractions and direct address—especially “Sir Henry,” “you see” and “you know”—reinforce the sense that Barclay is improvising for a single listener. Repetition also supplies emphasis: assertions about Tavor’s honesty, Hardman’s crookedness, the unknown regions of the earth and the exact truth of the map are reiterated until they become the premises of the final trick.

Dialogue drives almost the entire story. Characters are differentiated chiefly by speech: Barclay is slangy, cynical and energetic; Tavor is mild, trusting and conspicuously English; Hardman is terse, suspicious and acquisitive; Sir Henry asks the questions required to expose the solution. Barclay’s narration frequently embeds reconstructed conversations, producing several layers of reported speech. The result resembles both a club-room anecdote and a confidence trick performed on the reader. Physical description is sparse but memorable and morally suggestive: Barclay’s large, pock-marked face conveys rough power, Tavor’s plaster-like complexion signals exhaustion and impending death, and Hardman’s small “reptilian” eyes embody predatory calculation.

The plot is constructed as an extended piece of misdirection. It begins with the conventional idea of cursed treasure, develops into an exotic lost-caravan narrative and then narrows into a business bargain governed by exact verbal conditions. Copious references to the Shamo, El-Khali, ancient trade routes, maps, meridians and unexplored territory create documentary plausibility while encouraging the reader to assume that the treasure remains somewhere in Asia. The crucial information is nevertheless stated truthfully but ambiguously: the site is a level chalk-like plain near a coastal town, easily approached except for the final two miles. The ending retrospectively reinterprets every clue when “two miles” is revealed to mean vertical depth and the supposed desert treasure becomes gold in the wreck of the Titanic. The twist depends on literal truth, strategic omission and the reader’s unexamined spatial assumptions.

Suspense develops gradually rather than through action. Frequent digressions delay the transaction and cultivate mystery, while Barclay’s refrain that “the devil” is assisting him lends the scheme an air of supernatural justice. Yet the devil is largely metaphorical: the decisive forces are coincidence, rhetorical cunning and Hardman’s greed. The moral structure is knowingly paradoxical. Tavor, honest and brave but commercially helpless, receives his dream only because Barclay deceives a dishonest man without uttering a technical falsehood. Hardman is punished by his own acquisitiveness and cannot protest without damaging the cultivated scientific reputation he hopes to acquire. Barclay’s sardonic theology—God, the devil and luck treated as rival operators in a crooked system—gives the story its distinctive comic cynicism.

The tone combines admiration, indignation, sentiment and dry humour. Tavor is romanticised as a tireless imperial-era explorer, while his final wish for a country house, formal garden and lackeys supplies a sentimental motive for Barclay’s fraud. That sentiment is kept from becoming soft by Barclay’s hard-boiled diction and by the story’s pleasure in outwitting Hardman. Atmospheric details—the firelit room, curtained blue night, cigarette smoke rising toward the frescoed ceiling and dawn over Monaco—provide an elegant, leisured contrast to the deserts, docks and ocean depths in the embedded tale. Overall, the style is that of a polished oral mystery: discursive and geographically elaborate on the surface, tightly engineered underneath, and organised to culminate in a concise, epigrammatic final revelation.

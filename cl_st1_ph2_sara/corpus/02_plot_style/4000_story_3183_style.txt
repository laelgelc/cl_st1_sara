The story adopts a first-person, retrospective mode shaped by an unreliable, self-important narrator. He presents himself as sober, rational, and intellectually superior even while admitting to heavy eating, persistent drinking, drowsiness, and increasingly irrational conduct. This discrepancy creates sustained dramatic irony: his elaborate attempts to explain every mishap as a “natural accident” only make the supernatural pattern more obvious. The narrative voice is urbane and confessional but also pompous, using claims of dignity and reflective intelligence to expose the speaker’s vanity and limited self-awareness.

The prose is ornate, highly formal, and distinctly nineteenth-century in diction. Latinate words and ceremonious expressions—such as “consummated,” “betook myself,” “predisposed,” and “contemplative understanding”—elevate trivial actions to absurd importance. Long, periodic sentences accumulate qualifications, parentheses, lists, and precise circumstantial details before reaching their main point. This syntactic elaboration produces a mock-scholarly texture and slows the narration just enough to heighten the comic disproportion between style and subject. Formal circumlocutions frequently replace plain statements: drunkenness, nakedness, injury, and romantic humiliation are described with exaggerated delicacy. At moments of action, however, the syntax becomes more direct and sequential, allowing accidents to unfold rapidly.

The dominant tone is comic, satirical, and mock-Gothic. Fire, bodily danger, supernatural visitation, attempted suicide, and apparent persecution belong to Gothic or sensational fiction, but they are converted into farce through bathos, grotesque imagery, and improbable coincidence. The Angel’s body—assembled from a barrel, kegs, bottles, a canteen, and a funnel—is at once uncanny and ridiculous. Threats of death repeatedly collapse into physical comedy: a hog destabilizes a ladder, a wig ruins a proposal, a crow steals clothing, and a balloon rope prevents a fatal fall only to create another absurd predicament. The narrator’s calm, dignified phrasing intensifies rather than softens these grotesque effects.

Dialogue provides a strong stylistic contrast. The narrator speaks in polished, decorous English, while the Angel uses emphatic phonetic spelling, grammatical distortion, Germanisms, and malapropistic pronunciation: “Mein Gott,” “te troof,” “pizziness,” and “pelief.” This exaggerated eye dialect gives the Angel an immediately recognizable voice—booming, coarse, irritable, and comic—and turns each exchange into verbal slapstick. Repetition, insults, rhetorical questions, and misunderstandings animate the dialogue. The dialect is not naturalistic; it is a deliberately caricatural device that marks the Angel as alien and destabilizes the narrator’s linguistic authority.

The narrative is organized as an escalating chain of coincidences. An initial newspaper anecdote about an implausible accidental death establishes the governing motif, after which the narrator rejects the possibility of “odd” events and is subjected to a succession of them. Each episode follows a similar rhythm: a minor object or action is introduced, dismissed as insignificant, and later becomes the mechanism of disaster. The fractured clock glass admits a raisin stem; the missed insurance appointment magnifies the consequences of the fire; the lost hair leads to the wig catastrophe; and the drunken crow redirects the suicide attempt into an aerial ordeal. This careful planting of details gives the apparently chaotic plot a tightly engineered comic causality.

Pacing grows progressively faster and more episodic. The opening lingers over dinner, books, wine, and newspaper columns in leisurely descriptive prose. Once the Angel departs, the story accelerates through a catalogue of reversals, each more extreme than the last. Transitional phrases such as “Hereupon,” “in the meantime,” and “no sooner” sustain the feeling of mechanical inevitability. Repeated denials by the narrator act as refrains, preserving his skepticism while the evidence against it mounts.

The style relies heavily on irony, hyperbole, understatement, and literalized wordplay. The narrator calls obviously extraordinary occurrences ordinary, treats catastrophic losses as inconveniences, and describes ludicrous demands as “very reasonable.” Euphemism creates additional comedy, particularly when he refers to trousers as “the most indispensable portion of my apparel” or to suicide as having “considered it high time to die.” Verbal echoes and double meanings are also important: drunken “drops,” bottled limbs, spirits, and the Angel’s role as both supernatural agent and embodiment of alcohol bind the fantasy to the narrator’s intoxication.

Learned allusions, foreign phrases, book lists, quotations, and references to literary or religious works contribute to a parodically erudite surface. They characterize the narrator as cultivated while also suggesting indiscriminate consumption: books, food, wine, journalism, and ideas are all taken in to excess and imperfectly digested. Embedded forms—the newspaper report, verse quotation, multilingual phrases, and extended dialogue—add tonal variety and mimic the miscellany of periodical literature.

The ending uses a circular structure to preserve ambiguity. After the extravagant sequence of disasters, the narrator awakens amid the original room’s debris, with the empty liquor vessel nearby. The supernatural adventure can therefore be read as drunken dream, punitive fantasy, or genuine intervention. Rather than resolving that uncertainty, the closing sentence adopts the narrator’s supernatural explanation with mock solemnity. Overall, the style blends grotesque fantasy, mock erudition, unreliable narration, and escalating slapstick into a satire of complacent rationalism and the human tendency to deny inconvenient evidence.

The story is written as a documentary-style Western pursuit narrative, combining the factual manner of a newspaper report with the expansive scenery, moral certainty, and heroic characterization of frontier adventure fiction. It begins with a precise date, railway line, and location, then supplies distances, routes, mountain ranges, and procedural details that create an air of historical authenticity. The omniscient third-person narrator generally remains outside the action but speaks with considerable authority, explaining geography, reconstructing motives, assessing evidence, and providing background on both pursuer and pursued.

The prose is formal, confident, and distinctly period-inflected. Its vocabulary draws heavily on railroading, ranching, tracking, law enforcement, and mountain travel: “express car,” “through safe,” “running-iron,” “saddle stock,” “mesa,” “pack horses,” and “game trails.” Archaic or regional terms such as “hold-up,” “posse,” “rancho,” “six-shooter,” and “went over the road” reinforce the late nineteenth-century frontier setting. The narrator also uses elevated phrases—“silent solitude,” “incessant toil,” “his living grave”—alongside practical descriptions of horses, dogs, dynamite, and terrain. This mixture gives the story both utilitarian realism and romantic grandeur.

Sentence structure is predominantly long and syntactically layered, with subordinate clauses used to qualify events, explain causes, and connect action to background information. Chronological transitions such as “during the night,” “the next morning,” and “two weeks later” keep the extended chase intelligible. Semicolons, dashes, and cumulative clauses often allow the narration to pause for commentary without abandoning its forward movement. Shorter declarative sentences appear at points of emphasis—“He realized this,” “This afforded relief,” and “Now it became a hunt of man by man”—giving key developments a proverbial or dramatic weight.

Description is especially prominent in passages devoted to landscape. The Rockies are presented not merely as scenery but as an active strategic force: their canyons, lava beds, plateaus, passes, and isolated valleys alternately shelter the fugitives and exhaust their pursuers. The imagery becomes most ornate in the Grand Canyon passage, where granite walls form “serried columns” and “Gothic arch[es],” lending the wilderness an almost architectural sublimity. Elsewhere, personification makes the natural world legible to experienced frontiersmen: stars act as a compass, heat waves issue warnings, and wind and cloud seem to speak. Geography therefore functions both literally and symbolically, testing endurance and distinguishing those capable of reading the land from ordinary observers.

Characterization is archetypal but supplied with realistic occupational detail. Marshal Bob Banks is the relentless lawman, defined by competence, stamina, self-command, and an almost superhuman commitment to pursuit. The repeated comparison between him and a bloodhound makes relentlessness his central trait, while his tenderness toward the exhausted veteran dog humanizes him without diminishing his authority. Peg-Leg Eldridge is constructed as Banks’s dark counterpart: equally skilled in plainscraft, horsemanship, concealment, and endurance, but directed toward crime rather than law. His biography presents him partly as a “child of circumstances,” shaped by the disorder following the Civil War and unable to adapt when frontier customs hardened into settled law. This grants him complexity and stature while stopping short of excusing him.

The narrator’s moral position is clear. Law and punishment are treated as ultimately inevitable, while criminal ingenuity can delay justice but cannot defeat it. The narration admires Peg-Leg’s skill—calling him “an artist in his calling”—yet consistently identifies the robbery as an “outrage” and its perpetrators as criminals. The story’s governing irony is that Banks loses the literal bloodhounds but proves to be the true “human bloodhound.” This metaphor develops from practical description into the story’s central emblem and is finally voiced by Eldridge himself, whose pearl-button message serves as grudging recognition of the marshal’s superiority.

Pacing alternates between compressed action and prolonged pursuit. The robbery is executed and narrated rapidly, emphasizing its efficiency, whereas the chase unfolds through repeated cycles of climbing, tracking, resting, changing horses, crossing railways, and gathering testimony. This repetition conveys distance and physical attrition, though the narrative frequently summarizes whole days rather than dramatizing individual scenes. Suspense comes less from immediate confrontation than from measurements of the gap between the parties, the deterioration of animals, the loss of the dogs, and the possibility that the trail may disappear. The eventual arrest is handled through a sharp temporal leap and a compact set piece at a horse race, where the marshal uses surprise and timing rather than open violence.

Dialogue is sparse and strategically placed. Most information is conveyed through narrated report, preserving the story’s historical and testimonial tone. The shepherd’s broken-Spanish account provides eyewitness evidence and local color, while Eldridge’s final speech supplies the strongest individual voice in the story. His closing remark is formal, sardonic, and threatening even in defeat, allowing the narrative to end on character rather than exposition. The delayed revelation of Banks’s identity at the horse race—“One of these strangers was a United States marshal”—also reflects the story’s preference for understated dramatic disclosure.

The style includes broad generalizations that sound like frontier maxims: “You can believe the dog when you doubt your own eyes,” “Man-hunting is exciting sport,” and “Forcing a tired horse an extra hour has left many an amateur rider afoot.” Such statements create a seasoned raconteur’s voice, as though the narrator were drawing on practical experience and instructing readers in the realities of pursuit. Similar authorial observations about criminals, animals, and tracking occasionally interrupt the action, producing a didactic quality characteristic of older adventure and sporting prose.

Overall, the story’s style is factual yet romantic, procedural yet mythmaking. Its dominant effects come from precise frontier detail, panoramic geography, sustained chronological narration, restrained dialogue, moralized characterization, and the symbolic parallel between tracker and quarry. The result is a lawman-centered Western in which endurance, fieldcraft, and patience matter more than gunplay, and justice is portrayed as a force that may lose the visible trail but never abandons the hunt.

The story uses a polished, urbane realist style characteristic of early twentieth-century magazine fiction. Its prose is formal but accessible, combining social observation, brisk incident, sentimental moral development, and gentle satire. The narrative is told by an omniscient third-person narrator who remains closely aligned with Arnold Thorndike’s consciousness. Readers see the courthouse, its inhabitants, and the value of time largely through the financier’s privileged assumptions, while the narrator maintains enough distance to expose his vanity and limitations. Epithets such as “the great man” and “the Wisest Man in Wall Street” operate ironically: they acknowledge Thorndike’s public stature while quietly questioning what wisdom means.

The diction shifts according to social setting. Descriptive narration tends to be cultivated and precise, using phrases such as “the dramatic qualities of the situation,” “the secure and lofty heights of motherhood,” and “the long line of derelicts.” Court officials and working people speak more colloquially and directly, with contractions, slang, abrupt commands, and occasional profanity. Andrews’s speech is especially vigorous and informal, lending the courthouse scenes comic energy and making Thorndike’s refined manners appear stiff and sheltered. Capitalized words such as “SINCE,” “SAID,” “YOU,” and “NOW” reproduce emphatic speech in a manner associated with period popular fiction.

Sentence structure alternates between compact, businesslike exchanges and longer, balanced narrative sentences. The opening dialogue is clipped and efficient, mimicking the rapid handling of correspondence in a financier’s office. Longer sentences accumulate lists of Thorndike’s immense concerns—railroads, international loans, art purchases, cables, trusts—to dramatize the scale of his power and contrast it with Spear’s apparently minor case. Parallel constructions and escalating catalogues are frequently used for comic effect. Parenthetical interruptions and dashes introduce qualifications, ironic afterthoughts, or social detail, while short standalone statements deliver reversals and punch lines.

Dialogue carries much of the characterization and pacing. Thorndike speaks in commands and decisions, accustomed to instant obedience; Andrews speaks with irreverent confidence; the secretary contributes dry skepticism; and the judge adopts ceremonious, public rhetoric. Character is revealed less through direct psychological explanation than through behavior, verbal habits, and reactions to altered status. Thorndike’s growing discomfort is shown through covered jewelry, suppressed irritation, attention to the clock, and resentment at being known merely as the man “with Mr. Andrews.” Repeated commands to sit, stand, wait, or follow create a comic reversal in which a man who controls vast institutions must submit to ordinary procedure.

The humor is primarily situational, ironic, and character-based. Thorndike imagines that his intervention will be immediate and theatrically benevolent, only to discover that neither the district attorney nor the court treats his time as uniquely valuable. His calculation that rescuing Spear will cost a thousand dollars reduces compassion to a financial transaction, but the story gradually converts that calculation into genuine human involvement. Recurring references to the monetary value of his minutes sharpen the central contrast between market value and moral value. The final question about what it cost to keep Spear out of jail returns to this motif, and Thorndike’s answer—“it was worth it”—completes the shift from accounting to feeling.

The structure follows a clear moral-comic arc. An apparently trivial letter introduces Spear; the recollection of flowers establishes an emotional motif; the spring morning prompts Thorndike’s impulsive visit; the courthouse strips him of privilege; observation of other cases awakens sympathy; and his encounter with Spear and Mrs. Spear confirms his change. The narrative delays Spear’s appearance for much of the story, making Thorndike’s enforced waiting the principal action. This postponement allows the courthouse to function as an education: before Thorndike can help one defendant, he must encounter an entire social world previously excluded from his experience.

Pacing is brisk at the beginning and end but deliberately slowed in the courthouse. Procedural obstacles, waiting-room visitors, corridor crowds, courtroom formalities, and successive sentencing cases accumulate episodically. This extended delay reproduces Thorndike’s impatience while gradually redirecting his attention from his own schedule to other people’s lives. The conclusion accelerates into sociable comedy, with handshakes, congratulations, a luncheon invitation, and the banker’s gleeful decision to “waste” the rest of the day.

Descriptions rely on strong social and sensory contrasts. Wall Street is associated with speed, control, money, art, telegraphs, and international affairs; the courthouse is gloomy, crowded, damp, acrid, and physically degrading. The movement from spring sunshine into the “gloom of an echoing rotunda” gives the setting symbolic force. Yet the courthouse, initially presented through Thorndike’s disgust, becomes the place where he experiences warmth, usefulness, and fellowship. Spring imagery frames this moral renewal: clean air, sunlight, flowers, and the return outdoors at the end all suggest emotional reawakening.

Characterization frequently uses compressed visual types—“fierce-eyed aliens,” “collarless tramps,” “thin-lipped Broadwayards”—reflecting both the period’s journalistic shorthand and Thorndike’s class-bound gaze. Some ethnic and social labels are dated and stereotyped by contemporary standards. Even so, the story progressively complicates its initial categorization of the courthouse population. People first perceived as an undifferentiated mass of “unwashed humans” emerge as wives, mothers, workers, failures, and loyal friends with individual claims on sympathy.

The emotional register blends sentiment with restraint. The flowers Spear picked for his mother, the imprisoned son’s cake, Mrs. Spear’s fixed gaze, and Spear’s shame and gratitude are openly affecting details, but they are usually balanced by humor or irony. This prevents the moral lesson from becoming wholly solemn. Thorndike’s transformation is not presented as a dramatic renunciation of wealth; instead, it appears as a temporary liberation from efficiency, prestige, and duty. His benevolence remains mixed with vanity, competitiveness, and childlike pleasure, making him comic but not contemptible.

Overall, the style is socially observant, theatrical, and morally optimistic. It uses reversal to humble a powerful protagonist, juxtaposition to expose the narrowness of monetary thinking, and lively dialogue to animate institutional settings. The governing tone is affectionate satire: the narrative mocks Thorndike’s self-importance while allowing his dormant generosity to become real. Its central stylistic movement is from abstraction to immediacy—from loans, railroads, art prices, and calculated minutes to faces, voices, flowers, sunlight, and personal contact.

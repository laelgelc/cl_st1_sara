The story adopts the manner of an early twentieth-century British supernatural tale, combining country-house social comedy with whimsical fantasy and a mild, carefully controlled sense of the uncanny. Its prevailing tone is urbane, amused and gently ironic rather than frightening. The supernatural presence is treated as mischievous and delicate—a “Borrower” attracted to “little bright and lovely things”—so that wonder gradually displaces anxiety. Even at the moment of apparent revelation, the narration preserves ambiguity through qualifications such as “This he thinks he saw” and “he admits,” maintaining tension between an actual fairy visitation, a dream and imperfect perception.

The narration is in the third person and remains closely aligned with Dutton’s consciousness. His impressions, private jokes, self-corrections and shifting emotions govern what the reader sees. Free indirect discourse frequently blends his thoughts into the narrative: “Would he never go?”, “Odd,” and “What a blunderer I am!” This close focalisation allows the supernatural idea to emerge organically from his imagination before it appears to acquire external reality. The narrator nevertheless retains a lightly detached, teasing authority, particularly when describing Dutton’s physical awkwardness or retrospectively questioning the reliability of his experience.

Dutton is characterised through humorous contrasts. He is “big, shy, awkward,” slow-moving and “elephantine,” while the unseen creature is tiny, nimble, soft and elusive. This opposition in scale controls much of the imagery: Dutton and the servant resemble ants in a cave; the bedroom can swallow studios and easels; the four-poster and cupboards appear to watch or judge their human occupants; the goblin moves more lightly than a mouse’s whiskers. The vast house diminishes Dutton, but his own enormousness in turn threatens the miniature supernatural world. This produces a comic inversion in which an intimidated guest becomes a protective giant, moving on tiptoe lest he injure something “like a butterfly with a broken wing.”

The prose alternates between crisp character exposition and richly elaborated description. Short declarative sentences—“They swallowed him,” “Would he never go?”, “It was an absurd position”—give the narration firmness and comic timing. These are set against longer, cumulative sentences filled with semicolons, parenthetical qualifications and sequences of sensory detail. Lists are especially prominent, whether cataloguing the contents of the bag or the architectural features of the room. The recurring inventory of studs, scissors, cigar-cutter and pencil-sharpener turns mundane objects into narrative motifs and makes each disappearance measurable and concrete.

The diction moves between polished, formal narration and sensuous fairy-tale language. The country house is rendered through imposing words such as “solemn,” “formidable,” “capacious,” “sepulchral” and “embrasured,” while the creature’s movements attract diminutives and tactile or musical adjectives: “tiny,” “wee,” “dainty,” “delicate,” “soft,” “elegant” and “tender.” Repetition intensifies this miniature aesthetic, particularly in phrases such as “closer it came, and closer” and “deep, deep down.” Capitalised labels including “Borrower” and “Adventurer” elevate the unseen being into a folkloric personality without defining it too precisely.

Personification animates the physical setting throughout. The four-poster watches “contemptuously,” cupboards express “grave surprise,” the fireplace gapes, and lost objects display a “quizzical, malicious air of innocence.” This makes the room itself seem complicit in the disappearances and prepares the reader to accept a world in which reflections may become passages into another realm. Light and reflective surfaces are crucial visual motifs: silver objects, glass, mirrors and moonlight create both attraction and optical uncertainty. The climax deliberately blurs perception through intersecting reflections, allowing the fantastic event to be glimpsed rather than fully exposed.

Dialogue provides social texture and controls the release of supernatural information. Dutton’s speech is restrained, embarrassed and initially curt, reflecting both his class position and his fear of appearing foolish. The Irish servant’s dialogue is rendered through conspicuous phonetic spelling—“sorr,” “thim,” “wurrld,” “toime”—and syntactic markers of brogue. His voice is warm, eager and confessional, contrasting with the oppressive formality of the great house. He functions as a folkloric intermediary who already understands the rules of the intrusion. The dialect treatment is period-specific and somewhat stereotyped, but it gives the boy an immediately recognisable oral presence and associates him with Irish fairy lore.

The story’s structure is incremental and circular. It begins with social discomfort, moves through a suggestive remark, introduces minor disappearances, establishes rules for the “borrowing,” and culminates in a nocturnal attempt to witness the act. Suspense develops through recurrence rather than danger: each vanished object confirms the pattern while preserving plausible deniability. The moonlit episode slows the pace dramatically, using hushed sound imagery and minute physical movements to stretch a brief event into an extended climax. The final paragraph then returns abruptly to practical reality. Dutton neither proves nor publicly discusses what happened; he simply buys another pencil-sharpener. This understated ending preserves the mystery while giving the supernatural episode a dry comic aftertaste.

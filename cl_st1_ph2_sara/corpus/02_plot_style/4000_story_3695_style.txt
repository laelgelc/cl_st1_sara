The story adopts the form of a first-person travelogue that repeatedly expands into comic essay, historical digression, legend, scenic meditation, and anecdote. Its narrator is an observant, opinionated traveller who speaks directly and confidentially, presenting personal impressions as though in informal conversation with the reader. The perspective moves freely between immediate experience, recollection, hearsay, speculative reconstruction, and mock-authoritative commentary. Rather than following a conventional plot, the narrative progresses associatively: Swiss railways lead to preferred modes of travel, scenery to republican history, the Jungfrau to saints and miracles, shifting shadows to geological time, and finally to an extended comic encounter with the King of Greece.

The dominant tone is humorous, irreverent, and genial, though it can become sincerely awestruck or politically earnest. Sublime descriptions of mountains and national freedom coexist with satire of tourism, monarchy, organized religion, historical scholarship, legal procedure, and human self-importance. The comedy often depends on bathos: elevated language and solemn subjects are abruptly reduced to practical, commercial, or colloquial terms. Irish kings become underemployed laborers, a missionary becomes a “walking delegate,” religious conversion resembles coercive salesmanship, and kingship is misunderstood as salaried general jobbing. The narrator frequently adopts a straight-faced manner while advancing increasingly absurd claims, allowing exaggeration to masquerade as sober explanation.

The diction ranges widely. Scenic passages use elevated, lyrical, and sometimes biblical language—“heaven’s gates,” “the throne,” “spotless mass,” “shroud of snow”—while comic passages favor American colloquialisms, commercial vocabulary, slang, and deliberately blunt phrasing. Archaisms appear in reconstructed legend and mock history, especially in formal speech such as “why dost thou,” but they are undercut by modern references to labor unions, wages, courts, business, and moral responsibility. This collision of linguistic registers is central to the style: the sacred is described through the language of employment, the prehistoric through social comedy, and royal authority through the idiom of trade.

The syntax is expansive and rhythmically varied. Long, cumulative sentences build through parallel clauses, parenthetical qualifications, repetitions, and escalating lists. These structures are especially prominent in panoramic description and comic exaggeration, where each added phrase enlarges the image until it becomes extravagant. Short sentences and fragments then provide emphasis or comic timing: “Nothing going on,” “This fact is colossal,” or “The likeness is there, and is unmistakable.” Dashes are used heavily to mimic spontaneous speech, introduce corrections, prolong jokes, and permit the narrator to revise his thought in public. Repeated formulas such as “more and better,” “plenty,” “before,” and “a day so far back” give certain passages an oratorical, incantatory force.

Description is intensely visual and often theatrical. The Jungfrau is framed like a painting, personified as a virgin, transformed into a blazing conflagration, and treated as the face of a gigantic clock. Color, light, contrast, shape, and scale dominate the landscape writing. Natural effects are made legible through familiar human forms: mountain shadows become a face, a goatee, a shoe, a tower, and a pointing printer’s fist. Personification is sustained at length, particularly in the courtship fantasy in which the shadow lays its head upon the Virgin’s breast. Hyperbole magnifies both grandeur and absurdity, extending measurements from acres and miles to eons and “a thousand centuries.”

The treatment of time is similarly elastic. Precise clock times—four, four-thirty, 4:40, five, and six—lend observational credibility to the shadow sequence, while the narrative simultaneously reaches backward through the Middle Ages, Rome, prehistory, and geological epochs. This juxtaposition makes modern history seem trivial beside the antiquity of the landscape. Exact detail is often used not for realism alone but as a comic device: specificity gives impossible claims the appearance of scientific or historical authority.

The narrator’s humor relies on exaggeration, anachronism, literalization, anticlimax, false logic, and mock scholarship. He often begins from a plausible fact and elaborates it into fantasy without marking a clear boundary between the two. Statements are framed as reasonable conclusions even when their premises are ridiculous. The skeleton’s courtroom testimony, for instance, is assessed according to modern evidentiary standards rather than supernatural possibility, shifting attention from the miracle to the witness’s legal credibility. This habit of calmly debating the wrong issue produces much of the deadpan effect.

The dialogue is strongly characterized and socially revealing. In the final anecdote, the German-American’s speech is energetic, intrusive, repetitive, and filled with contractions, business clichés, practical advice, and self-confident misreadings. He dominates the exchange, interrupts qualifications, answers his own questions, and forces royal circumstances into a middle-class narrative of employment, family responsibility, trades, promotion, and ownership. The king’s restrained, evasive replies create a comic imbalance: his politeness sustains the misunderstanding while the other speaker’s certainty continually deepens it. Character emerges less through description than through contrasting speech rhythms and assumptions.

Structurally, the piece favors digression over economy. Transitions such as “However,” “But to continue,” “Meantime,” and “I suppose” openly acknowledge the narrator’s wandering course. Episodes often begin with a factual or descriptive premise, swell into imaginative or satirical elaboration, and end in a concise comic judgment. The pacing alternates between lingering scenic contemplation, rapid historical burlesque, and extended dialogue. The result feels conversationally improvised, yet individual passages are carefully shaped toward reversals and punch lines.

Beneath the humor lies a consistent admiration for political liberty, independence, practical intelligence, and refusal to submit to arbitrary authority. Switzerland is praised not merely for scenery but for its republican history, while dynastic wars, monarchies, clerical legends, and institutional pretension are diminished through ridicule. At the same time, the narrator remains skeptical of solemn hero worship and disputes the importance of literal historical trivia when symbolic or moral truth matters more. The overall style combines romantic responsiveness to nature with democratic skepticism, treating grandeur sincerely but refusing to let any human institution remain grand for long.

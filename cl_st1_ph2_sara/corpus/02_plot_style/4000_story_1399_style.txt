The story adopts a reflective, antiquarian style characteristic of nineteenth-century moral romance and the familiar essay. It is narrated retrospectively in the first person by an educated, observant visitor whose account moves freely between recollection, character sketch, anecdote, philosophical speculation, and social commentary. The narrator is not merely a witness: he repeatedly interprets the people he encounters, turning ordinary transactions in a tombstone-carver’s workshop into meditations on grief, memory, love, mortality, and immortality. His judgments are presented as provisional and personal through phrases such as “methinks,” “it seemed to me,” and “perchance,” giving the voice a contemplative, slightly self-questioning quality even when its conclusions are expansive.

The prose is formal, elevated, and deliberately old-fashioned. Its vocabulary mixes abstract moral and spiritual terms—“Providence,” “sympathy,” “individuality,” “consciousness,” and “immortality”—with the material language of the sculptor’s trade: marble, slate, chisel, slabs, inscriptions, and carved emblems. Archaic or literary words such as “thither,” “whithersoever,” “bespeak,” “fain,” “lugubrious,” and “sepulchral” reinforce the period atmosphere. Biblical echoes and religious idioms are frequent, including references to resurrection, the soul, dust, Heaven, and “man that is born of woman.” The diction can be ornate, but it remains attentive to concrete objects, especially gravestones and their symbols.

The syntax is predominantly long, balanced, and periodic. Sentences often accumulate qualifications, contrasts, and parallel clauses before arriving at a moral or ironic conclusion. Semicolons, dashes, parentheses, and appositive phrases allow the narrator to suspend an idea while adding commentary or refining its implications. Antithesis is especially important: living and dead, body and soul, earth and Heaven, sincerity and convention, remembrance and forgetting, sorrow and comfort are continually set against one another. Shorter sentences and emphatic repetitions occasionally interrupt this flow—“These, these were graves where loved ones slept”—to intensify emotion or mark a decisive insight.

The tone blends melancholy, tenderness, dry comedy, and moral seriousness. Death is omnipresent, yet the treatment is rarely oppressive. The tombstone-carver’s cheerful practicality provides comic relief, while the narrator’s imagination repeatedly discovers absurdity in solemn customs. Much of the humor depends on incongruity: grief is negotiated in “dollars and cents”; a supposedly dead husband invalidates an expensive epitaph; a man considers ordering memorials for several wives in bulk; and the sculptor judges the return of a living sailor chiefly as a waste of excellent marble. This comedy is gently satirical rather than cruel. It exposes vanity, thrift, conventional sentiment, and professional narrowness while preserving sympathy for the people involved.

Mr. Wigglesworth is drawn through affectionate caricature. His entire understanding of humanity is filtered through tombstones, yet his narrow professional outlook repeatedly yields compact pieces of wisdom. His remarks are plain, earthy, and aphoristic, contrasting with the narrator’s elaborate reflections. Statements such as the claim that familiar epitaphs “stretch to suit a great grief and shrink to fit a small one” condense complex emotional truths into homely imagery. Dialogue therefore serves both comic and philosophical purposes: it punctures the narrator’s abstractions, reveals character economically, and converts each episode into a small debate about death or remembrance.

Structurally, the story is episodic rather than plot-driven. The framing situation—the narrator’s visits to the sculptor’s workshop—supports a sequence of customer portraits and memorial commissions. Each vignette presents a different relation between the living and the dead: idealized first love, repeated widowhood, marital fidelity, mistaken death, childhood bereavement, hatred surviving its object, religious faith, unbelief, miserliness, and anticipation of one’s own death. Transitions often arise by association rather than chronology, as one object or incident “reminds” the narrator of another. The cumulative arrangement gives the work the quality of a gallery of moral cases, with the workshop functioning as a miniature theatre of human nature.

Characterization is selective and emblematic. Secondary figures are identified through a few sharply chosen traits—a “storm-beaten” whaling captain, a tavern-keeper bargaining with board, a miser commissioning his own grand monument, or a pale girl choosing her headstone. They are psychologically interpreted, but they also represent recognizable moral or emotional types. Gravestone designs reinforce this emblematic method: the broken rose, weeping willows, open book, broken bow, scattered arrows, cherub, urn, and death’s-head visually summarize the lives or sentiments attached to them. At the same time, the story questions whether conventional symbols can adequately represent individual grief.

Figurative language is abundant and sustained. Metaphors repeatedly connect emotional experience with inscription, sculpture, commerce, harvest, fragrance, shadow, and imprisonment. Affection “graves” letters upon the heart; sorrow interprets and remakes a commonplace epitaph; a broken rose sheds sweetness through forty years; hatred becomes sustenance; and gravestones press upon the survivor’s spirit like dungeon architecture. Personification is frequent: Sorrow reads, Death engraves, the grave speaks, and the ocean preserves or buries memory. Images of ascent and confinement—skies, butterflies, souls, dust, slabs, tombs, and exuviae—organize the final philosophical conflict.

The narrator’s characteristic method is to begin with a concrete observation and enlarge it into a universal proposition. A widow’s choice of memorial prompts reflection on feminine grief; a conventional verse becomes an argument about the private meanings of cliché; an enemy’s monument leads to the paradox that hatred may be “love under a mask.” These meditations are often idealizing and sentimental, but irony prevents them from becoming wholly solemn. The narrator also recognizes the uncertainty of his interpretations, and the conclusion deliberately withholds a simple doctrine. His rejection of gravestones as symbols of bodily confinement is challenged by Wigglesworth’s instinctive defense of remembrance, leaving the story suspended between transcendence and attachment.

Overall, the style is meditative, symbol-rich, and gently satirical, with philosophical depth emerging from provincial scenes and practical conversation. Its governing paradox is that sorrow may comfort, conventional language may become intensely personal, narrow experience may produce wisdom, and objects associated with death may reveal the deepest energies of life.

The story uses a nineteenth-century moral-allegorical style, blending Christmas fantasy, social criticism, religious reflection, and civic commentary. Its governing conceit—the spirits of church bells gathering in a steeple to report on the dying year—turns the city into a symbolic moral landscape. The bells are both witnesses and voices of conscience: elevated above ordinary life, they can observe commerce, wealth, poverty, worship, immigration, education, and political reform while remaining unable to intervene directly. This creates a characteristic tension between supernatural enchantment and urgent realism.

The narration is third-person, omniscient, and openly sympathetic. Descriptive passages establish a wintry, moonlit, reverential atmosphere through images of snow-covered roofs, deserted streets, stars, shadows, and resonant music. The narrative then recedes for long stretches while each bell-spirit delivers an extended dramatic monologue. These speeches provide the story’s main structure, with each speaker representing a district, social constituency, denomination, or reforming outlook. Characterization is consequently emblematic rather than psychologically intricate: clothing, caps, ornaments, voices, and verbal mannerisms immediately identify each spirit’s sphere. The maritime bell speaks in nautical metaphors, the Catholic bell uses Irish-inflected phrasing and ecclesiastical imagery, and the reforming bell adopts military and political language.

The diction combines elevated devotional language with colloquial vigor. Biblical phrases, hymnal cadences, literary allusions, proverbs, civic references, and personified abstractions coexist with expressions such as “bless you,” “hocus-pocus,” “spin my yarn,” and “up and at ’em.” The prose frequently employs balanced clauses, accumulative lists, antithesis, and parallel constructions: wealth is set against poverty, profession against conduct, earthly suffering against heavenly consolation, and sectarian difference against universal charity. Sentences are often long and rhythmically shaped, with semicolons and coordinated clauses allowing moral arguments to build toward emphatic conclusions. Exclamations, apostrophes, rhetorical questions, repeated phrases, and direct appeals to imagined listeners give the speeches a sermon-like, performative force.

The tone is earnest, reformist, sentimental, and didactic, though relieved by warmth, humor, and theatricality. Satire is directed at commercial speculation, fashionable extravagance, complacent congregations, religious narrowness, sensational literature, and shallow education. Yet the criticism is rarely wholly cynical. Each report balances rebuke with praise, identifying honest merchants, generous wealthy people, compassionate clergy, brave sailors, immigrants, teachers, reformers, and charitable believers. This pattern gives the work a fundamentally hopeful moral vision: society is flawed, but individual sympathy and collective reform remain possible.

Sentiment reaches its height in the episode of the impoverished seamstress. Here the style slows into a miniature melodrama, using progressive physical detail—the fading cheeks, hollow eyes, thin shawl, bare feet, dying lamp, and abandoned doves—to make deprivation emotionally immediate. Repetition of “I saw,” the hourly striking of the clocks, and the bell’s unanswered cries create a ritualized movement toward death. The treatment is intentionally pathetic and religiously consolatory: social neglect is condemned, while the girl’s death is transformed into entrance into a heavenly home. Concrete urban suffering is thus framed through Christian symbolism, especially the sparrow, the cross, Christmas, and the contrast between shelterless earthly life and divine welcome.

Imagery is strongly organized around sound. Bells ring, toll, clash, cry, chime, jangle, echo, and sing; voices are sonorous, tuneful, breezy, or vigorous. Sound becomes a metaphor for moral communication, while human deafness signifies spiritual indifference. Light imagery performs a similar function: moonlight, lamps, stars, dawn, beacons, and heavenly brightness suggest conscience, revelation, and hope. Other recurring metaphorical systems include commerce as gambling, religion as shepherding, reform as warfare, life as a sea voyage, and the city as a living body whose districts reveal different moral conditions.

The pacing is episodic and cumulative rather than plot-driven. The framing vigil supplies a simple temporal arc from midnight to dawn, but the real movement comes from the succession of reports. Grave social indictment gives way to tragedy, then nautical comedy, sectarian debate, reformist optimism, and finally communal song. Tonal transitions are often marked by gestures or atmospheric responses—the spirits uncovering their heads, music floating upward, laughter chiming through the belfry, or soft air seeming to whisper assent. These touches preserve the fairy-tale atmosphere amid lengthy public argument.

Dialogue is highly individualized but also rhetorical. The speakers sound less like naturalistic conversationalists than public orators, preachers, reformers, and representative social voices. Dialect and occupational idiom are used broadly to distinguish them, sometimes approaching affectionate caricature. The humor depends on mimicry, denominational stereotypes, puns, and shifts from solemnity to homely speech. Even so, the exchanges ultimately promote tolerance: competing traditions are allowed to criticize one another, but the final ethical standard is charity rather than doctrinal victory.

Overall, the style is that of a civic Christmas sermon recast as a supernatural pageant. It seeks to move the reader through picturesque fantasy, moral indignation, tears, laughter, and hope. Its final dawn and collective hymn resolve the competing voices into a universal Christian-humanitarian message, with “Peace on earth, good will to men” serving both as devotional conclusion and as a practical demand for economic honesty, compassion, religious breadth, and social responsibility.

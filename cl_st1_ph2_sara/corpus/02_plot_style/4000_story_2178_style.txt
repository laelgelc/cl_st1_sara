The story adopts a dry, urbane, satirical style built around the manners and machinery of the British colonial administration in India. Its first-person narrator is both witness and amused commentator: he participates at the edge of events, claims privileged knowledge of official life, and relates Nafferton’s revenge with a mixture of admiration, irony, and moral detachment. The voice is conversational and anecdotal, frequently addressing the reader directly—“You may think”—or digressing into general observations about regional character, bureaucratic habits, and varieties of official “earnestness.” These digressions create the impression of an insider telling a well-known service anecdote while also broadening the personal quarrel into an institutional comedy.

The tone is predominantly sardonic, playful, and mock-serious. A minor dispute over a dangerous horse is elevated into a prolonged “hunt” conducted through paperwork, with Pinecoffin treated as quarry and bureaucracy as Nafferton’s weapon. The opening verse establishes this governing metaphor by replacing conventional sport with “the hunting of Man” and “the search for the Soul / To its ruin.” Hunting language then recurs throughout: the horse is an “official stalking-horse,” Pinecoffin “flounce[s] into the trap,” Nafferton spins a “web,” pursues “side-issues,” and finally “unmask[s] his batteries.” This mixture of hunting, warfare, and administrative imagery makes intellectual harassment feel both comic and predatory.

Much of the humour depends on incongruity and disproportion. The trivial topic of “Pig” acquires grand institutional dimensions through exhaustive research, official correspondence, ethnology, agricultural chemistry, religious policy, trade, tanning, and imperial finance. The repeated capitalization of “Pig” turns an ordinary animal into an absurdly imposing administrative abstraction. Similar mock dignity appears in invented departmental labels such as the “Department of Castigation” and in pompous formulations about the “Imperial aspect” of pork or the acclimatization of “the exotic Pig.” The narrative repeatedly contrasts inflated language with petty motives: Nafferton professes public-minded earnestness while pursuing a private grudge, and Pinecoffin’s desire to be thorough makes him vulnerable to limitless demands.

The diction moves fluently between colloquial speech, period Anglo-Indian vocabulary, technical terminology, and bureaucratic jargon. Informal phrases such as “sell him pups,” “get your knife into,” “that boy,” and “cry quite now” sit beside terms including “shikar,” “Civilian,” “Competition-wallah,” and geographical references to Punjab districts and frontier postings. Lists of specialist subjects—“bullocks and temporary wells, and opium-scrapers”—lend the setting administrative density, while mock-scholarly phrases such as “comparative longevity,” “ethnological excursus,” “flesh-former,” and “essentially hirsute characteristics of its oriental congener” parody the language of reports. The colonial vocabulary and generalized descriptions of Indian populations reflect the assumptions and hierarchies of the historical milieu rather than a neutral modern register.

Sentence structure is highly varied. Long, accumulative sentences reproduce the proliferation of official business, often using semicolons, parentheses, dashes, embedded qualifications, and enumerated subclauses. These are balanced by abrupt comic statements such as “They were Pig,” “Who asked for more,” and “Nafferton was very sympathetic.” Such short sentences serve as punchlines, crystallizing the absurdity after a passage of elaborate explanation. Capitalization, italics, quotation marks, numbered sections, and pseudo-documentary extracts imitate the visual and rhetorical forms of official files. The conspicuous “THEN” marks the revenge’s climax with theatrical emphasis.

The narrative is chronologically straightforward but episodic and recursive. It begins with the origin of the quarrel, pauses to explain the personalities and administrative system, then follows the correspondence through escalating stages. Each cycle has essentially the same comic pattern: Nafferton asks a question, Pinecoffin answers at excessive length, Nafferton files the answer and opens another branch of inquiry. Repetition creates cumulative humour while also conveying Pinecoffin’s entrapment and exhaustion. The pace accelerates near the end, when the bureaucratic persecution becomes public humiliation, and the final face-to-face meeting returns the plot to the original horse grievance.

Characterization is compressed into caricatural but effective traits. Pinecoffin is earnest, industrious, proud of his expertise, socially vulnerable, and constitutionally unable to leave a question incomplete. Nafferton is patient, vindictive, perceptive, and theatrically courteous; his cruelty lies less in overt aggression than in understanding how Pinecoffin’s vanity and professional conscience can be turned against him. The narrator sketches both through anecdote, regional stereotyping, occupational classification, reported behaviour, and ironic commentary rather than sustained psychological analysis. Government itself is personified as a credulous, forgetful organism that “gushes,” writes dangerous instructions on letters, and can be awakened by pulling the correct bureaucratic “string.”

Dialogue is sparse until the closing scene. Most conflict occurs indirectly through letters, reports, annotations, and quoted official prose, reinforcing the story’s central conceit that paperwork can function as a form of violence. When direct dialogue finally appears, Pinecoffin’s weak bluster and Nafferton’s mild politeness expose the imbalance between them. Nafferton’s final comparison between public mockery over the horse and Pinecoffin’s humiliation over the pigs gives the revenge a neat circular closure. The sweetness of his manner intensifies rather than softens his menace.

Overall, the style combines tall-tale narration, institutional satire, mock scholarship, and black comedy. Its principal effects come from verbal inflation, escalating repetition, ironic understatement, grotesquely precise detail, and the sustained transformation of administration into blood sport. Beneath the comic surface lies a sharp account of wounded pride, obsessive revenge, professional vanity, and a system in which official procedure can magnify private malice while allowing the aggressor to appear diligent, reasonable, and public-spirited.

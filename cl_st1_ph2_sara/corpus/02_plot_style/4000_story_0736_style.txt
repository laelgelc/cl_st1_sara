The story uses a formal, courtly, and morally instructive narrative style associated with medieval romance and the exemplary tale. An omniscient third-person narrator recounts events with calm authority, supplying characters’ motives, social circumstances, and emotional responses while maintaining a measured distance from them. The opening quickly establishes Federigo through lineage, reputation, and social standing, and the narrative repeatedly situates individual behavior within broader customs and expectations through phrases such as “as often happens,” “as is the custom,” and “the common laws of other mothers.” This gives the story the quality of a representative moral case as well as a personal tragedy.

The diction is elevated but accessible, with a strong emphasis on courtliness, virtue, honor, nobility, generosity, and duty. Titles and forms of address such as “Messer,” “Monna,” “Madonna,” and “my lady” preserve the Florentine social setting and reinforce its hierarchical etiquette. Abstract moral terms recur throughout, while material details—property, revenues, inheritance, tablecloths, the falcon, and the absence of pawnable objects—ground those ideals in economic reality. Poverty is presented not merely as deprivation but as a test of character: Federigo’s wealth disappears, yet his courtesy and largeness of spirit remain intact.

Sentences are often long, cumulative, and syntactically elaborate. They link causes, qualifications, and consequences through repeated coordination and subordination, producing the rhythm of an orally recounted chronicle. Repetitions such as “it happened that,” “and so,” “but,” and “since” make the progression feel inevitable. Parenthetical explanations clarify motives and circumstances, while balanced contrasts sharpen the moral design: Federigo is poor in money but rich in nobility; Giovanna appears harsh but is virtuous; the falcon is both his final possession and the means by which he proves himself worthy of future prosperity.

Dialogue is formal, extended, and rhetorically composed rather than naturalistic. Characters speak in courteous arguments, carefully acknowledging obligations, possible offenses, and social proprieties before making requests or replies. Giovanna’s appeal for the falcon proceeds through maternal duty, apology, and praise of Federigo’s nobility. Federigo’s response similarly turns grief into a speech about Fortune, service, and honor. These speeches reveal character through ethical reasoning rather than colloquial individuality. Even under intense emotion, both characters remain controlled by ceremonial manners.

The narrative pacing is economical across long spans of time but deliberately slow at the central meal and revelation. Early reversals—Federigo’s ruin, Giovanna’s widowhood, and the child’s illness—are summarized briskly. By contrast, the visit to Federigo’s house is rendered scene by scene, allowing dramatic irony to build: the reader sees him sacrifice the falcon before Giovanna explains that the bird is precisely what she has come to request. The meal therefore becomes the structural and emotional pivot. Suspense arises not from uncertainty about action but from the approaching collision of two honorable intentions.

Emotion is treated with restraint, though the plot itself is strongly sentimental. Grief, shame, love, and admiration are generally reported in dignified language rather than explored through intimate psychological detail. Federigo’s tears provide the most visible emotional rupture, yet even this is followed by an orderly explanation and physical proof—the feathers, feet, and beak. The boy’s death is similarly compressed into a few sober lines, preventing the tale from becoming melodramatic and preserving its exemplary tone.

A central stylistic device is irony. Federigo destroys the falcon in order to honor Giovanna just before she asks him to preserve it for her son; the gesture that seems to ruin her purpose ultimately secures her esteem and transforms his life. Fortune is personified as a hostile force that arranges painful reversals, but character determines their final moral meaning. The ending converts loss into restoration: the child and falcon die, yet Federigo’s sacrifice leads to marriage, wealth, and wiser conduct.

The conclusion has the compact decisiveness of a fable or novella. Giovanna’s declaration that she would rather have “a man who needs money than money that needs a man” functions as an aphoristic moral, reducing the story’s values to a memorable antithesis. The happy ending rewards innate worth while also correcting Federigo’s earlier extravagance: he regains prosperity but manages it “with more prudence.” Overall, the style blends courtly idealism, social realism, tragic irony, and moral symmetry in a polished, linear tale whose chief concern is the superiority of character to wealth.

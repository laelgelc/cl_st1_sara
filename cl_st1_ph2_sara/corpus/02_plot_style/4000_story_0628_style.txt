The story uses a comic epistolary form: a sequence of dated letters addressed to an intimate friend, culminating in a postscript that delivers the final deflation. The first-person narration creates immediacy and confines the reader to Mary Jane Dement’s partial, emotionally distorted understanding. Because Irene’s replies are absent, they are suggested through Mary Jane’s rebuttals, questions, and teasing accusations, making the correspondence feel like one half of an animated conversation. The dates divide the courtship into brisk episodes and allow fascination, anxiety, humiliation, and romantic fulfilment to escalate in clear stages.

The narrative voice is exuberant, self-dramatising, and highly self-aware. Mary Jane alternates between the polish of a socially ambitious heiress and the blunt vitality of her mining-camp upbringing. Her language moves easily from cultivated references, French expressions, and ceremonious phrasing to slang such as “gall,” “wormwood,” “little wretch,” and “blubbered.” This social and linguistic doubleness is central to the style: she wants to sound sophisticated, yet her strongest personality emerges through frontier idiom, comic aggression, and candid bodily embarrassment. Her direct addresses to “Irene,” affectionate apostrophes, mock demands, and repeated rhetorical questions give the prose an intimate, performative energy.

The humour depends heavily on verbal irony and disproportion. Mary Jane describes ordinary attraction in the elevated language of enchantment, black art, divinity, and supernatural danger. Dr. Barritz becomes a “god,” his gaze seems magical, and even the sand supposedly brightens beneath his feet. These romantic exaggerations are repeatedly punctured by practical or physical details: gossip gathered from a bell-boy, a man going straight to bed, a sleeping leg, fallen hair, a wet handkerchief, and the urge to kick while being embraced. Parenthetical qualifications and abrupt self-corrections continually undermine the narrator’s grand poses. The result is affectionate satire rather than cruelty; Mary Jane’s vanity and credulity are mocked, but so are social pretension, romantic convention, and the appetite for exotic mystery.

The diction is ornate and allusive without remaining uniformly formal. Biblical phrases, literary comparisons, mock-philosophical observations, melodramatic capitals, and references to fashionable occultism coexist with mining vocabulary and Western anecdote. Italics, dashes, exclamation marks, parentheses, repetitions, and capitalised pronouns reproduce the rhythms of emotional speech. Sentences often begin in stately balance, then expand through qualifications or collapse into comic confession. Long periodic passages are offset by short exclamations and fragments—“The strangest thing!”, “Fancy!”, “Irene, I blubbered!”—which sharpen the timing and simulate spontaneous letter-writing.

Characterisation is achieved chiefly through voice and anecdote rather than detached description. Mary Jane reveals herself while attempting to describe others: her idealisation of Barritz exposes her romantic susceptibility, her fear of being recognised exposes class insecurity, and her memories of “Gunny, Giggles, and Dumps” reveal loyalty beneath her embarrassment. Raynor is established through hyperbolic gossip metaphors and comic invective, while Barritz is initially filtered through infatuation and therefore appears almost impossibly accomplished. His relative silence preserves the mystery until the recognition scene.

Structurally, the story is a controlled comic misdirection. Hints of occult power, foreign travel, and hidden identity encourage a sensational interpretation, while the Redhorse reminiscence quietly plants the information needed for the real revelation. The climax parodies a conventional romantic tableau: sunset, beach, hand-holding, faintness, confession, and embrace are all present, but each is compromised by awkwardness and bodily comedy. The declaration that Barritz has loved Mary Jane for “years and years” turns the apparent supernatural mystery into a recognition plot when he identifies himself as Dumps. The postscript then removes the remaining exotic glamour by revealing that Raynor invented the Thug story to stimulate her curiosity.

The overall tone blends romantic comedy, frontier tall-tale, social satire, and affectionate burlesque. Its distinctive effect comes from the gap between Mary Jane’s interpretation and the facts available to the reader, from the collision of elevated rhetoric with mundane reality, and from a narrator whose pretensions are inseparable from her warmth, loyalty, and comic charm.

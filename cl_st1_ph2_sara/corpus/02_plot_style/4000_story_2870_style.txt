The story adopts a polished, urbane realist style shaped by the conventions of the late nineteenth-century French conte. It begins with an economical social frame: a group of affluent Parisian men dine together, and the most practiced raconteur among them supplies the evening’s anecdote. This framing establishes a sophisticated, masculine salon atmosphere while allowing most of the narrative to unfold as Joseph de Bardon’s first-person monologue. His voice is worldly, amused, self-confident and morally detached. He speaks candidly about desire, boredom and inconstancy, treating his own questionable conduct less as a confession than as material for an entertaining psychological puzzle.

The narration combines social observation with ironic character analysis. Bardon is introduced through balanced qualifications—brilliant but not profound, knowledgeable but not truly learned—which immediately define both his charm and his limitations. Similar contrasts recur throughout the story: cemeteries are restful yet sorrowful, grief is sincere-looking yet potentially theatrical, and the apparent widow is both vulnerable mourner and calculating “huntress of the tombs.” The narrative repeatedly moves between sentiment and skepticism, inviting emotion before puncturing it with worldly suspicion. Its dominant tone is therefore one of elegant, amused cynicism, though passages concerning mortality and autumn possess genuine melancholy.

The prose is descriptive and discursive, with long, carefully coordinated sentences interspersed with brisk dialogue. Bardon often wanders into reflections on Paris, art, death, memory and human vanity before returning to the action. These digressions are integral to his conversational persona: he notices monuments, judges sculpture, mocks epitaphs and converts the cemetery into a metaphorical “city of the dead.” Parallel constructions, accumulations and rhetorical questions give his speech the rhythm of an accomplished after-dinner performance. Repetition is also prominent, particularly around words such as “alone,” “sadness,” and “dead,” intensifying emotion while occasionally revealing how emotion is being used as persuasion.

The diction is formal, cosmopolitan and occasionally French-inflected. Expressions such as “petit verre,” “messieurs,” “grisette,” “distingué,” and “chic,” together with references to Parisian boulevards, Montmartre, artists and literary figures, preserve the story’s cultural setting and cultivated social register. Some phrasing has the slightly stiff or ornate quality of an older translation, including explicit explanatory clauses and melodramatic formulations. Visual details are precise and painterly: the woman’s fair hair resembles “rays of dawn” beneath dark mourning clothes, her veil spreads over the tomb, and the weakened autumn sun contributes to the atmosphere of decline. Black, white, gray and yellow form a subdued funerary palette.

Structurally, the story is a compact anecdote organized around delayed reversal. The leisurely opening establishes Bardon’s character; the cemetery walk creates a reflective, melancholy setting; the encounter with the grieving woman develops into seduction; and the later return to the cemetery produces the comic and unsettling revelation. The woman’s earlier behavior is retrospectively reinterpreted as a practiced routine. Foreshadowing is understated but clear: her conveniently absent maid, her readiness to accept intimacy, her separate cemetery and town costumes, and her rapid emotional recovery all hint at calculation. The ending does not explain her outright. Instead, a sequence of speculative questions preserves ambiguity and lets the final joke emerge through implication.

Dialogue is economical and socially coded. Polite forms of address—“madame,” “monsieur,” and “sir”—maintain a veneer of propriety while intimacy develops beneath it. The woman’s fragmented speech initially supports the impression of physical and emotional weakness, whereas Bardon’s short, helpful replies present seduction as gallantry. Their exchange gradually transforms mourning into flirtation without an abrupt tonal break. This smooth progression is central to the story’s irony: conventional gestures of sympathy, respectability and romantic consolation become instruments in a transactional encounter.

The setting functions symbolically as well as realistically. The cemetery is at once a repository of memory, a museum, a crowded city, a place of courtship and a marketplace for emotional opportunity. Its monuments and graves represent lasting devotion, but the behavior of the living exposes the instability of such ideals. The story repeatedly juxtaposes death with appetite, sacred remembrance with erotic pursuit, and solemn ritual with commercial exploitation. Even the restaurant near the cemetery, where mourners “celebrate the funeral,” embodies this collision of grief and ordinary pleasure.

Characterization is achieved more through conduct and narrative attitude than through interior depth. Bardon is perceptive about others but indulgently blind about himself. He suspects the woman of exploiting bereaved men, yet his own behavior is equally opportunistic: he turns her apparent grief into a sexual adventure and abandons her after three weeks. This unspoken symmetry gives the story much of its sophistication. The woman remains enigmatic because she is filtered entirely through his gaze; she is first aestheticized as a beautiful mourner, then desired as a lonely widow, and finally classified as a possible professional predator. The story does not settle which version is true, and Bardon’s assumptions are part of the satire.

Overall, the style is anecdotal, ironic, psychologically curious and socially observant. It blends leisurely philosophical commentary with a tightly engineered plot, using tonal contrast and a final twist to expose the theatricality of civilized behavior. Its characteristic effect comes from making melancholy and comedy coexist: the language lingers over death, loneliness and vanished love, while the narrative steadily converts those solemn subjects into a witty study of vanity, desire and deception.

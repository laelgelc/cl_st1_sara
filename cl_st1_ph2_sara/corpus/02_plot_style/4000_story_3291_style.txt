The story adopts the manner of a late-Victorian or Edwardian literary tale, combining antiquarian realism, restrained Gothic atmosphere, gentle comedy and sentimental pathos. Its first-person narrator is cultivated, observant and self-effacing, recounting the episode retrospectively with urbane composure. Although he participates in the action, he chiefly functions as a sympathetic witness who gradually interprets the strange household before being drawn into its sustaining fiction. His occasional asides—such as admissions that he was not listening or wry remarks about his own literary reputation—create intimacy without disturbing the narrative’s formal poise.

The prose is richly descriptive and deliberately leisurely. Long, carefully balanced sentences lead the reader through landscape, architecture and family history, while shorter statements mark discoveries or emotional turns. The syntax often employs semicolons, parenthetical qualifications, inversions and periodic constructions, giving the narration an educated, slightly old-fashioned cadence. Chronological transitions such as “in time,” “by-and-by,” “at any rate” and “and so” lend the account the rhythm of an oral reminiscence. Suspense is produced not by overt dramatization but by the measured accumulation of anomalous details: the English watermark on Fritz’s supposedly foreign letter, the implausibly feminine handwriting, Melchior’s vagueness and Wilhelmina’s instant apprehension.

The diction is elevated but concrete. Words such as “albeit,” “availed,” “prospective,” “inkling” and “docketed” contribute to the period register, while exact names for plants, paintings, architectural features and objects establish social and material specificity. Spanish chestnuts, the ha-ha, Jacobean masonry, Dutch masters, faded gilt and decaying lead statues place the story within a cultivated world whose inherited grandeur is physically collapsing. The references to painters, naval history, Shakespeare, Virgil, Tennyson, heraldry and Church liturgy assume a classically educated readership. Latin quotations and family mottoes are not merely ornamental: they reinforce the household’s dependence on lineage, memory and inherited forms.

The visual style relies strongly on contrasts between magnificence and neglect. The chestnut avenue remains noble, but its road has vanished beneath grass; valuable paintings perish in their frames; elaborate interiors are carpetless; flowers and creepers trail unsupported; clocks, statues and fountains have lost parts or ceased to function. Decay is rendered through precise, sensuous particulars rather than generalized gloom: cracked shutters, missing nails, moss-dappled gravel, snail tracks and turf plugging a Triton’s conch. This makes the setting quietly Gothic, but never sensational. The house appears haunted less by supernatural beings than by ancestry, bereavement and the elaborate maintenance of an illusion.

Tone shifts with unusual delicacy. The opening descent and ruined estate carry melancholy and foreboding, while Wilhelmina’s improvised performance introduces whimsical, almost nonsensical comedy. Her speech mixes Shakespeare, nursery rhyme, ballad, heraldic terminology and absurd narrative invention, immediately establishing her as imaginative, precocious and accustomed to turning painful realities into stories. The two old Admirals are treated with affectionate irony: Peter’s blindness is both literal and symbolic, while Melchior’s endless accounts and bewildered repetitions are gently comic. Yet the humour never becomes cruel. It softens the pathos and preserves the dignity of characters whose eccentricity arises from loyalty and grief.

Dialogue is socially formal and character-revealing. Courteous phrases, ceremonious introductions and evasive answers reflect the household’s old-fashioned codes. Wilhelmina’s dialogue moves between childish play, aristocratic command and mature protectiveness, embodying the story’s central ambiguity about her age. Peter speaks in genealogical and naval formulas, interpreting the present through the prestige of the family past. Melchior’s vague repetitions expose his discomfort with the deception, while the narrator’s restrained replies show tact and increasing complicity. Much of the emotional content remains unspoken, and pauses, hesitations, looks and changes of subject carry as much meaning as explicit declarations.

Structurally, the story is built as a gradual revelation. A chance injury motivates entry into an isolated world; the estate’s visible decay prepares for the concealed family tragedy; hints concerning Fritz accumulate until the church monument supplies the decisive disclosure. The narrative then transforms from a mystery about forged letters into an account of compassionate authorship. The narrator’s vocation as a novelist becomes integral to the plot: storytelling is presented not as fraud for personal gain but as a form of mercy capable of sheltering the vulnerable. The compact retrospective ending extends the episode over years and gives the monthly impersonation of Fritz the quality of a private ritual.

The closing butterfly image gathers the story’s modes into a single lyrical symbol. The red admirals first appear as vivid visual details around Wilhelmina; their name echoes the family’s naval ancestry; and the final folk belief that butterflies contain departed souls gives them a faint supernatural resonance. The ending remains suggestive rather than conclusive. Its punning association between admirals and “red admirals,” together with the narrator’s hope of returning, balances wistfulness with warmth. Overall, the style is ornate yet controlled, humorous without flippancy, and elegiac without overt sentimentality, using cultivated narration, material detail and understated revelation to explore decay, loyalty, imaginative consolation and the humane uses of fiction.

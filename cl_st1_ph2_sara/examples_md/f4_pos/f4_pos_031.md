## How the Count Hurt His Hand

Och hon! and is it the little Frinch count’s hand ye’re axing about—the same hand that’s tied up this blessed minute in a white silk handkerchief, wid a blue ribbon round it, as if it was a sugar-plum intended for a christening? Thin sit down, mavourneen, and I’ll give ye the whole transcaction, plain, straight, and circumstantial; for no man can relate an affair of honour and tinderness like Sir Pathrick O’Grandison, Barronitt, of Ballybrogan Castle, County Clare—which is mesilf, ye’ll understand, and no common bog-throthing spalpeen, though there was a time, before me title came upon me, when I had no more notion of being a barronitt than a pig has of playing the piano-forty.

The title came to me by family descent, sideways, through me mother’s aunt’s husband’s grandfather, who married a lady related to somebody at Coort; and if that isn’t good blood, show me better. There may be them that says I bought the title from a lawyer in Dublin for seventeen pounds, two wigs, and a grey mare wid a cough; but envy is a green-eyed vegetable, as the poet says, and I scorn the imputation.

Well, no sooner did I become Sir Pathrick O’Grandison, Barronitt, than I perceived an alteration in all the ladies. Before that, they’d say, “Pathrick, take yer muddy boots off the chair,” or, “Pathrick, keep away from the jam-closet”; but afther me elevation they said the very same things wid a respectfulness in their looks that was beautiful to contemplate. I got a sky-blue coat wid brass buttons, a yellow waistcoat embroidered in roses, two collars that near cut me ears off, and a pair of boots so tight that I walked like a gintaal dancing-master suffering from repentance. Thus equipped, I was irresistible, and I knew it.

Now, next door to me ancestral property—which is a handsome house whenever ye look at it from behind the hill where the broken windows can’t be seen—there lived the Widow Tracle. Mrs. Tracle was her name, and a mighty agreeable widow she was: black eyes, red cheeks, white hands, and a fortune that was reported to be twenty thousand pounds, though I’d have loved her just the same if it was nineteen. Her husband, poor Tracle, had been dead two years, and a more fortunate circumstance for him I couldn’t desire, seeing that otherwise me courtship might have appeared indelicate.

The first time she beheld me in me barronitt’s uniform she turned her head away and shut the window.

“Ha!” says I to mesilf. “She’s struck. The sight of so much nobility is too strong for the tender craythur.”

The second time she crossed the road to avoid me.

“Ha!” says I. “She fears her own emotions.”

The third time she ordered her maid to say she wasn’t at home, though I saw her peeping from behind the curtain.

“Ha!” says I. “She’s dying for me entirely, but female purliteness forbids her to confess it.”

For, belave me, my jewel, there’s nothing so easy as understanding a woman, provided ye never listen to what she says and always interpret what she does in the manner most favourable to yersilf.

But there was a little obstacle—five feet of obstacle in shoes wid heels—and his name was Count Alphonse de Poppinjay. He was a Frinchman, though not to blame for it, being born so; a small, thin, perfumed article, wid a black moustache twisted up at both ends like the handles of a soup-tureen. His coat fitted him as if it had been painted on, his gloves were lemon-coloured, and he carried a cane no thicker than a knitting-needle. When he bowed, his body doubled up like a pocket rule; and when he spoke, he said, “Pully wou, woully wou, pardong, mong noble,” which, for all I knew, might be the Frinch for potatoes.

This Count Alphonse had the impudence to admire Mrs. Tracle. He walked under her windows; he sent her flowers; he kissed his fingers to her little dog; and once I caught him writing poetry on a pink card. Poetry, indeed! I could have written twice as much if I’d known how to spell it.

But the little reptile had one advantage: he’d been introduced to the widow, while I, Sir Pathrick O’Grandison, Barronitt, had not. Accordingly I treated him to dinner—four courses, counting the cheese twice—and afther the third bottle I put me arm round him in the most aristocratical manner.

“Count,” says I, “ye adore the Widow Tracle.”

He laid his hand upon his waistcoat. “Ah! madame ees an angèle!”

“Never mind what she is,” says I. “Ye adore her.”

“Je swee—pully wou—brûlé d’amour!”

“And very likely ye deserve it,” says I. “But ye’re a gintleman, and I’m another. Introduce me.”

At that he jumped from his chair. “Introduce! Sir Patrique—to Madame Tracle? Jamais! Nevvaire! You ees too magnifique. She see you—she love you—Alphonse ees destroy!”

There, ye percave, he knew me power. Still, by representing that I cared nothing for the widow and only wished to discuss agriculture wid her, I soothed his fears. I also held his hat out the window until he consented. So it was settled that we should call the following Tuesday at three.

On Tuesday I dressed mesilf wid such splendour that the looking-glass cracked from pure admiration. Me coat was green, me waistcoat crimson, me breeches lavender, and me neckcloth had enough linen in it to sail a small boat. I put a diamond pin in the cloth, though it was only glass on weekdays, and rubbed me hair wid bear’s grease till a fly that landed upon it skated from one ear to the other.

The Count arrived in pale blue, smelling of violets and vanity.

“Sir Patrique,” says he, staring at me, “you ees—how say?—ver’ visible.”

“Count,” says I, “that’s the object of dress.”

And away we went.

Mrs. Tracle received us in her drawing-room. It was a beautiful apartment, full of chairs nobody could sit upon, tables nobody could put anything upon, and little china shepherdesses that looked as if they’d never seen a sheep. In the middle stood a long sofa covered in yellow satin. The widow herself sat near the fire, dressed in mourning, though there was a bit of pink ribbon at her throat which, ye may swear, she had put on expressly for Sir Pathrick O’Grandison, Barronitt.

When she saw the Count she smiled.

When she saw me she stopped smiling.

There’s love for ye! Her feelings were too deep for merriment.

“Madame,” says the Count, bending himself till his nose nearly touched the carpet, “permittez-moi présentay le grand, le terrible, le charmant Sir Patrique O’Grandisong.”

I stepped forward, caught me spur in the rug, and made an obaysance so profound that me forehead struck a china shepherdess off the table. I caught the little figure before it reached the floor, however, and put it into me pocket wid perfect composure.

“Madam,” says I, “Sir Pathrick O’Grandison, Barronitt, places his title, his sword, his estates, his education, and every drop of his ancient blood at yer feet.”

“I hope you will place yourself on a chair, Sir Patrick,” says she.

A modest woman! She couldn’t accept everything at once.

The Count flew to her right side. I flew to her left. He reached the sofa first, but I sat down first, partly upon the cushion and partly upon his coat-tail, which detained him very prettily until he gave it a tug. Then there we were, the widow in the middle, the Count on one side, and me on the other, like a rose between a pair of thorns—though I’ll lave ye to judge which was the rose.

The Count began: “Madame, la weather ees charmante.”

“It has been raining for a week,” says she.

“Ah, oui! Frinch rain—très élégant.”

“Madam,” says I, “we have rain in Ireland that would drown this poor foreign drizzle in five minutes. I remember a shower in Ballybrogan that filled seven wells, floated Father O’Murtagh out of his chapel, and washed a cow through the second-storey window of a mill.”

“Indeed?” says she.

She looked toward the bell-rope, overcome wid astonishment at me conversational powers.

Thin the Count spoke of music. “Madame play ze harpicord?”

“A little.”

“Sir Patrique,” says he, sneering, “perhaps play some instrument?”

“Every instrument,” says I.

This was near enough to truth, for I had once beaten a drum and frequently rung a dinner-bell.

“The flute?” asks the widow.

“Like an angel.”

“The violin?”

“Like two angels.”

“The pianoforte?”

“Like the whole heavenly choir dancing a jig upon a tin roof.”

Mrs. Tracle covered her mouth. She was struggling to conceal her passion; but the Count, ignorant savage that he was, thought she was laughing.

While we discoursed, I noticed that the widow’s left arm rested along the back of the sofa, her hand disappearing behind a large cushion. Here was me opportunity. I slipped me right hand behind her as delicately as a barronitt taking a pudding from a pantry, reached past the cushion, and found a hand waiting there.

Och, the thrill of it! A small hand it seemed—though tightly gloved—and trembling like a rabbit in a saucepan. I gave it the gintlest squeeze in the world.

It squeezed me back.

“Ha!” thinks I. “She confesses all. ‘Pathrick,’ says that squeeze, ‘I have fought against yer beauty, yer rank, and yer lavender breeches, but nature is victorious.’”

I gave it two squeezes, signifying, “Darling, hope on.”

It answered wid three, signifying, beyond contradiction, “I am yours till death, and perhaps longer if the property is settled properly.”

Meanwhile the Count was talking at a terrible rate.

“Madame,” says he, “mon cœur ees papillon—fly, fly, brûlé in your eye—pish, pash, amour, toujours, pom de terre!”

Mrs. Tracle stared at him.

“Does your friend often become ill in company, Sir Patrick?” says she.

“Constantly, madam,” says I. “It’s the Frinch constitution. They live upon frogs, and the frogs disagree wid them.”

The concealed hand squeezed mine sharply.

There was jealousy! She feared I might pity him.

“Do not be alarmed, me adored one,” I whispered, leaning toward her. “The creature is nothing to me.”

“I beg your pardon?” says Mrs. Tracle.

“I was merely observing the curtain,” says I, for I’m ready-witted beyond mortal men.

The Count had now put one arm along the back of the sofa too. His face grew red, his eyes shone, and he smiled at nothing. The poor fool was undoubtedly imagining that the widow favoured him, though what reason he had, except vanity and complete stupidity, I cannot tell ye.

I tightened me grasp.

The hand tightened too.

The Count gave a little squeak.

“What ails ye?” says I.

“Nothing, monsieur,” says he, smiling through his teeth. “I am—ravished.”

“Ye look pinched,” says I.

“Love, monsieur! Love make ze douleur sweet.”

At that, the hand squeezed me till me knuckles grated.

“Arrah, my darling,” thinks I, “what volcanic affection!” and I returned the pressure wid interest, to show her that Irish love was no weak foreign beverage but a full bucket of boiling sentiment.

The Count’s eyes watered.

“Madame,” says he faintly, “your beauté ees crush—crush—crush my existence.”

“Then perhaps you had better move,” says she.

But he remained where he was, for the hand I held appeared to tug desperately in his direction. Naturally I supposed the widow feared he would depart before witnessing me triumph.

Now I began telling her of me estates.

“There are nine hundred acres,” says I, “not including the parts under water; forty-six tenants, all devoted to me except those that have absconded; twelve horses, if ye count the three donkeys and a mule; and a castle built in the reign of Brian Boru by the finest architect that ever forgot to put in a staircase.”

“How interesting,” says she, rising a little.

But the hand behind the sofa clung tighter than ever.

She wished to escape the room, ye percave, yet her secret heart couldn’t release me.

“Stay!” I murmured. “Fate commands it.”

“Sir Patrick,” says she, “are you speaking to me?”

“To yer soul, madam.”

“I should prefer you to speak to someone else’s.”

The Count laughed—a thin, insulting, Frinch cackle.

Blood and thunder! Could I, Sir Pathrick O’Grandison, Barronitt, endure to be mocked by a creature whose legs resembled two quill pens? Never. Yet I maintained me purliteness for nearly seven seconds.

“Count,” says I, “ye’re laughing.”

“Mais non.”

“Ye are.”

“Non.”

“Then yer face is doing it without permission, and I advise ye to discipline it.”

Mrs. Tracle stood up suddenly. “Gentlemen, this conversation is intolerable.”

The hand still remained locked in mine behind the sofa. Ah, thinks I, though modesty compels her to rise, devotion leaves her hand as a hostage!

The Count stood also—or attempted to—but something held him bent sideways. “Madame, do not partez! Sir Patrique ees savage—barbare—Irish hippopotam!”

“Hippopotam, is it?” I roared. “Ye scented monkey! Ye pocket-sized dancing-master! Ye boiled frog in a blue coat! If the lady wasn’t present, I’d fold ye into yer own hat and post ye to Paris!”

Mrs. Tracle gave one look at me, one at the Count, and one toward the door.

“James!” she cried, and swept from the room.

“Follow her if ye dare!” says I, crushing the beloved hand, which now struggled wid extraordinary violence. “She is mine!”

“Lâchez! Release! Murder!” screamed the Count.

“Who’s murdering ye?”

“You! My hand!”

“Yer hand?”

I looked behind the sofa.

Och, murther alive!

There was me noble right fist wrapped round the Count’s miserable yellow glove, while his fingers were twisted through mine like a bundle of frightened sausages. All that time—every squeeze, every tender answer, every declaration behind the cushion—it was the little Frinchman and mesilf courting one another hand to hand!

For one moment we stared. Then he shrieked. I roared. He pulled. I held. The sofa tipped backward, the china shepherdess flew from me pocket, and in rushed James the footman, a man built like a wardrobe.

“Out!” says he.

“Put that foreigner out,” says I. “I’m a barronitt.”

“Both of you,” says he.

He took the Count by the collar and me by the waistband, and the next thing we knew we were travelling down the stairs in company—bump, bounce, wallop—like two sacks of potatoes escaping from a cart. We reached the hall, crossed it without touching the floor more than was necessary, and were deposited on the street, where the Count still begged me to release his hand.

I did release it, of course. No gintleman could do otherwise. But before releasing it I gave one final affectionate squeeze—jist to remind him never again to call Sir Pathrick O’Grandison, Barronitt, a hippopotam.

So that, mavourneen, is why Count Alphonse wears his hand in a sling. He says the bones are crushed to raspberry jam; but Frinchmen exaggerate. And as for the Widow Tracle, she has forbidden me the house—which proves, beyond the least particle of doubt, that she loves me desperately and cannot trust herself in me presence at all at all.

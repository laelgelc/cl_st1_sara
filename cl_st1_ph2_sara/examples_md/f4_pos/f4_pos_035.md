Well, sir, we’re ’Piscopals now—all exceptin’ the brindle cow, an’ I ain’t prepared to say what she is, though she has had advantages enough to make her anything she’s a mind to be.

Yes, sir, me an’ Marthy both. Joined the church together, the same day, under circumstances thet I reckon the bishop himself would allow was uncommon, though lawful in the main. Sonny joined too, of co’se. Fact is, he was the occasion of it. But he joined at a distance, an’ under protest, an’ with conditions attached, like he does most everything.

Sonny’ll be six year old come cane-grindin’ time, an’ he’s got views. I don’t say they’re always correct views, nor yet suitable to his age, but they are fixed. A mule’s opinion is open to argument alongside o’ Sonny’s. His mother says that shows strength of character, an’ I reckon it does, though sometimes I’ve thought a little weakness, distributed judicious, would make family government easier.

No, sir, we never did aim to raise him without religion. We wanted him to have all the religion suitable to his constitution, only we differed some about what sort. Marthy’s people was Methodists, mostly, except Uncle Wash, which took up with the Hard-shell Baptists after a spell of rheumatism an’ held out faithful till he died. My folks was divided. Father leaned Presbyterian because he liked a sermon that proved things, an’ mother went where the singin’ was best. I’d always held that a man could get to heaven by any respectable road if he traveled honest, an’ so when Sonny come I says to Marthy:

“Let the child choose for hisself.”

She looked at him layin’ there in the cradle, not more’n a week old an’ with no more expression than a peeled apple, an’ she says:

“Deuteronomy, he ain’t competent.”

“Not now,” says I. “But when he gets old enough to show a preference.”

Well, sir, he showed preferences early. He preferred to sleep daytime an’ discuss matters at night. He preferred the cream pitcher to his tin cup. He preferred my razor strop for hitchin’ the cat to the churn. But in religion he was slower. We didn’t press him. I don’t believe in pressin’ a child’s conscience, especially when pressin’ any other part of him is liable to bring on a fit of kickin’.

So we give him opportunities. Every Sunday for nigh on a year we carried him somewheres different. He went to the Methodist meetin’, an’ liked the hymns, but when Brother Bledsoe slapped the pulpit an’ hollered sudden, Sonny ducked under the bench an’ bit Marthy on the ankle, supposin’, as he said afterward, that the preacher was shootin’. We took him to the Baptists, an’ he was pleased with the immersion till he learned it was intended for people as well as preachers. Then he ’lowed they could do it to Brother Hanks as frequent as they wanted, but they needn’t count on him.

At the Presbyterian church he admired the carpet an’ the minister’s spectacles, but he said the sermon was too long between drinks. That was because we’d promised him a dipper at the spring afterward. He behaved scandalous at the Cumberland meetin’ because a June bug got into the window, an’ he considered its struggles more doctrinal than the discourse. Still, we never judged the denomination by that. A church can’t be held responsible for what crawls in through its windows.

Then one Sunday Mis’ Penelope Carter invited us to Saint Luke’s, the little ’Piscopal church over beyond Turkey Creek. It’s made out o’ stone, with colored glass an’ a bell no bigger’n a wash kettle, but powerful sweet-toned. The rector wore a white garment, an’ there was risin’ an’ settin’ down, an’ speakin’ together, an’ answers at proper places. Sonny took to it from the first minute.

Children like to know what’s comin’ next, sir, particularly if they expect to oppose it.

He watched Reverend Ambrose Vale walk in behind the choir boys, an’ he whispered, loud enough for the front half of the congregation to hear:

“Is thet man goin’ to sleep in his gown?”

Marthy hushed him, but the rector heard. He never changed countenance. That impressed Sonny. Most grown people change countenance when Sonny speaks in public.

Then the organ started, an’ he set up straight. When the folks knelt, he knelt. When they stood, he stood on the cushion so he could stand higher. When they answered, he answered too, though not always the same words. An’ when the rector come down near the chancel rail, Sonny says:

“I choose this here kind.”

There wasn’t no uncertainty about it. Coming home, he said he liked the windows, the little bell, the red cushions, the singin’ boys an’ the rector’s nightshirt, an’ he intended to be a ’Piscopal when he got big enough to own boots.

Marthy says, “You can belong before boots.”

He studied over that, an’ then says, “Do they cut you?”

“No,” says she.

“Do they stick you?”

“No.”

“Do they pull a tooth?”

“No, honey.”

“Then I’ll belong now.”

That looked settled. Reverend Vale come to see us Tuesday evenin’, bringin’ a book an’ a manner with him. He was a tall, mild-spoken man, with hands that looked like they had never shelled corn, though I found out later they was capable hands in an emergency. He explained baptism, sponsors, vows, regeneration an’ spiritual membership, an’ I listened careful. Marthy listened too. Sonny set under the table makin’ a corral out o’ the rector’s coat tails.

When the rector says “baptismal water,” Sonny come out.

“What water?” says he.

“The water used in holy baptism,” says Reverend Vale.

“What do you do with it?”

“I place it upon the forehead.”

“With a needle?”

“No, my son.”

“Show me the needle.”

“There is no needle.”

Sonny looked at me, an’ he looked at Marthy, an’ his face shut up like a morning-glory after sundown.

“I ain’t goin’ to be vaccinated,” says he.

Well, sir, there was the trouble. He had got baptism an’ vaccination twisted together in his mind, an’ all the explanations in the county couldn’t untwist ’em.

You see, he’d had some experience with vaccination—or, more exact, he’d had experience avoidin’ it. There had been smallpox down at Briar Junction the winter before, an’ Doctor Peavy said every livin’ soul on our place ought to be vaccinated. He come out with his little leather case an’ a piece of ivory, an’ Sonny seen him scrape Marthy’s arm.

“What you doin’ to Ma?” says Sonny.

“Protectin’ her,” says the doctor.

“Does protection hurt?”

“Just a little.”

“Then don’t protect me,” says Sonny, an’ went through the pantry window without openin’ it.

He run like zig-zag lightnin’. By the time we got around the house, he was in the smokehouse loft, behind two hams an’ a sack of dried apples. Doctor Peavy coaxed. Marthy coaxed. I commanded. Sonny said no to each one in a separate tone, suitable to our rank.

Finally the doctor ’lowed maybe it would encourage him to see the rest of us done. So I rolled up my sleeve. I’d been vaccinated twice already, but there are moments when a father’s arm don’t rightly belong to him. The doctor scraped me, put on the virus an’ tied a rag over it.

Sonny peeped down.

“Did it hurt?”

“Not a mite,” says I, though Doctor Peavy had dug like he was plantin’ beans.

“Do it again,” says Sonny.

The doctor done the other arm.

“Now come down,” says I.

“Do Ma again.”

Marthy never flinched. She held out her left arm, though her right one was still red.

“Now?” says I.

“Do Aunt Dicey.”

Aunt Dicey wasn’t our aunt by blood, but she cooked for us an’ claimed kin whenever there was work to be divided. She was out by the chicken yard, an’ when we explained, she says she’d sooner face the smallpox itself than Doctor Peavy with that scraper. But she was fond of Sonny, an’ after some discussion about wages, providence an’ whether vaccination was mentioned in Scripture, she submitted.

Sonny watched from the loft.

“Do it take?” says he.

“Not yet,” says the doctor. “It takes time.”

Thet answer was unfortunate. It give Sonny room for policy.

“Then wait till it takes,” says he.

We couldn’t stay under the smokehouse ladder a week, so we tried further persuasion. We vaccinated Amos, the hired boy, though he claimed he’d had it in Alabama. We vaccinated Lemuel, which happened to be bringin’ meal from the mill an’ had no connection with the household exceptin’ friendship. We even sent for Mis’ Carter, because Sonny trusted her more than most adults, but he says he trusted her too much to have her cut.

By sundown Doctor Peavy was tired an’ irritable. He said he’d never met such a child. I told him Sonny was high-strung. The doctor said so was a fiddle string, but folks didn’t let it run the family.

Then Sonny called down, “Vaccinate Brindle.”

We looked at the cow.

Doctor Peavy says, “This has gone far enough.”

But Marthy’s lower lip begun to tremble. She says, “Doctor, if it would reconcile him—”

So he made a little scratch on Brindle. She switched her tail, stepped on his foot an’ upset the milk pail. That pleased Sonny, but it did not reconcile him.

“Now the dog,” says he.

Old Pickles was vaccinated behind the shoulder, though he was immune to nearly everything except fleas an’ thunder. Then the gray cat was attempted, but she objected with such decision that Doctor Peavy withdrew professionally. We dabbed a little medicine on the red rooster, not enough to damage him, an’ Sonny demanded the mule.

There I stood firm.

“No, sir,” says I. “Solomon is valuable, an’ he won’t understand.”

“I don’t understand,” says Sonny.

Thet struck Marthy deep, an’ I felt it myself. A child sometimes puts a case in a way that leaves a parent without a leg, morally speakin’, though he may have two good ones physically.

Still, I refused to vaccinate Solomon. He had kicked a trace chain through the barn wall the week before, an’ there is such a thing as carryin’ parental sacrifice too far.

Night come on. Sonny ate bread an’ jam lowered to him in a basket, an’ slept in the loft. Next mornin’ Doctor Peavy had to go tend a woman with twins, so the vaccination was postponed. Two days later Sonny come down of his own accord, but whenever he seen the doctor’s buggy after that he vanished. None of our vaccinations took, exceptin’ mine, which took in both arms till I couldn’t put on my coat, an’ Aunt Dicey’s, which she said was judgment on her for meddlin’ with white folks’ foolishness.

So when Reverend Vale mentioned baptismal water, Sonny recollected every scratch.

The rector tried theology. He said baptism was an outward and visible sign of an inward and spiritual grace. Sonny says he didn’t want no outward sign. Reverend Vale says little children was received into Christ’s flock. Sonny says flocks was sheep an’ he was a boy. The rector says no instrument was used. Sonny asked what he carried in his coat pocket. It turned out to be a fountain pen, but Sonny considered the shape suspicious.

At last Reverend Vale says there was no hurry, as the child was healthy an’ approaching the age when he could answer more intelligently for himself.

I didn’t favor that final phrase, but we let it pass.

For three months Sonny attended Saint Luke’s unbaptized. He learned the responses, the hymns an’ which choir boy kept peppermint in his sleeve. He loved the service. He called Reverend Vale “my preacher,” an’ would follow him after church askin’ questions about angels, collection plates an’ why the Lord let wasps get made. But the minute baptism was mentioned, he put both hands over his sleeves an’ backed away.

Now, I set out to tell you how we all become ’Piscopals, an’ here I’ve got tangled in Doctor Peavy’s business. But you need to know the vaccination to understand the baptism. One error grows out of another, sir, same as squash vines crossin’ a path.

It happened on a Thursday in August, hot an’ still. Sonny had been playin’ sawmill under the back porch with a broken shingle. Nobody knows why a child can’t use a whole shingle. He always chooses one with splinters.

Near dinner he come limpin’ in, sayin’ a piece of the porch had bit him. Marthy set him on the table an’ found a long splinter drove deep into the ball of his foot. He wouldn’t let her touch it. He wouldn’t let me. He said the splinter belonged there an’ he aimed to keep it.

We tried reason, candy an’ authority. Reason failed first, authority next, an’ candy made him close his toes tighter. Finally Aunt Dicey come up behind him with the dinner bell an’ rang it sudden over his head. While he jumped, Marthy pulled the splinter.

It was a wicked-looking piece, dark at one end. We washed the foot, soaked it, put on turpentine, then took the turpentine off because Mis’ Carter said that drove poison inward. We used plantain leaves, then bacon rind, then a poultice Aunt Dicey made without revealin’ the ingredients. By bedtime the place was red. Next mornin’ it was swelled.

Marthy looked at it an’ went pale.

“Deuteronomy,” says she, “lockjaw.”

Now I don’t believe in borrowin’ trouble. Never did. Trouble generally arrives without a loan. But I’d heard of a boy over in Clayton County that stepped on a rusty nail, locked his jaws by sunset an’ died facin’ east. Why facin’ east I don’t know, unless the bed stood that way, but the detail had remained in my mind.

I tried to open Sonny’s mouth.

“What you doin’, Pa?” says he.

“Lookin’ at your tongue.”

“What for?”

“To see if it’s loose.”

He stuck it out an’ wagged it.

That reassured me for maybe a minute. Then Marthy says lockjaw can come sudden, an’ what if our child should pass away unchristened?

Sir, I felt my backbone go soft like a feather bolster in rain.

We sent Amos for Doctor Peavy, but the doctor was twenty miles off tendin’ a threshin’-machine accident. Then Marthy says, “Fetch Reverend Vale.”

I saddled Solomon. He seemed to understand it was an emergency, because he only tried to bite me once. I rode the four miles to the rectory at a pace I won’t encourage in ordinary travel, found Reverend Vale trimmin’ a rosebush, an’ told him Sonny was dyin’ of lockjaw, unbaptized, an’ likely to resist.

He dropped his shears.

“Has the child developed spasms?”

“No, sir.”

“Is the jaw rigid?”

“No, sir, but Marthy says it may get that way any minute.”

He looked at me. “What are the physician’s instructions?”

“The physician ain’t there.”

“What are the symptoms?”

“A sore foot an’ a contrary disposition.”

“The latter appears chronic,” says he, but he got his hat.

We come back in his buggy. Before we reached the gate, Marthy run out wringin’ her hands.

“He’s gone!”

Reverend Vale turned white.

“Gone where?”

“Up the bean arbor!”

Sure enough, there he was.

The arbor stood beyond the kitchen, where I’d trained butter beans over a framework of pine poles an’ laths. It was never intended to bear a child. Fact is, it was hardly equal to the beans. But Sonny had climbed the side, crawled over the vines an’ settled hisself on the very top, where two crosspieces met. His sore foot was wrapped in a red flannel rag, an’ he held the watering gourd in one hand for defense.

The poles bowed every time he breathed.

“Come down, son,” says I, gentle.

“No.”

“Your preacher has come to see you.”

“He can see me from there.”

Reverend Vale stepped forward. “My dear little boy—”

“Take that pen out o’ your pocket.”

The rector took it out.

“Lay it on the porch.”

He obeyed.

“Show both hands.”

He showed ’em.

Then he began explainin’ the necessity an’ comfort of baptism in cases of grave illness. He did it beautiful, with words about covenant mercies, Christian assurance an’ the tender bosom of the church. Sonny listened till a bean beetle crawled across his knee; then he flicked it down at us.

“I ain’t sick,” says he.

“Your foot is inflamed,” says the rector.

“Feet can’t be baptized.”

“We baptize the person.”

“Then I’m busy.”

Marthy begun cryin’. Sonny couldn’t bear to see her cry, but he generally aimed to cure it by tellin’ her not to, which has limited effect.

“Don’t cry, Ma,” he says. “I’ll get christened when I’m seven.”

“You may not live till seven!” she wailed.

He considered that. “Then I won’t need it long.”

Reverend Vale coughed into his hand. I think he had swallowed a laugh by mistake, though the occasion was solemn.

We fetched the ladder, but the arbor shook when I leaned it there, an’ Sonny moved toward the weakest corner.

“If you climb, I’ll jump.”

“Don’t you dare!” says Marthy.

“I ain’t darin’. I’m promisin’.”

So we took the ladder away.

The rector suggested we distract him while Amos crept up from the far side. Sonny watched Amos’s bare feet under the vines an’ poured the watering gourd on his head. Amos come down quicker than he went up.

Then Marthy says, “Honey, what if Papa an’ me was baptized first?”

The words struck him. He looked at us through the leaves.

“You ain’t ’Piscopals,” says he.

“We can become so,” says Marthy.

I confess, sir, I hadn’t expected the day’s program to include changing my denomination. But when your only child is perched over probable death with a swollen foot, denominational boundaries seem less like stone walls an’ more like chalk marks after rain.

Reverend Vale hesitated. “There would ordinarily be instruction.”

“We’ve been attendin’,” says Marthy.

“An examination as to intention.”

“We intend for Sonny to come down,” says I.

“I mean spiritual intention, Mr. Jones.”

“Well, sir, I intend honest. I favor your church, an’ I don’t see why the Lord would object to receivin’ us somewhat ahead of schedule.”

The rector looked at the arbor, which give a long creak.

“I believe,” says he, “the circumstances justify pastoral latitude.”

Sonny leaned over. “What’s latitude?”

“Room,” says I. “It means he’s got room to christen us.”

“Do it,” says Sonny.

So Reverend Vale sent Amos into the house for a basin of clean water. Aunt Dicey brought out the blue china washbowl Marthy’s mother left her. The rector said a smaller vessel would do, but Marthy ’lowed if we was changing our eternal condition she wanted something without a crack in it.

We stood by the porch steps. Reverend Vale put on his white surplice, which he’d brought folded in a satchel. When it fluttered out, the mule snorted an’ the red rooster challenged it from under the fig bush.

The rector instructed us in what to answer. We answered. He asked if we renounced the world, the flesh an’ the devil. I said I’d do my best, though I’d have to know exactly how much of the world was meant, on account of the farm. Marthy nudged me, an’ Reverend Vale simplified the question.

Then he baptized her, proper an’ dignified.

Sonny watched close.

“Did it hurt, Ma?”

“No, darlin’.”

“Did it take?”

“The rector says it did.”

Then my turn come. I bowed my head, an’ Reverend Vale put water on it in the name of the Father, Son an’ Holy Ghost. Some run down behind my ear. It was cool an’ not unpleasant.

I looked up at Sonny. “There, son. You see?”

He says, “Do it again.”

Reverend Vale says, “Baptism is not repeated.”

“Do Pa’s other side.”

“It is not a vaccination,” says the rector, with more feeling than theology generally requires.

Sonny frowned. “Do Aunt Dicey.”

Aunt Dicey, who had been standin’ in the kitchen door with her arms folded, stepped backward.

“No, indeed,” says she. “I been baptized once in Cedar Creek, an’ they held me under sufficient.”

Sonny says, “Then do Amos.”

Amos rubbed the water out of his hair from the gourd incident. “I got religion at Mount Zion,” says he.

“Do Mis’ Carter.”

“She ain’t here,” says Marthy.

“Send for her.”

Reverend Vale lifted his face toward the sky, perhaps askin’ patience. Clouds had begun gatherin’ over the west field, purple underneath, but nobody had noticed till then.

“My child,” he says, “holy baptism is not an exhibition to be prolonged for your entertainment.”

Sonny hugged the top pole. “I ain’t entertained.”

“You have seen your parents receive the sacrament.”

“Do Solomon.”

“The mule?” says the rector.

“He ain’t had it.”

Reverend Vale looked at me.

I says, “It’s the vaccination pattern. He goes by precedent.”

The rector removed his spectacles an’ wiped ’em. “Animals are not proper subjects of Christian baptism.”

Sonny says, “Then the red rooster.”

“No.”

“Pickles?”

“No.”

“The cat?”

“Certainly not.”

From under the porch the cat gave a sound as if agreeing fully with church law.

Then the first thunder muttered. Marthy looked up.

“Sonny, it’s goin’ to rain! Come down this minute.”

“No.”

A wind moved through the bean leaves. The arbor swayed, an’ Sonny swayed with it, but he had the balance of a squirrel. I stepped close with my arms ready, though if he fell from that corner he’d strike the cucumber frame first.

The rector whispered, “Can we seize the structure and lower it?”

“It’ll fold,” says I.

“Can the vines be cut?”

“He’ll jump.”

“Is there no person he obeys?”

“Marthy, mostly, unless he differs.”

The rain come in big separate drops, each one makin’ a dark spot in the dust. Sonny blinked when one hit his nose. Reverend Vale looked at the sky, then at the washbowl, then at Sonny.

All at once his face changed. He wasn’t pompous any more, nor worried about rules. He looked like a man that had found a gate in a fence.

“Mr. Jones,” says he, “does that porch roof extend sufficiently?”

“For what?”

“For me to stand under.”

He took the book, the little silver cross an’ the bowl onto the porch. Then he called to Sonny:

“My son, will you consent to baptism if no one approaches the arbor?”

Sonny narrowed his eyes. “You stay there?”

“I shall stay here.”

“No needles?”

“No needles.”

“No climbin’?”

“No climbing.”

“An’ I stay here?”

“If you insist.”

Sonny considered the bargain while the rain thickened. “How can you reach me?”

“I need not touch you, provided water is applied and the words are spoken with proper intent.”

Sonny held out one hand. Rain was runnin’ off his fingers now.

“This is water.”

“Yes.”

“Is it proper?”

The rector looked at the black cloud, then upward beyond it.

“It comes from a sufficient authority,” says he.

Well, sir, the rain broke loose. It rattled the porch roof, flattened the dust an’ bowed the bean vines. We all crowded under the eaves except Sonny, who set aloft soaked through, his hair pasted over his forehead an’ his nightshirt-colored blouse clingin’ to him. He looked solemn, too. Whether from religion or chill I couldn’t say.

Reverend Vale opened his book.

“What name is given this child?”

Marthy whispered, “We never settled his church name.”

His everyday name was Sonny. We had called him that from the start, an’ somehow the matter of a formal name had drifted. His birth paper said “Male Jones,” because I’d gone to fetch the registrar an’ got delayed by a washout.

I called, “Sonny, what name do you want?”

He answered prompt: “Deuteronomy Jones.”

I felt proud. A man can’t help it when his son chooses his full name.

Marthy says, “We’ll call him Deuteronomy Jones Junior.”

From the arbor come a sharp voice:

“No! Senior.”

I says, “Son, you can’t be Senior. I’m Senior.”

“I want all your name.”

“Junior would be all of it, with a Junior.”

“I don’t want Junior.”

Reverend Vale says, “The suffix is not essential to the baptismal name.”

Sonny shouted through the rain, “Put Senior!”

The arbor give another creak.

“Put it,” says Marthy.

So Reverend Vale, standing dry on the porch, while heaven supplied the water, raised his hand an’ says in a voice that carried above the storm:

“Deuteronomy Jones Senior, I baptize thee in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Ghost. Amen.”

At that exact instant a stream poured off a bean leaf square onto Sonny’s forehead. I never seen timing more complete. He shut his eyes, spluttered once, an’ wiped his face.

“Is that all?” says he.

“That is all,” says Reverend Vale.

“Did it take?”

“We shall trust so.”

Sonny remained on the arbor.

We thought maybe he’d come down as a natural result of grace, but natural results don’t hurry Sonny. Reverend Vale finished the prayers, packed his surplice an’ book, shook hands with me an’ Marthy, an’ said he would call again when the household was calmer. He advised us to get the boy dry, have the foot examined an’ discourage any further theological negotiations from elevated positions.

When his buggy passed through the gate, Sonny swung one leg over the pole.

“Don’t move!” Marthy cried.

“I’m comin’ down.”

He come down easy as a cat, steppin’ from crosspiece to crosspiece, an’ landed in my arms. Marthy wrapped him in a quilt, wet clothes an’ all, an’ carried him to the kitchen. The swollen foot didn’t prevent him from eatin’ two slices of apple pie, which weakened our belief in lockjaw considerable.

Doctor Peavy arrived near dark, cross an’ muddy. He looked at the foot, said it was a common inflammation, opened the place with a lancet before Sonny understood what was happenin’, an’ found a little piece of splinter still inside.

Sonny hollered, “You stuck me!”

“Correct,” says Doctor Peavy. “An’ if you climb anything tomorrow, I’ll stick you twice.”

Sonny studied him an’ decided he meant it.

After the doctor left, we set by the kitchen stove dryin’ Sonny’s hair. Marthy rubbed it with a towel while I warmed his feet. He was sweeter than usual, thanked her for the pie, called me “dear Papa” without wantin’ anything immediate, an’ even apologized to Amos for pourin’ water on him—though he added that Amos had no business climbin’.

Presently he says, “Pa, next time I get christened, I’ll stand by the bowl.”

I says, “There won’t be no next time.”

He stopped still. “Why?”

“Baptism can’t be repeated.”

“Not ever?”

“No, son. Once is enough.”

He thought a long while, pullin’ a loose thread from the quilt.

Then he looked up, troubled.

“S’pos’in’ mine don’t take?”

Marthy looked at me, an’ I looked at her. We both recollected all them vaccinated arms, Aunt Dicey’s judgment, Brindle kickin’ the doctor, an’ Sonny safe in the smokehouse loft.

But just then he leaned over an’ kissed his mother, of his own accord, an’ offered me the largest half of his last piece of pie.

So I says, “Well, son, judgin’ by symptoms, I reckon it’s commenced to take already.”

An’ Marthy says, “Don’t you doubt it, Deuteronomy.”

Which one she meant, me or him, I don’t know. There’s two Seniors in the family now, accordin’ to church record, though only one of us can write it. But we’ve had peace ever since, exceptin’ Sonny says when the bishop comes he intends to ask whether a person baptized by rain is allowed to carry an umbrella.

I reckon Reverend Vale’ll answer him.

He’s a patient man, sir.

He needs to be.

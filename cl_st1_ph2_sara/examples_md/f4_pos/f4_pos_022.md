# The Keeper of Lost Hours

Every morning at seven, when the bells of Saint Orra struck the hour, Elias Vale opened the narrow blue door beneath the railway bridge and swept yesterday’s time from the floor.

It gathered in the corners like silver dust.

Most people walked past without noticing the door. They hurried toward offices, markets, schools, and factories, carrying umbrellas, briefcases, or half-eaten pieces of toast. Above them, trains shook the iron bridge and sent pigeons flapping into the gray sky. To anyone who happened to glance down, the sign over Elias’s door appeared to read:

**VALE & SON, CLOCK REPAIR**

But there had never been a son, and Elias repaired very few clocks.

Behind the blue door lay a workshop filled with glass jars. Thousands stood on shelves from floor to ceiling, each holding a small quantity of light. Some glowed warm and golden. Some flickered like winter stars. Others were pale as milk.

They were lost hours.

People misplaced time more often than they realized. Five minutes dropped while staring through a rainy window. An afternoon lost to grief. A summer forgotten after childhood. A final conversation delayed until it could never happen. Such hours slipped loose from memory and rolled away, finding their way through cracks in the city until, sooner or later, they appeared as silver dust beneath Elias’s bridge.

His task was to collect them, polish them, and keep them safe.

On each jar he wrote a name, a date, and—if he could discover it—the place where the hour belonged.

**MARA VENN — 14 OCTOBER — THE HOSPITAL GARDEN**

**JONAS PIKE — 3 JUNE — BESIDE THE RIVER**

**UNKNOWN CHILD — MIDSUMMER — A RED KITE**

For thirty-seven years Elias had guarded the lost hours. He had returned some. Many remained unclaimed.

He lived alone in two rooms above the workshop. He ate boiled eggs for breakfast, soup for supper, and one square of dark chocolate before bed. On Sundays he visited the cemetery and sat beside a stone bearing the name of his wife, Clara.

He never spoke to her there. He had said everything he knew how to say before she died.

Or so he told himself.

One November morning, Elias found an entire hour lying in the center of the floor.

Usually lost time arrived in fragments: a few bright grains, a thin thread of light, perhaps a minute beating like a tiny heart. But this was whole. It hovered an inch above the boards, turning slowly, round and luminous. Within it, shadows moved.

Elias shut the door quickly and drew the curtains.

He placed a glass bell over the hour and examined it through a magnifying lens. He saw a long table set for two. Candles. A loaf of bread. A woman in a green dress standing by an open window.

Clara.

Elias dropped the lens.

The hour pulsed beneath the glass.

For several minutes, he could not move. Then he turned away and gripped the edge of his workbench until the room steadied.

It was impossible. The dead did not lose time. Their hours were fixed, sealed by the end of their lives. He had never found a single grain belonging to someone buried.

Yet Clara stood inside that hour exactly as he remembered her at thirty-two: dark hair pinned carelessly, one hand resting on the window frame, a faint line between her eyebrows.

Elias forced himself to look again.

The man seated at the table had his back to him, but Elias recognized his own narrow shoulders. This was their old apartment on Bell Street. The yellow walls. The blue cups. Rain on the window. A white envelope beside the bread.

He knew the date before the label typed itself inside his mind.

The evening Clara left him.

Not the final leaving. That came twenty-one years later, in a hospital room that smelled of soap and lilies.

This was the first.

Elias carried the hour to his strongest cabinet, locked it inside, and put the key in his pocket. Then he swept the floor twice, though there was no dust left to gather.

At ten o’clock, someone knocked.

The sound was so unexpected that Elias stood perfectly still.

People rarely knocked. Those who needed him usually found themselves already inside, confused about how they had entered. The lost were drawn to their time without understanding why.

The knock came again: three sharp taps.

Elias opened the door a hand’s width.

A girl stood outside. She was perhaps fourteen, with brown skin, black curls escaping from a yellow woolen hat, and a schoolbag covered in patches. Rain shone on her coat.

“Are you Mr. Vale?” she asked.

“There is no other Mr. Vale.”

“Do you fix clocks?”

“Occasionally.”

She held up a pocket watch. Its case was brass, scarred and dull.

“It belongs to my grandfather,” she said. “It stopped yesterday.”

“Then take it to Harren’s on King Street.”

“I did. Mr. Harren said there was nothing wrong with it.”

Elias frowned. “A stopped watch has something wrong with it.”

“That’s what I said.”

Against his better judgment, he let her enter.

The girl looked around the workshop, and her eyes widened at the shelves. Most visitors saw only clocks, tools, and boxes. The mind disguised what it was not prepared to understand.

But the girl whispered, “They’re beautiful.”

Elias closed the door.

“What do you see?”

“Jars.”

“What is inside them?”

“Time.”

He took the pocket watch from her.

The hands had stopped at 4:17. When Elias pressed the release, the case sprang open. Instead of gears, he found a small dark hollow that seemed deeper than the watch itself.

“When did your grandfather die?” he asked.

The girl drew a breath. “Yesterday. At 4:17.”

“I’m sorry.”

“He gave me the watch last week. He said I’d know where to take it.”

Elias turned it over. On the back, nearly erased by years of handling, were the initials O.V.

His heart tightened.

“What was your grandfather’s name?”

“Orin Vale.”

The watch slipped from Elias’s fingers and landed on the padded workbench.

The girl watched him carefully. “He said you might do that.”

Elias sat down.

Orin had been his younger brother.

They had not spoken in forty years.

As boys, they had shared everything: a room under the eaves, a red bicycle, a secret language, and the belief that no locked door could remain locked if one had enough patience. Then their father died and left the workshop to Elias. Orin wanted to sell it. Elias refused.

Harsh words became cruel words. Orin left the city. Letters came at first, then stopped. Pride did the quiet work of distance.

Elias stared at the girl.

“You are Orin’s granddaughter?”

“Nina.”

“Why didn’t he come himself?”

“He was sick for a long time.”

“That is not what I meant.”

“I know.” Nina removed her wet hat. “He said you were both stubborn, but you had more practice.”

Despite himself, Elias almost smiled.

Nina pointed toward the pocket watch. “Can you fix it?”

“No.”

“He said you would say that too.”

“Your grandfather seems to have formed many opinions about me.”

“He had forty years.”

Elias picked up the watch again. The dark hollow inside trembled. Something was trapped there, but it was not an hour. It was a path.

“Did Orin tell you what this watch does?”

“He said it goes where regret points.”

“That sounds like him. Dramatic and unhelpful.”

“He also said you’d understand.”

Unfortunately, Elias did.

The watch had belonged to their father. It was the first object Elias had seen hold time. When they were children, he and Orin discovered that if they wound it backward at midnight, it revealed moments people wished to recover.

Their father called it a dangerous toy.

“Regret,” he once told them, “is a compass that always points behind you. Follow it too long and you’ll forget the road ahead.”

After his death, the watch vanished. Elias assumed Orin had taken it.

Now it had returned, empty and stopped at the minute of Orin’s death.

“There should be something inside,” Elias murmured.

“A spring?”

“An hour.”

Nina glanced at the shelves. “His?”

“Perhaps.”

She stepped closer. “Can we find it?”

“No.”

“Why not?”

“Because hours belonging to the dead cannot be found.”

“There are a lot of things people say can’t be found. Mostly they mean they don’t want to look.”

“You sound like him.”

“Good.”

Elias’s hand moved toward the cabinet key in his pocket.

First Clara’s impossible hour. Then Orin’s watch. Two violations of the oldest rule in his life, arriving before noon on the same day.

He disliked coincidences. They were usually patterns wearing masks.

“Come back tomorrow,” he said.

“I’m here now.”

“I have work.”

Nina surveyed the silent room. “It doesn’t look urgent.”

“All time is urgent.”

“Then we should hurry.”

Elias pointed to the door.

She put on her hat but paused before leaving.

“My grandfather’s last word was your name,” she said. “I thought you should know.”

The blue door closed behind her.

For the rest of the day, Elias tried not to think. He cleaned jars, repaired labels, and sorted minutes by date. At sunset he went upstairs, warmed his soup, and did not eat it.

At midnight, he returned to the workshop.

He took Orin’s watch from the bench and Clara’s hour from the cabinet.

The hour glowed more brightly now. Inside it, Clara waited at the window while his younger self sat at the table. That evening had never been lost before. Elias remembered every word.

Clara had received an offer to study music in Vienna for one year. Elias had just inherited the workshop. He could not leave, and she would not stay.

“You care more for other people’s lost time than for ours,” she had told him.

“That isn’t fair.”

“It is exactly fair.”

“If you go, don’t expect me to wait.”

“I’m not asking you to wait. I’m asking you to come.”

He refused.

She left in the rain. One year later she returned. They married and were happy, more often than not, until illness took her.

Elias had always believed the recovered happiness proved he had made the right choice. Yet he also knew Clara had never played the piano professionally again.

He wound the pocket watch backward.

Once.

Twice.

On the third turn, the watch began to tick.

Clara’s hour rose from beneath the glass and stretched into a long ribbon of light. It wound around the workshop, passed through the blue door, and vanished into the night.

Elias grabbed his coat and followed.

Nina was waiting outside.

“I knew you’d do it at midnight,” she said.

“How?”

“My grandfather said that’s when you always did dangerous things.”

Elias had no answer.

The glowing ribbon moved along the pavement. Nina followed it, and Elias followed Nina.

It led them through streets slick with rain, past shuttered shops and empty tram stops. The city changed as they walked. Neon signs became gas lamps. Cars blurred into horse-drawn carriages and back again. Buildings rose, crumbled, and rebuilt themselves in the corners of Elias’s vision.

“Don’t touch anything,” he warned.

“What happens if I do?”

“I don’t know.”

“That means you’ve never tried.”

“It means don’t touch anything.”

The ribbon brought them to Saint Orra Station. The gates were locked, but the pocket watch clicked, and the iron bars opened like folding hands.

Inside, no ordinary trains were running.

A black locomotive waited at the far platform. Its windows shone with the amber light of late afternoon. Above it hung a sign:

**DEPARTED HOURS — NO RETURN TICKET**

Nina climbed aboard before Elias could stop her.

The carriage was filled with passengers who were not truly there. A soldier repeatedly opened a letter. A mother lifted a sleeping baby from a cradle. Two old men played the final move of a chess game. Each scene lasted a few seconds before beginning again.

“These are memories,” Nina said.

“Regrets,” Elias corrected.

“Is there a difference?”

“A memory asks to be carried. A regret asks to be changed.”

They moved through the carriages. With each door, Elias felt the weight of unlived lives pressing around him. Apologies never offered. Voyages never taken. Hands never held.

At the final carriage, they found Orin.

He sat alone beside the window, not as an old man but as he had been at twenty-five: broad-faced, red-haired, impatient. The white scar above his eyebrow came from the day he and Elias crashed their shared bicycle into a stone wall.

Orin looked up.

“You took your time,” he said.

Elias could not breathe.

Nina whispered, “Granddad?”

Orin smiled at her. “Hello, starling.”

She moved toward him, but Elias caught her shoulder.

“He isn’t alive,” he said gently.

“I know.”

Orin’s smile faded. “She knows more than you think.”

“What is this?” Elias asked. “Why is Clara’s hour here?”

“Because it was never Clara’s.”

Orin pointed to the light circling Elias’s wrist.

“It was yours.”

“That hour is not lost. I remember it.”

“You remember what happened. Not what it meant.”

The train began to move.

Outside, the station dissolved into darkness. Scenes appeared in the windows: Elias and Orin as children racing along a canal; Clara at a piano; Orin holding his infant daughter; Nina learning to walk.

Orin leaned back.

“When Father left you the workshop, I thought it meant he loved you more. I wanted to sell it because I couldn’t bear the idea of you becoming him.”

“Our father helped people.”

“Our father hid from them. So did you.”

Elias stiffened.

“You know nothing of my life.”

“I know you missed my wedding.”

“You didn’t invite me.”

“I know you returned every letter I sent after Clara got sick.”

Elias looked away.

“I was angry.”

“So was I.”

The train gathered speed. The scenes outside blurred.

Nina sat opposite her grandfather. “Why did the watch stop?”

“Because I put my last hour inside it,” Orin said. “I meant it for Elias.”

“Hours of the dead cannot be recovered,” Elias said.

“Not from the dead. But I gave mine away while I was still living.”

The darkness beyond the window opened onto a hospital room.

Old Orin lay in bed, thin and pale. Nina sat beside him, gripping the pocket watch. A clock on the wall showed 3:17—one hour before his death.

“He gave you his final hour,” Elias said.

Nina nodded slowly. “He asked me to leave the room. I thought he wanted to sleep.”

“He used the watch to send the hour backward,” said young Orin. “But it caught on your regret, Elias. That evening with Clara was the strongest road leading to you.”

“Why?”

“Because it was the moment you decided staying was always nobler than leaving.”

The words struck harder than accusation.

Elias saw the pattern of his life: the workshop, the bridge, the jars, the cemetery. He had called his stillness loyalty. He had called his fear duty. Even after Clara returned, even after they built a life together, some part of him remained at that table, defending the choice not to follow.

“What did you want me to see?” he asked.

Orin gestured to the hospital scene.

The final hour unfolded.

Elias expected a confession or message. Instead, he watched his brother sleep. Rain tapped the glass. A nurse changed a vase of flowers. Orin woke once and turned toward the empty chair beside him.

That was all.

An hour of waiting.

“You thought I would come,” Elias whispered.

“I hoped.”

“I didn’t know.”

“You knew I was ill.”

Elias lowered his head.

“I’m sorry.”

The train shuddered.

Every passenger in every carriage turned toward him.

An apology to the dead was a dangerous thing. The dead could not accept it, and so the words remained in the world, heavy and unfinished.

But Orin smiled.

“Say it to the living,” he replied.

Cracks of white light spread across the windows.

The train was reaching the end of its track.

“What happens now?” Nina asked.

“The hour returns to the person it was given to,” Orin said.

Elias looked at the pocket watch. “But it was given to me.”

“No,” said Nina.

She understood first.

Orin had not sent his last hour so Elias could keep it. He had sent it so Elias could choose what to do with it.

A whole hour could not restore the dead. It could not change the past. But placed in the hands of the living, it could become sixty unwritten minutes.

A return ticket.

The carriage began to come apart. The chess players vanished. The soldier’s letter scattered into sparks. The sleeping baby’s blanket became light.

Elias grabbed Nina’s hand.

Orin stood by the window, already fading.

“Wait,” Elias said. There were forty years of questions and no time to ask them.

Orin touched the scar above his eyebrow and grinned.

“You were steering the bicycle,” he said.

Then he was gone.

The train disappeared.

Elias and Nina stood in the workshop beneath the railway bridge. Morning sunlight pressed against the curtains. The shelves were quiet.

In Nina’s palm lay the pocket watch.

Its hands showed 3:17.

“Open it,” Elias said.

Inside, the dark hollow had filled with gold.

An untouched hour waited there.

Nina looked at him. “What do we do with it?”

Elias thought of all the ways people tried to use recovered time. They returned to lovers, prevented accidents, changed decisions, and whispered warnings to younger selves. Sometimes they found peace. More often they created new regrets.

Then he remembered Orin’s words.

Say it to the living.

“You keep it,” Elias said.

“For what?”

“For nothing yet.”

He took a blank label from his desk and wrote:

**NINA VALE — ONE HOUR — THE ROAD AHEAD**

He attached it to the watch chain.

Nina read the label twice. “Does this mean I’m your apprentice?”

“It means you possess an hour.”

“The sign says Vale and Son.”

“You are neither.”

“We could repaint it.”

Elias looked around the workshop. For thirty-seven years, he had guarded time as though keeping it safe was the same as using it well. Dust lay thick on the highest shelves. Thousands of hours glimmered in their jars, waiting for people who might never come.

He opened the curtains.

Morning flooded the room.

Under true sunlight, the labels changed. Names Elias had thought permanent faded, and new words appeared. The hours had never belonged only to those who lost them. Some were meant to be given.

**TO SOMEONE WHO NEEDS COURAGE**

**TO SOMEONE WHO HAS FORGOTTEN JOY**

**TO SOMEONE WHO MUST SAY GOODBYE**

Elias began to laugh.

It was a rusty sound, almost a cough. Nina stared, then laughed with him.

Above them, Saint Orra’s bells rang seven.

Elias unlocked the blue door and turned the sign around. For the first time in decades, the lettering showed exactly what was written there:

**VALE & VALE, KEEPERS OF LOST HOURS**

“First lesson,” he said.

Nina removed her coat and hung it beside his.

“About clocks?”

“About sweeping.”

“That’s disappointing.”

“Time often is.”

Together they gathered the silver dust from the corners. Nina worked too quickly and sent bright clouds spinning through the air. Elias began to correct her, then stopped. The workshop filled with glittering minutes, each one catching the sunlight before settling gently into place.

Later that morning, Elias wrote a letter to Orin’s daughter.

After lunch, he visited Clara’s grave. He told her about the train, the watch, and the girl in the yellow hat. He told her she had been right about Vienna. He told her he was sorry.

The dead did not answer.

The autumn wind moved through the cemetery trees, carrying leaves over the stones and down toward the city.

Elias sat quietly until he understood that silence was not the same as emptiness.

Then he stood and walked back beneath the railway bridge, where Nina was waiting with the door open and a newly painted sign above her head.

For once, Elias did not hurry.

For once, he did not remain still.

And the hour ahead of him, bright and unclaimed, began.

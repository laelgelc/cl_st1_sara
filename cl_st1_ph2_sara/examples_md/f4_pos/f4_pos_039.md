### The Sculptor’s Two Visions

There was once a young sculptor named Alfred Vale, who possessed talent enough to make his friends proud of him, ambition enough to make himself unhappy, and good looks enough to cause elderly ladies to prophesy that he would marry badly.

He had won a scholarship and spent three years in Italy, where he studied the antique, quarrelled with modern critics, admired the sunlight upon old marble, and learned to say “form” in a tone which made it signify nearly everything under heaven. When at last he returned, the provincial newspaper announced that “our distinguished young townsman, whose genius has received recognition in the classic land of Art,” had once more taken up residence among his fellow-citizens.

This was not precisely true, since Alfred had been born twenty miles away; but a newspaper, like an affectionate aunt, may be allowed to adopt any promising young man in the neighbourhood.

Before a month had passed, he was invited to a party at the house of Councillor Brandt. It was there that he first saw Kaela.

She stood near a window, dressed in pale blue, with one slender hand resting upon the back of a chair. Her hair was golden, her eyes were of that clear and changeable blue which painters attempt and generally spoil, and her features had the faultless repose of a Greek head. She spoke to no one. Now there are silences which proceed from wisdom, silences which proceed from sorrow, and silences which proceed simply from having nothing to say; but the last are often mistaken for the first two, particularly when the silent person is beautiful.

Alfred looked at Kaela and forgot the rest of the room.

“Who is she?” he asked his hostess.

“That is Kaela Morn,” replied Mrs. Brandt. “She lives with her mother. A very quiet girl.”

“Quiet!” said Alfred. “Yes, I can see that. There is contemplation in her face.”

Mrs. Brandt had known Kaela from infancy, and might have corrected him; but at that moment a servant let fall a tray, and truth lost its opportunity, as it so frequently does through some domestic interruption.

Presently Alfred was introduced to Mrs. Morn, a round, restless widow who talked as if silence were an enemy pursuing her.

“So you are the Italian gentleman!” she cried. “Kaela, my dear, here is the Italian gentleman. Not that you are Italian, of course, for Mrs. Brandt says you were born at Millford; but having lived abroad alters people wonderfully. Did you see the great organist in Rome?”

“The great organist?” Alfred repeated.

“Yes, a stone one—very ancient. It stands upright, I believe.”

“Perhaps you mean an obelisk.”

“That is the word. I always confuse those foreign professions. Kaela, did I not say it was something musical?”

Kaela smiled.

It was an exquisite smile, slight and remote, and Alfred instantly decided that it contained indulgence, humour, and filial tenderness. How much there may be in a smile—or how much a hopeful observer may put into it—is a question upon which many marriages have been founded.

Mrs. Morn continued:

“My daughter adores Art. Show Mr. Vale the little picture over the mantelpiece, Kaela—the one with the white fire.”

“It is a charcoal drawing of moonlight,” said Alfred.

“Exactly; white fire. I knew there was coal in it somewhere. Kaela understands all such matters, though she never displays her knowledge. She has always been deep.”

Kaela sighed and looked through the window.

Alfred’s heart trembled. He believed she was thinking of Rome, of moonlit ruins, perhaps of the imprisonment of the soul within mortal beauty. In reality, Kaela was wondering when supper would be served.

He sought her society from that evening. At first he found her reserve enchanting. When he spoke of Phidias, she listened; when he spoke of Michael Angelo, she listened; when he explained that sculpture was not the imitation of the body but the revelation of the idea imprisoned within it, she still listened, and once said, “Indeed?”

No applause is sweeter than that which we imagine.

“Do you not feel,” he asked her one afternoon, “that every noble form is but the visible garment of an invisible harmony?”

Kaela looked at her sleeve.

“This garment is new,” she said. “Mamma thinks the colour too faint.”

Alfred was momentarily checked; but youth in love is like a traveller who has paid in advance for his room: however uncomfortable the lodging, he is determined to think well of it.

“You have chosen the colour wisely,” he replied. “It resembles the first light over the Campagna.”

“Is that a shop?”

“A plain near Rome.”

“Oh.”

She gave another little sigh, and he attributed to it a longing for Italy. A sigh is a small vessel into which the imagination may pour a sea.

Mrs. Morn encouraged him eagerly.

“Kaela requires a superior husband,” she declared. “Not that ordinary husbands are inferior in a bad sense. Her poor father was a fine man and sold excellent timber; but he did not care for mythologies. You artists, I understand, have to know them all—Venus and Apollo and the gentleman who married the statue.”

“Pygmalion.”

“Yes, Pig Million. A very wealthy person, no doubt.”

Alfred laughed, but afterward he looked at Kaela and thought of Galatea. The sculptor in the old tale had loved a beautiful form and prayed life into it; Alfred, less fortunate and more conceited, believed life already present merely because the form was beautiful.

There were friends who attempted a warning. One observed that Kaela never read; another that she had no opinions; a third, more practical, remarked that she was always tired. Alfred dismissed them all. The envious call serenity stupidity, he said; the noisy suspect the silent; coarse natures demand that every flower should explain its perfume.

So he married her.

The wedding was greatly admired. Kaela looked more beautiful than ever, and Mrs. Morn wept throughout the ceremony, though she afterward explained that her tears came chiefly from a tight bonnet. Alfred felt himself the happiest of men. He had obtained the face he wished to see opposite him at breakfast for the rest of his life, without considering that breakfast is a conversational meal.

During the first weeks he continued his courtship after marriage, which is a noble custom and rarer than it ought to be. He read poetry aloud, brought Kaela sketches, and told her of his plans. She smiled, arranged flowers, complained of headaches, and asked whether the new cook could make custard.

At length Alfred completed a model upon which he had laboured for many months. It represented Dawn awakening the Earth: a bending female figure, grave yet tender, touching with one hand the closed eyes of a sleeping child.

He led Kaela into the studio.

“Well?” he asked.

“It is very white.”

“It is plaster.”

“Will it stay white?”

“No. The final work may be in marble.”

“Then it will stay white.”

“What do you think of the conception?”

Kaela walked around it obediently.

“Her foot is rather large.”

Alfred stared at her. The foot was not large; indeed, it was the finest part of the work.

“Is that all you see?”

Kaela, hearing displeasure but not understanding its cause, began to cry. Her tears were as becoming as her smiles, which made the quarrel more difficult. Alfred kissed her, called himself a brute, and for several days blamed his own vanity. Yet a little door had opened in his mind, and though he pushed it shut, it would not remain closed.

Marriage has many such doors. Before marriage, affection hangs curtains before them and paints landscapes upon the walls; afterward, some ordinary draught blows the curtains aside.

Mrs. Morn visited almost daily. She praised Alfred’s work with fearless inaccuracy.

“This figure is quite like the Torvaldsen,” she said one morning.

“Thorwaldsen was a sculptor, not a figure.”

“Of course. I mean she is like something he would have been, had he been a woman. And what will you call her?”

“Psyche.”

“Why does she?”

“Psyche is her name.”

“How curious! Kaela, love, Alfred says she sighs because that is her name.”

“She does not sigh,” said Alfred.

Mrs. Morn peered at the statue. “No; you are right. She looks as if she were holding it in.”

Such conversations, repeated often, wore upon Alfred like a loose button at a formal dinner: a small thing, certainly, and unworthy of tragedy, but impossible to forget once one has noticed it. Worse still, Kaela never perceived her mother’s absurdities. Sometimes she repeated them. At other times she sat tranquilly while Alfred struggled between courtesy and despair.

He began to travel more frequently, on the excuse of commissions. Absence gave him relief, but not happiness. He was ashamed of his disappointment, for Kaela had not deceived him. She remained precisely what she had been: beautiful, gentle when not inconvenienced, passive, and easily fatigued. He had mistaken the casket for its contents, and it is unjust to reproach a casket because we ourselves imagined jewels within it.

When he returned from one journey, he found a visitor in the house.

“This is Sophy Lorne,” Kaela said. “We were at school together. She is to stay a fortnight.”

Sophy was small, brown-haired, and plain enough that no one had ever compared her to a goddess. Her nose was too decided, her mouth too wide, and her hands showed evidence of useful employment. But her eyes were steady and kind, and when Alfred spoke, she listened not merely with her ears but with her intelligence.

At dinner Mrs. Morn described a recent lecture.

“The gentleman proved,” she said, “that the Greeks painted all their statues bright red and green. I have always said the ancients had very cheerful tastes.”

“He argued only that traces of colour remain on some sculpture,” Sophy observed.

“Precisely. Red and green.”

“Not necessarily.”

“But if the red has vanished, my dear, how can he know it was not red? That is logic.”

Sophy smiled. “By the same reasoning, Mrs. Morn, your black shawl may once have been orange.”

Mrs. Morn looked alarmed and examined the fringe.

Alfred laughed—freely, gratefully, almost with astonishment. Kaela stared from one to the other and asked whether they would have pudding.

The fortnight extended into a month. Sophy brought into the house what fresh air brings into a long-closed room. She knew when Kaela’s weariness was real and when it was habit; she quieted Mrs. Morn without wounding her; she ordered the servants competently; she read new books; she understood Alfred’s work, and, what was rarer, she disagreed with him intelligently.

“You have made the face too conscious of its sorrow,” she told him of a figure representing Captivity.

“Can sorrow be unconscious?”

“Much of the deepest sorrow is. A child suffers before it understands that suffering has a name.”

Alfred altered the face.

He began to perceive that sympathy is not merely agreement, nor intelligence merely stored knowledge. Sophy’s mind met his as one clear stream meets another, changing direction without losing itself. Yet he guarded his thoughts, for Kaela was his wife, and Sophy loved her.

Indeed, if Kaela had little to give, Sophy gave to her without calculation. She brushed her hair, read to her when she would listen, walked with her slowly in the garden, and bore a hundred small complaints with an ease that made patience appear natural. Those who possess rich inward lives are not always generous; they may hoard their treasures. Sophy’s wisdom was beautiful because it served.

Winter came, and Kaela’s delicacy increased. She coughed, grew pale, and tired after crossing a room. Physicians were called. One recommended the south; another complete rest; a third, who had a magnificent beard and therefore inspired Mrs. Morn’s confidence, prescribed both travel and immobility.

They went to the coast, but the sea winds were too sharp. They returned home, and Kaela took to her bed.

Her beauty did not vanish at once. It became finer, more transparent, almost unearthly, and Alfred, sitting beside her, felt the old enchantment mingled with remorse. The lovely casket was breaking. He would gladly have surrendered every disappointed hope if he could have restored her to health.

Sophy nursed her by day and night. Mrs. Morn talked of recoveries she had known, all miraculous and none applicable.

“My cousin’s daughter was worse,” she assured them. “The doctors had entirely given her up, and then they discovered it was only her stays.”

Kaela listened faintly.

“Shall I be well in spring?” she asked Sophy.

“I hope so, dear.”

“But do you think so?”

Sophy took her hand. Compassion does not always answer questions; sometimes it merely refuses to leave the questioner alone.

Alfred came in one evening and found Kaela looking at a small clay figure on the mantelpiece, an early study he had made of her before their marriage.

“I was prettier then,” she said.

“You are beautiful now.”

“Yes,” she whispered, pleased; and after a pause she added, “You always liked me to be beautiful.”

The words entered him like a rebuke, though she had not meant them so.

“I should have liked you in any form,” he said.

Kaela smiled, but perhaps illness had granted her one moment of clearer sight, for she answered, “I do not think you would.”

She died before the first spring flowers opened.

At the funeral Mrs. Morn’s grief was loud, confused, and entirely sincere. Alfred’s was silent. Sophy stood beside them both. Kaela was laid beneath a stone on which Alfred carved only her name and a spray of lilies. He would not carve her face. For the first time in his life, he understood that art cannot preserve what life itself has never fully awakened; and yet he also understood that weakness is not wickedness, nor emptiness a crime.

Sophy returned to her own family. The house became intolerably still. Alfred worked, travelled, and gained fame; but praise no longer intoxicated him. He had learned that a man may model heroic limbs and remain ignorant of the human heart, just as one may know every measurement of a temple and never pray.

Two years passed before he visited Sophy.

He found her in a modest garden, tying a rosebush to a wall. She looked up, and the welcome in her face was neither surprise nor display, but warmth governed by understanding.

Alfred loved her; perhaps he had long loved her, though honour had forbidden him to name the feeling even to himself. He spoke now without ornament.

“Sophy, I once chose as a sculptor chooses—with my eyes. I worshipped form and imagined spirit within it. I have learned that beauty is dust and clay unless a living soul shines through.”

Sophy’s hands became still upon the rose stem.

“You speak too harshly of beauty,” she said.

“Kaela was beautiful, and there was nothing behind it.”

“There was something,” Sophy answered. “Something weak, undeveloped, and sleeping, perhaps; but not nothing.”

“You, who knew her, can say that?”

“I knew her well enough to pity her. She had been praised all her life for what required no effort, and no one asked her to become more. You admired the statue, Alfred, but did you ever try patiently to awaken the woman?”

He bowed his head.

Sophy laid her hand upon his.

“We are all unfinished,” she continued. “Some are rough blocks; some are finely shaped and hollow; some are marred by careless hands. But if there is another life—and I believe there is—perhaps the Master does not throw away the clay because the first moulding failed. Kaela may yet grow into the harmony you expected too soon.”

Alfred looked at her, and it seemed to him that he saw beauty as though for the first time—not the beauty that arrests a room, but that which enlarges the heart of the beholder.

They were married quietly the following year.

Their happiness was not the happiness of a fairy tale, if by that we mean a life without illness, disagreement, bills, foolish visitors, or burnt dinners. Such happiness would be unsuitable for human beings and very tiresome besides. They differed often; but their differences led somewhere. They suffered; but neither suffered alone. Alfred’s art became gentler and greater, while Sophy’s plain face grew dearer to him with every year, until he could not understand how he had ever called it plain.

And above his worktable he kept no portrait of antique loveliness, but a small unfinished figure of a sleeping girl, one hand slightly raised, as if she might yet awaken.

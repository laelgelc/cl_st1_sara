# THREE CASES AT TWILIGHT

It was on one of those London evenings when daylight seems less to depart than to be absorbed by brick, smoke, and damp, that Inspector Wield favoured us with three passages from his professional recollections. He had come in quietly, as was his custom, and had seated himself where the last grey light from the window fell upon one side of his face, leaving the other in shadow. There was nothing theatrical in the arrangement, though it would have served a painter very well.

“You ask me, sir,” said he, “whether a little thing mayn’t tell a large story. It may. Likewise it may tell no story at all, or tell the wrong one; which is a distinction some people won’t make till they’ve got an innocent man halfway to the gallows.”

This observation introduced the first case.

## I. THE GLOVES UNDER THE PILLOW

“You’ll recollect the Grimwood murder, perhaps,” said Inspector Wield. “Eliza Grimwood, a woman living by herself in a small house off the Borough Road. She wasn’t rich, she wasn’t young, and she wasn’t what you’d call well connected; but she was respectable, hard-working, and known for twenty years in the neighbourhood. Found dead in her bed, sir, with marks of violence about the throat and the room turned over as if money had been looked for.

“There was no money found, though it was believed she’d saved some. There was no weapon found. There was no person seen to enter, except that people will always remember a stranger after a murder, and never before it.

“I went over the room careful. Window fastened. Back door fastened. Front-door lock had been worked by a key, or else the woman herself had let the party in. Cup and saucer on the table, and a second chair pulled a little away from it. That looked friendly. No common street robber would sit down to tea before he began.

“Under the dead woman’s pillow we found a pair of gentleman’s gloves.

“Now, sir, there was the little thing that promised the large story.

“They was light-coloured kid gloves, had once been good, but was soiled, creased, and worn thin between the fingers. There was a split by the thumb of the right hand, and inside the left was written, very small, a name—‘Trinkle.’ Not printed by the maker, mind you, but wrote in ink.

“‘Who’s Trinkle?’ says the local officer.

“‘That’s what we’ve got to learn,’ says I.

“It didn’t take long to learn there was a young man of that name, an upholsterer, living over Lambeth way. Quiet, modest young fellow, with a widowed mother. He had been seen speaking to Eliza Grimwood three nights before she died; he had once done a little work at her house; and he had left his employment rather sudden on the morning after the murder.

“Beautiful!” said Wield, with a dry extension of the word. “Beau-ti-ful evidence, if you didn’t look at it too close. Name in the gloves. Gloves in the bed. Young man known to the woman. Young man leaving his shop directly afterwards. There’s your case tied up with pink tape and ready for court.

“But I didn’t know how the gloves got there.

“I went to Mr. Trinkle’s employer. He was a cabinet-maker and upholsterer in a narrow shop, all horsehair, varnish, shavings, and sofas standing on end like coffins that had changed their minds.

“‘Trinkle gone?’ says I.

“‘Gone to Birmingham,’ says the master.

“‘Why?’

“‘Better wages.’

“‘When arranged?’

“‘A fortnight ago.’

“That knocked the sudden flight on the head. It had only happened sudden to them as didn’t know of it.

“‘Did he own light kid gloves?’ says I.

“The master laughed. ‘Young men own more gloves than they can pay for,’ says he.

“His shopmates remembered the gloves. One thought Trinkle had worn ’em at a tea-garden; another said it was at his cousin’s wedding; a third said he’d given ’em away; and a fourth was sure he’d lost ’em out of his pocket on Westminster Bridge. Four witnesses, four certainties, and not a penn’orth of truth among the lot of ’em.

“I got the address in Birmingham, but before taking him I wanted the gloves followed backwards. A pair of gloves don’t walk under a pillow, sir. They’re carried, dropped, sold, lent, stole, or given. There’s always a hand behind ’em, even when there ain’t a hand inside ’em.

“I tried the makers first. The gloves was French, sold by half a dozen houses. Then the shops. Then the little dealers. Then the cleaners.

“Glove-cleaning was more of a trade then than it is now, and a queer trade too. You’d take in a pair at a hosier’s in the Strand, and they might travel to Clerkenwell, then to Soho, then to a room up three pairs of stairs where a woman and two girls cleaned forty dozen a week and slept among the chemicals. Nobody kept proper books. One man remembered a stain and forgot a face; another remembered a face and swore the gloves was black; a woman said she’d never handled ’em, then owned she might have handled five hundred just like ’em.

“So I went here, and I went there; from the Borough to Holborn, from Holborn to Wardour Street, and from Wardour Street to a court where the smell coming out of the houses was enough to clean the skin off you without troubling about the gloves.

“At last I got word that a cleaner named Priddle sometimes marked customers’ goods in pencil under the wrist. I looked again, and there it was—a faint little cross and a figure seven. Priddle had moved. Of course he had. Useful people in inquiries always have moved; useless people live for fifty years in the same parlour.

“I traced him to a public-house near the New Cut. He had gone out. I waited. He didn’t come. I called next morning. He was in bed. I called at noon. He had gone to the theatre to see a friend employed behind the scenes.

“I went to the theatre, and there, by one of those chances that’s always called wonderful after you’ve worked three days to arrive at it, I met a man I knew.

“‘Why, Wield!’ says he. ‘What brings you among the drama?’

“‘Gloves,’ says I.

“‘Then you want Priddle. He’s a glove-cleaner.’

“‘That’s exactly the man I’ve been following.’

“‘Following him! He’s drinking half-and-half across the road.’

“There he was, sure enough: a little sharp-faced man with pale hands, as if he’d been washed in something stronger than water. I showed him the gloves.

“He smelled ’em.

“‘Mine,’ says he.

“‘Cleaned by you?’

“‘Tried to be. Grease won’t come out of that seam.’

“‘Who brought ’em?’

“He turned the left glove inside out, squinted at his mark, and rubbed his chin.

“‘Young woman.’

“‘What young woman?’

“‘Brown bonnet.’

“‘Name?’

“‘Don’t know.’

“‘Where did she leave ’em?’

“‘Hosier’s in Blackfriars Road, most likely.’

“We went to the hosier’s. The hosier remembered nothing until his wife came down; then he remembered everything she told him. A young woman had left the gloves, had called twice, and had refused to pay because they wasn’t properly cleaned.

“‘What became of ’em?’ says I.

“‘She took ’em,’ says the wife.

“‘Who was she?’

“‘I don’t know.’

“‘Would you know her?’

“‘Anywhere.’

“That generally means nowhere; but I kept her description. She was short, brisk, had a scar near the mouth, and wore a brown bonnet with a green ribbon.

“I made inquiries round Mrs. Grimwood’s neighbourhood, and found that her cousin, Mrs. Pell, answered pretty well to it, allowing for the ribbon having become blue in six months of recollection. Mrs. Pell worked at a laundry. I saw her.

“‘Did you take these gloves to be cleaned?’ says I.

“She looked at ’em and says, ‘Yes.’

“‘Whose were they?’

“‘Young Mr. Trinkle’s.’

“‘How did you come by ’em?’

“‘He gave ’em me.’

“‘What for?’

“‘Nothing.’

“‘Why did you put ’em under Eliza Grimwood’s pillow?’

“Then she gave a scream, dropped into a chair, and called me a wicked man.

“‘I never put ’em under no pillow!’ says she.

“‘They was found there.’

“‘Then Eliza put ’em there herself.’

“‘How came she to have ’em?’

“And out it came. Trinkle was courting Mrs. Pell’s daughter. He had spoiled the gloves with oil or varnish at the shop and was going to throw them away. The daughter thought they might be cleaned for her brother. Mrs. Pell took them to the hosier’s, quarrelled over the charge, and brought them home. Finding them still dirty, she gave them to Eliza Grimwood, who used old kid gloves in dusting polished furniture because, as she said, they took the bloom off better than a cloth. Eliza had likely placed them beneath the pillow while straightening the bed and forgot them there.

“So I went to Birmingham and arrested Mr. Trinkle.”

“You arrested him after that explanation?” I asked.

“I’d started before I got all of it proved,” said Wield. “Messages crossed. We hadn’t electric wires doing every job in those days. Besides, an explanation ain’t a fact merely because a frightened woman gives it to you.

“I found him at work, and I never saw a young fellow turn whiter.

“‘Am I took for the murder?’ says he.

“‘You’re took for inquiry,’ says I.

“‘Them gloves has done it,’ says he. ‘I knew them gloves would come up again.’

“That sounded bad.

“‘Why?’ says I.

“‘Because Mrs. Pell’s daughter and me had words about ’em. She said I gave her brother nothing but rubbish. I said her family was always making something out of what belonged to somebody else. Then I heard the gloves was found, and I thought they’d say I’d been there.’

“He came quietly. Before we reached London, the girl, her mother, the hosier, and Priddle had all confirmed the history. The dates agreed. Trinkle had been at his employer’s house on the night of the murder. He was discharged.

“The murderer was afterwards taken on other evidence, and confessed. He knew nothing of the gloves.

“That case taught a young constable something, and it may teach older heads the same. Evidence is evidence, sir; but meaning is another article. Them gloves spoke loud enough. Only they spoke false.”

The light had by this time wholly withdrawn from the window. A lamp was brought, and Inspector Wield, having contemplated it as though it too might require tracing through several hands, proceeded to his second account.

## II. THE HAND AT EPSOM

“The Swell Mob,” said he, “was very strong at Epsom that year. Every sort was there: prop-makers, magsmen, ring-droppers, pickpockets, and fellows dressed so handsome you’d trust ’em with your daughter while you went to fetch a policeman.

“Sergeant Witchem and me had four in view. Professional thieves, all of ’em. There was Long Ned, there was Billy Voss, there was a neat little man called Captain—not because he’d ever commanded anything but other men’s pockets—and there was Peachey Tom, who smiled continual, whether pleased or not.

“We watched ’em moving through the crowd. They didn’t keep together. Bless you, no. One pushed, one stopped, one begged pardon, and one profited. They’d come apart like drops of quicksilver and run together again twenty yards off.

“A gentleman near the stand had a diamond pin in his neckcloth, a large one, and foolishly shown. Witchem saw Long Ned mark it. I saw Peachey Tom place himself behind the gentleman. The Captain came across in front with a race-card, Billy Voss drove his shoulder into another man, and for half a second there was a jam.

“The pin was gone.

“‘Now,’ says Witchem.

“We went in. Witchem took Long Ned; I took Peachey; two country officers closed on the others. Then the crowd, seeing a struggle, pressed round to enjoy it, and every man cried, ‘What’s the matter?’ while making the matter ten times worse.

“Long Ned had the pin. Witchem knew it, and Ned knew he knew it. But to find a small diamond pin in the sleeves, cuffs, mouths, waistcoats, and hands of four practised men, all kicking, twisting, and passing whatever they held—there’s the difficulty.

“I had Peachey against the rail. He was all civility.

“‘Don’t tear my coat, inspector,’ says he. ‘It’s paid for.’

“‘That’s unusual,’ says I.

“Billy Voss went down. The Captain went over him. A country officer caught the Captain by the collar and received Billy’s heel in his stomach. Hats was flying, ladies screaming, and one old gentleman struck me with his umbrella under the impression that he was assisting justice.

“Long Ned had his right fist shut. Witchem gripped the wrist. Ned tried to bring the hand to his mouth. Witchem stopped him. Then Witchem did a curious thing.

“He loosened his hold for the smallest part of a second and tapped the back of Ned’s hand twice with his finger.

“Just so.” Wield demonstrated lightly upon the table. “Tap-tap.

“It was the signal an accomplice used when ready to receive.

“Ned never looked. He felt the taps, opened his fingers, and slipped the pin backwards into Witchem’s palm.

“Witchem closed his hand on it.

“Then says he, very quiet, ‘Thank you, Ned.’

“Ned’s face! I’d have paid a shilling to see it again. Rage, fright, and admiration all fighting together. A thief likes a clever trick, sir, even when it hangs him.

“We got the four away and found the owner, who had not discovered his loss. The pin was valuable; a family stone, he said, though family stones have a wonderful habit of appearing first at a jeweller’s.

“At the hearing, Witchem gave his evidence. Long Ned watched him with great attention. The court was crowded. The prisoners stood together, and there had been some carelessness about the passage leading down from the dock.

“While a witness was turning to identify Billy Voss, Ned vaulted the rail.

“He was through the passage before anybody understood he’d moved. Out by a side door, across a yard, over a low wall, and gone.

“There was a cry, and out we all came—officers, clerks, boys, idlers, and two gentlemen who had no more business there than the pigeons. We searched the lane, the sheds, the stables, the carts. Nothing.

“‘He can’t have vanished,’ says a magistrate’s clerk.

“‘No,’ says I, ‘but he can have climbed.’

“There was an old tree leaning over the wall, thick with leaves. I saw one branch move against the wind. We got beneath it.

“‘Come down, Ned,’ says Witchem.

“No answer.

“‘You’ll spoil your trousers,’ says I.

“A voice out of the leaves says, ‘You’ve spoilt my character already.’

“We saw one boot, then a knee, then his face looking down, very mournful. He had climbed high but chosen a branch too thin to move along and too rotten to trust coming back. He was as neatly imprisoned as if the tree had grown a lock.

“‘Fetch a ladder,’ says somebody.

“‘Fetch a saw,’ says Ned. ‘I’ll come with the branch.’

“We fetched the ladder. He kicked it away once, but Witchem climbed another limb and persuaded him. The persuasion consisted chiefly of explaining that a fall would break his neck, while a trial would only inconvenience him for a term of years.

“He was tried. The gentleman swore to the pin; Witchem swore to receiving it from Ned’s own hand; and Ned’s counsel tried to make out that the tap-tap was an unlawful temptation.

“‘Did you compel him to give you the pin?’ counsel asked.

“‘No,’ says Witchem.

“‘Did you request him?’

“‘Not in words.’

“‘Did you represent yourself as his confederate?’

“‘I represented two fingers on the back of his hand,’ says Witchem. ‘He supplied the rest.’

“That finished it. They was convicted.

“Witchem always maintained it was not a trick, but presence of mind. I told him presence of mind is the name an officer gives a trick when it succeeds.”

Inspector Wield smiled, but only for a moment.

“There’s comedy in our business,” he said. “You couldn’t keep it out if you tried. But it lies close beside things that ain’t comic at all.”

## III. UNDER THE SOFA

“The last case,” said Wield, “was Sergeant Dornton’s. He told it better than I can, and if you please, sir, I’ll give it as near as possible in his own words.”

Dornton, as Wield represented him, was a grave, patient officer, not easily carried by first appearances. His account ran thus:

“Well, sir, there was a hospital where the students hung their coats in a room adjoining the lecture-room. Money began to go. Half-a-crown first; then seven shillings; then a sovereign; then a pocketbook with notes. Always during lecture, and always from the coats.

“The porter was suspected. Porters generally are. They have keys, they’re poor, and they’re in the way; three circumstances enough to hang a man in common conversation.

“The gentleman who spoke to me was very strong against him.

“‘He alone can enter,’ says he.

“‘Then how do the students enter?’ says I.

“‘Of course they enter.’

“‘Then he ain’t alone.’

“The porter was an old soldier, stiff in one leg, with a wife ill at home. I watched him. He might have been dishonest, but he wasn’t our thief. He never entered the coat-room while lecture was on, except once to carry coal, and then money was lost from a coat at the opposite end while he stood in full sight of two pupils.

“‘One of the students,’ says I.

“They didn’t like that. A student might be wild, might run into debt, might play cards, might even borrow another man’s umbrella perpetual; but steal from a coat-peg—never!

“‘Very good,’ says I. ‘Then the money walks.’

“I examined the room. One door from the passage, one into the lecture-room, two windows too high for convenient use, and a sofa against the wall. An old horsehair sofa, low in front, torn underneath, and long enough for a man if he didn’t object to being folded contrary to nature.

“I objected, but I got under it.

“Before doing so, we prepared a pocketbook with marked money and put it in the breast-pocket of a coat near the middle peg. The owner went into lecture. I crawled beneath the sofa. They let the room settle quiet, and there I lay.

“It was dusty, sir. Dust above me, dust beneath me, horsehair in my collar, a spring against my ribs, and my nose about three inches from a button that had probably been there since the sofa was made. I could see the floor and the lower part of the coats. By turning my head I could see the door.

“The lecture began. I heard the professor’s voice through the wall, rising and falling. Ten minutes passed. Twenty. My left leg went dead. Then my right arm. I began to think the porter had been told, the students warned, and me planted there to study the habits of dust.

“The passage creaked.

“Somebody came to the door.

“He stopped.

“I saw a pair of boots. Young boots, well polished.

“He listened.

“Then he went away.

“Five minutes after, he came back.

“He opened the door very soft and stepped in. Green coat, light trousers, quick walk. I knew him by sight—a student belonging to the hospital. Expensively dressed, brisk in his manner, and thought well of by his companions.

“He shut the door, but not close. Stood still. Listened.

“Then he walked once round the room.

“He touched nothing first time. That was caution. He looked into the lecture-room through the crack, crossed to the windows, came back, and listened again.

“I scarcely breathed.

“He began with the coats nearest the door. Not plunging his hands in—no. He felt outside, gentle, with the tips of his fingers; a breast here, a side-pocket there; knew by the shape what was inside. Handkerchief. Keys. Case of instruments. Purse.

“He stopped at a sound from the lecture-room.

“Back he went to the window and looked out, as innocent as daylight.

“The sound ceased.

“He returned.

“He passed the planted coat once. Then twice. He was suspicious of it, I think, because it hung too conveniently. On his third round he took the pocketbook out, opened it, counted the money, put part into his trousers-pocket, and returned the empty book to the coat.

“That was enough.

“I came from under the sofa.

“Not dignified, sir. No man can emerge from under a low sofa with dignity. I got one shoulder out, then my head, then dragged the rest after me, covered with fluff and with my handkerchief tied round my forehead to keep the dust off.

“The young gentleman turned and saw me.

“He didn’t run. He couldn’t. His mouth opened, but nothing came.

“‘You’ve dropped something,’ says I.

“He looked at the floor.

“‘Not there,’ says I. ‘In your pocket.’

“He backed against the coats. The pegs rattled. He was as white as the wall.

“‘Who are you?’ says he.

“‘Police.’

“‘You’ve made a mistake.’

“‘Then give me the marked money, and I’ll apologise.’

“He put his hand into his pocket, pulled it out empty, and says, ‘Search me.’

“‘Presently.’

“‘Search me now!’

“He was getting braggadocia, hoping perhaps he had slipped the money somewhere. But I had seen every movement.

“I took him by the wrist, put my hand into the pocket, and brought out the coins. The marks answered.

“He sat down on the sofa—the very sofa I’d been under—and began to shake.

“‘Don’t let them see me,’ says he.

“The lecture was ending. Chairs moved beyond the wall.

“‘I can’t help that,’ says I.

“‘My father will die of it.’

“‘You should have thought of him before.’

“‘I did think. That’s the worst. I thought of everything.’

“The students came in, laughing and talking, and stopped when they saw us. The porter came behind them. I shall remember the porter’s face. He looked first at the young man, then at me, then at all the gentlemen who had suspected him. He said nothing.

“The prisoner was taken before the magistrate and afterwards committed for trial. Other losses was proved against him. He had debts, though his allowance was good; cards, betting, dinners, little presents—holes everywhere, and his honour used to stop ’em.

“He pleaded guilty.

“I visited him once in prison because he asked for me. He was quieter then.

“‘Sergeant,’ says he, ‘was there any chance you mightn’t have come out?’

“‘None.’

“‘I kept thinking I saw the sofa move.’

“‘It did move. I was cramped.’

“‘If I’d gone away the first time?’

“‘You wouldn’t have been charged that day.’

“‘That day,’ says he. ‘No. Another day.’

“He seemed to understand himself at last, which ain’t always in time to be useful.

“Before sentence, he got poison. How, I never knew for certain. Some said he had concealed it when arrested; some said it reached him in a parcel. He took it in his cell and was dead before the surgeon could save him.

“That’s all, sir.”

Inspector Wield ceased. The lamp made a small sound in the silence.

“And the porter?” I asked presently.

“Kept his place,” said Wield. “But some of them as accused him never could be quite civil to him afterwards. An innocent man’s apt to be disliked by those who have wronged him. He reminds ’em of the evidence.”

Outside, the London night had gathered complete: wheels in the wet street, a distant cry, hurried feet passing towards business, pleasure, danger, or home. The inspector rose and buttoned his coat.

“Gloves,” said he, “may accuse the wrong man. A thief may give the evidence out of his own hand. A gentleman may hide a thief under a fine coat, while the officer hides under a sofa. There ain’t one road to truth, sir. But whichever road you take, you’ve got to travel it to the end.”

With that he went out into the city, where innumerable little things were even then lying silent in rooms, pockets, gutters, shops, and human hands—each waiting for somebody to decide whether it had a story to tell, and whether that story was true.

Wal, you remember Parson Carryl, don’t ye? Him that preached over to North Winton nigh on thirty year, and could take up the prophecy of Ezekiel and turn it inside out afore a congregation without once losin’ his place. He knew Hebrew roots and Greek endings, and could tell what all the kings of Judah ought to have done if they’d only had the sense to ask him. But set him down afore a churn, or ask him whether a cucumber come on a vine or a bush, and there he was. His learning stopped short at the kitchen door.

When Mis’ Carryl died, folks was rail sorry. She’d been a still, patient sort of woman, always slidin’ round and makin’ things easy without lettin’ on there was anything to make easy. The Parson supposed the house naturally kept itself, same as the sun rose, till arter the funeral, when the fire went out, the cream soured, and there warn’t a clean collar in the drawer.

Then Huldy come.

She was Mis’ Carryl’s sister’s gal, or cousin’s gal—I never could keep them branches straight—but she was eighteen or thereabout, quick-footed, clear-eyed, and capable as a whole committee. She warn’t one o’ your rattlin’, gigglin’ gals. She could laugh, and did, but she knew what she was laughin’ at. She come to stay a spell, and inside of a fortnight the Parson had hot biscuits agin, and his stockings warn’t darned with white yarn across black heels.

But Mis’ Pipperidge and Mis’ Deacon Blodgett and old Aunt Jerushy Pike all said Huldy was too young.

“What Parson Carryl needs,” says Mis’ Pipperidge, “is a settled woman of experience.”

She herself was a widow of fifty-three, and had been settled so long she was nigh petrified.

“A house like that,” says she, “with parishioners comin’ and goin’, requires maturity.”

Wal, Huldy never answered. She jist went on makin’ the brown bread rise and keepin’ the Parson’s papers out o’ the apple-pie cupboard. But their remarks worked on him, for a minister hears so much advice he gets to think it’s his Christian duty to use some on’t.

One mornin’ he come into the kitchen with his coat buttoned up, lookin’ solemn.

“Huldy,” says he, “it has occurred to me that I ought not to leave the entire responsibility of my domestic establishment upon one so young.”

“No, sir?” says Huldy, slicin’ apples.

“I ought to be instructing and guiding,” says he. “The head of a household should comprehend its operations.”

“Yes, sir,” says Huldy, and she put another apple into curls without so much as winkin’.

That same day he commenced on the turkeys.

Huldy had a gray turkey-hen settin’ in a box under the shed, and she’d marked thirteen eggs for her. But the creature got skittish and quit the nest. There was the eggs gettin’ cold, and Huldy vexed, for she’d counted on them turkeys for Thanksgivin’, donations, and two ministerial dinners.

The Parson found her lookin’ at the deserted nest.

“What is the difficulty?” says he.

“The gray hen’s left,” says Huldy. “I’ve tried puttin’ her back, but she won’t set.”

“Is there no other fowl?” says he.

“Not a hen that’s broody.”

The Parson looked out in the yard, and there went the great bronze gobbler, spreadin’ his tail and puffin’ like a deacon that’s been asked to pray without warnin’.

“What prevents that one?” says the Parson.

Huldy stared. “That’s the tom.”

“I am aware of the distinction,” says he, dignified. “But the instinct of preservation is not confined to one sex. Moral suasion may awaken dormant responsibility.”

Huldy told me arterward she near swallowed a whole apple-seed, but she kept her face.

“I don’t believe he’ll set,” says she.

“He has never been properly directed,” says the Parson.

So he ketched the tom-turkey.

Now, catchin’ a tom-turkey that ain’t willin’ is like arguin’ doctrine with a man who’s brought his own texts. The Parson drove him from the woodpile to the pump, and from the pump to the currant bushes, and the turkey run and dodged and gobbled, and the Parson’s coat-tails streamed out behind. Finally they met at the shed door, both goin’ opposite ways. There was a cloud of dust, a flutter of feathers, and when it cleared the Parson had him by both legs.

“There!” says he, pantin’. “You perceive firmness has prevailed.”

The turkey was upside down and didn’t appear convinced.

The Parson lugged him to the nest, and Huldy stood by with her apron up to her mouth. He sot the bird atop them thirteen eggs and held him there with both hands, as solemn as if he was ordainin’ him.

“Remain,” says the Parson.

The tom’s neck stretched out a yard.

“Remain,” says the Parson agin.

Then the turkey riz straight up, with the Parson hangin’ on. They fussed and fuzzled and wuzzled all over that box. Straw flew, the board tipped, and the eggs went crack—one, two, six, all on ’em—till the Parson slipped and sot down square in the middle.

Lordy massy! There was yellow on his trousers, shell on his cuffs, and that turkey went out o’ the shed like he’d been converted agin domestic life forever.

For half a minute Huldy never spoke. Then she says, gentle as could be, “I guess instinct didn’t wake up.”

“No,” says the Parson, risin’ careful. “There appear to be physiological obstacles I had not sufficiently considered.”

He went into his study and took down the biggest Hebrew book he had. Whenever worldly affairs proved unreasonable, he went into Hebrew. There warn’t no eggs in Hebrew to break under him.

Huldy cleaned the nest, borrowed fifteen eggs from Mis’ Abner Pease, and set a little speckled hen on ’em. She never said one word round the village, neither. Mis’ Pipperidge heard it from the hired boy, who’d heard the gobblin’, and by night she’d improved the account so the Parson had tried to hatch the eggs himself. But Huldy always maintained it was the turkey, and I believe her.

After that he consulted her more, though not enough to prevent the pig business.

The parish had put up a new well-curb, a handsome square concern with a cover and a windlass. It stood near the back fence, and Zeb White, the carpenter, left some rails and boards around it. That same week Huldy bought a little spotted pig from Liphlet’s farm. She’d bargained him down fifty cents, too, by pointin’ out a crook in the pig’s tail which didn’t injure the pig but done considerable harm to Liphlet’s price.

The pig was to go in a pen behind the barn. But before Zeb finished it, the critter got loose.

The Parson come out from a funeral sermon and seen that pig rootin’ in the parsnip bed. He also seen the new square structure by the fence and, never havin’ examined it, took it for the pigpen.

“Huldy need not be troubled,” says he. “This is a simple matter.”

He chased the pig.

The pig warn’t theological. He didn’t care who was head of the household. He run through the beans, round the lilacs, under the clothesline, and between the Parson’s legs. The Parson ketched him at last by the hind parts, while the pig screeched enough to wake Pharaoh, and carried him, kickin’ and wrigglin’, to the new curb.

The cover warn’t nailed yet.

The Parson lifted it, says, “Now, sir, remain where you belong,” and dropped the pig in.

There come a splash.

Then there come a silence.

Then there come a squeal from the bowels of the earth.

The Parson looked down and seen the pig swimmin’ round in the parish water supply.

Huldy heard him callin’, and she come runnin’. She didn’t stop to ask how nor why. She fetched a clothes-basket, tied it to the well-rope, scattered meal in the bottom, and lowered it. The pig climbed in; Zeb and the hired boy hauled; and presently up he come, drippin’, indignant, and smellin’ of wet pig from one end to t’other.

“I mistook the purpose of the structure,” says the Parson.

“So I see,” says Huldy.

“I fear the water may require attention.”

“Yes, sir,” says Huldy. “Quite a lot of it.”

That well had to be pumped and scrubbed and limed, and for three days they carried water from Deacon Blodgett’s. The village women had a good deal to say.

“It shows,” says Mis’ Pipperidge, “what comes of entrustin’ a house to a child.”

Now how the Parson throwin’ a pig into a well proved Huldy was too young, I never could see. But gossip don’t need a bridge if it can jump.

The Parson, though, begun to see daylight. Arter the pig he gave up instructin’ Huldy in domestic operations. If a fence wanted mendin’, he asked her which man to hire. If a barrel of flour come cheap, he asked whether cheap flour was worth eatin’. If a hen looked melancholy, he didn’t prescribe moral suasion. He called Huldy.

And things prospered.

Huldy had peas climbin’ strings afore anybody else’s had made up their minds to sprout. She had pinks along the walk, sweet-williams by the stone wall, and mornin’-glories clear to the chamber windows. The pantry shelves shone with currant jelly, quince preserve, pickled beans, cucumber relish, dried apples, and jars of things no man ever notices till there ain’t none.

She kept accounts in a little blue book, and the Parson found at year’s end he’d saved thirty-seven dollars, though he’d ate better than before. She made Jonas, the hired man, hoe straight rows by jist standin’ at one end and lookin’. She cured the cow of kickin’ by tyin’ a soft rope where Jonas had used a club, and she got twelve turkeys from the speckled hen, every one lively, without employin’ the tom for anything but ornament.

Then came the ministers’ meetin’.

There was to be eighteen ministers, with several wives, two missionaries, and a professor from Andover who couldn’t digest onions but always ate ’em if they was visible. Mis’ Pipperidge said Huldy would never manage it.

“It takes experience,” says she. “There’ll be confusion.”

Wal, there was baked ham, roast fowl, chicken pie, mashed turnips, squash, potatoes, beans, brown bread, white bread, three kinds of pickles, mince pies, apple pies, Indian pudding, doughnuts, cheese, and coffee strong enough to carry a weak argument. Huldy arranged the spare rooms, borrowed chairs, labeled the ministers’ umbrellas, and put the onion dish down by the missionary where the professor couldn’t see it.

Everything went as smooth as oil on a warm griddle. The professor praised the dinner twice, and Parson Carryl looked across the table at Huldy as if she’d translated the whole Old Testament while makin’ gravy.

That was when the women changed their tune.

They couldn’t say she warn’t capable, for the evidence was settin’ on the table and askin’ for a second piece of pie. So they begun to say she was too capable on purpose.

Mis’ Pipperidge come to Huldy one afternoon while she was cuttin’ roses.

“My dear,” says she, “you are innocent of the world.”

“Mebbe,” says Huldy.

“A young woman cannot be too guarded.”

“No,” says Huldy.

“People notice,” says Mis’ Pipperidge.

“What do they notice?”

“That you consult with the Parson. That he depends upon you. That you sit together evenings over accounts.”

“We generally have the accounts between us,” says Huldy. “That makes it difficult to sit much farther apart.”

Mis’ Pipperidge puckered her mouth.

“I speak for your own good. Such things make talk.”

“Who makes it?” says Huldy.

Wal, that was too plain a question. Mis’ Pipperidge said she couldn’t betray confidences, though she’d come expressly to betray all she had.

Arter that Aunt Jerushy dropped in to warn Huldy that young gals should know their place, and Mis’ Blodgett wondered aloud whether the Parson’s late wife would approve, and two others peeked and poked and sighed till the house was full of sighs enough to drive a windmill.

Huldy stood it awhile. She laughed in public and worked as usual, but it hurt her. She’d loved Mis’ Carryl, and the notion that folks thought she was schemin’ for the Parson cut deep.

At last she packed her trunk.

She told me herself how it happened. She had folded her blue dress, put in her mother’s Bible, and laid the little account book on the Parson’s desk. Then she went to tell him she was leavin’.

He was writin’ a sermon on Christian charity.

“I guess you’ll have to get somebody else,” says Huldy.

The Parson took off his spectacles. “Somebody else for what?”

“For the house. I’m goin’ to Aunt Maria’s.”

“Is she ill?”

“No, sir.”

“Are you dissatisfied?”

“No, sir.”

“Then why?”

Huldy twisted her apron. “Folks are talkin’.”

“What folks?”

“All sorts.”

“What do they say?”

She wouldn’t tell him at first, but he questioned and questioned till she did. Then the Parson got up and walked from the desk to the window and back. He was a mild man, but his ears turned red.

“Huldy,” says he, “when my wife died, I believed my usefulness was nearly ended. I could not order my own days. I was lonely, confused, and, as subsequent events have demonstrated, liable to impose grave responsibilities upon unsuitable turkeys.”

Huldy laughed in spite of herself.

“You have restored peace to this house,” says he. “You have guarded my interests, improved my health, prevented waste, and rescued livestock from consequences into which I had ignorantly precipitated it.”

“The pig warn’t much harmed,” says Huldy.

“That does not lessen the principle,” says he. “I have depended upon you because your judgment is superior to mine in matters where mine is exceedingly poor. If malicious persons have misrepresented that dependence, I regret it. But I cannot consent to lose you.”

“I can’t stay and be talked about,” says Huldy.

The Parson walked agin. Then he stopped afore her with the look he wore when he’d reached the final division of a sermon.

“There appears,” says he, “to be one honorable and decisive method of rendering such conversation pointless.”

Huldy looked up.

“If you were my wife,” says the Parson, “no person could object to your managing my household, my garden, my accounts, or myself. And I should esteem it the greatest mercy Providence has shown me since my bereavement. Huldy, will you marry me?”

She went red as the roses she’d been cuttin’.

“Is that because of the talk?” says she.

“No,” says he. “The talk has merely compelled me to understand what the turkey, the pig, the ministers, and every peaceful day in this house ought to have taught me already.”

Wal, Huldy says she made him wait near a whole minute, though I expect it only seemed so to him.

Then she says, “Yes, sir.”

They was married the next Thursday. Mis’ Pipperidge attended in her best bonnet and said she’d suspected it from the first, which was true enough, though not in the way she meant.

And the funniest part was, arter the ceremony the Parson asked Huldy where they ought to go for their wedding journey.

“Nowhere jist yet,” says she. “The beans need pickin’, the north chimney needs pointin’, and them turkeys won’t feed themselves.”

“Your judgment,” says the Parson, “is entirely sound.”

So they stayed home, and, from that day to this, Parson Carryl never undertook to improve a turkey, pen a pig, or instruct Huldy in anything she already understood.

Which, I tell ye, was the principal sign that marriage had made him a wiser man.

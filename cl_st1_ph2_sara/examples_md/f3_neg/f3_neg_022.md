### The White Denizen

It was only when the last tremulous flame upon my torch had dwindled to a blue and almost immaterial bead that I admitted, with the cold deliberation of one pronouncing sentence upon himself, the full enormity of my situation. I was lost—hopelessly and, as it seemed, eternally lost—in those vast and labyrinthine recesses which extend beneath the green pastoral surface of Kentucky like the corridors of some buried, pre-human world.

For nearly three hours I had wandered without encountering any token of the authorised route: no blackened scorch upon the limestone, no pencilled name left by some irreverent tourist, no discarded fragment of candle, nor any footprint distinguishable in the ancient dust. Each passage had divided into others; each seeming return had betrayed me into some unsuspected gallery or sepulchral vault. Above, beyond an incalculable weight of rock and earth, the sun might still have shone upon woods, roads, and inhabited farms; but to me those things had already assumed the remoteness of memories belonging to another existence.

I had separated myself from the party by design. This confession, though injurious to any reputation I may possess for prudence, is indispensable to the proper relation of the affair. The guide, a coarse but competent fellow named Elias Gentry, had warned us repeatedly against the lateral openings which perforated the chief avenue. Yet prohibition has ever exercised upon the philosophical mind an attraction scarcely inferior to that of discovery itself. Whilst my companions admired certain columns and mineral curtains to which childish appellations had been attached, I observed behind a shelving rampart a narrow passage whose floor bore no recent marks. The promise of solitude, together with an antiquarian curiosity regarding whatever relics might lie beyond the vulgar circuit, overcame my better judgement.

I intended an absence of ten minutes.

For a time the adventure rewarded me. The passage widened into chambers possessing a solemnity no cathedral could equal. Pale formations descended from the roof like the frozen draperies of gigantic mourners, and here and there the walls exhibited curious scratches which I endeavoured to interpret as the rude memorials of aboriginal habitation. My torch, though imperfect, sufficed to disclose those wonders; and I advanced in that composed ecstasy which attends the contemplation of nature where nature has forgotten man.

It was not until I attempted to retrace my steps that the deception of the place became apparent. Features which, on entering, had seemed unmistakable were multiplied everywhere with malignant uniformity. A pointed stone which I had thought unique confronted me in three separate corridors. A low arch, remembered with certainty, led not to the main avenue but to a descending fissure whose breath was damp and glacial. I called once for the guide, but the cave absorbed the sound so strangely that it appeared less a cry than the remote complaint of another wanderer.

Thereafter I husbanded both voice and flame. I reasoned that my companions, discovering my absence, must soon communicate the fact to Gentry; that a search would commence; and that the surest course lay in remaining near some conspicuous junction. Unfortunately, this conclusion occurred only after many injudicious changes of direction. I could no longer identify the place where I had quitted the party, nor even determine whether I stood above or below the level of the public galleries.

The torch declined with terrible rapidity. Its light contracted from a broad yellow blossom to a wavering tongue, and from that to a red point surrounded by an immensity of blackness. In the final minute I held it close to the floor and beheld a tract of grey dust, several stones, and the smooth wall curving away at my left. These mean objects, because they were the last things I expected ever to see, impressed themselves upon my recollection with unnatural distinctness.

Then came extinction.

No darkness known upon the earth’s surface may be compared with that of a cavern. Night has its stars, its vague horizons, its pallid vapours, and the obscure distinction betwixt earth and sky. Even the sealed chamber retains some suggestion of surrounding life: the rumble of wheels, the trembling of glass, the whisper of wind against a roof. But there, in the bowels of the continent, blackness was not the mere absence of light. It possessed density, weight, and an almost palpable malignity. I raised my hand before my face and could not prove, save by muscular sensation, that the hand existed.

For a while I maintained a tranquillity which I have since regarded with mingled pride and astonishment. Panic could provide no illumination and no food; therefore panic was useless. I sat against the wall, arranged my coat beneath me to resist the chill, and considered the probable duration of life under such conditions. There was moisture enough upon the stone to postpone death by thirst, though starvation and gradual failure of bodily heat remained inevitable. I calculated, with perhaps absurd exactitude, the number of days during which consciousness might continue.

Yet reason, however ceremoniously invoked, is a frail lamp in total darkness. The silence magnified each involuntary act of the body. My breathing became the labour of a bellows; the movement of my sleeve resembled the stirring of some hidden reptile; the beating of my heart seemed loud enough to guide all subterranean creation towards me. Disordered fancy peopled the passages with beings for which natural history had no name—blind serpents coiled in chasms, pallid insects of monstrous dimensions, and survivals from those elder geological epochs whose bones alone had hitherto announced them to mankind.

To interrupt these phantoms I began speaking aloud. I recited fragments of Latin verse, reviewed the chronology of the Roman emperors, and even delivered to the darkness a short discourse upon the consolations of philosophy. My voice sounded thin and impertinent amid those interminable recesses. At length composure broke beneath the indignity of my proposed extinction. To perish anonymously, within a few miles of roads and habitations, seemed more intolerable than death itself.

I rose and shouted.

“Gentry! Halloo! Gentry!”

The appeal rushed away through unseen corridors, was divided and distorted by a thousand stony surfaces, and returned as a congregation of distant cries. I shouted again, then waited until the last mockery had subsided.

From somewhere ahead came an answering sound.

My first emotion was so violent a hope that I laughed aloud. It was not an articulate response, but a faint disturbance—an irregular series of contacts upon stone. I called again and listened. The noise ceased, as though its author had halted to attend. After an interval it recommenced.

There could now be no doubt of locomotion.

I addressed the approaching person in several forms, supposing Gentry might be advancing cautiously or that my voice, deformed by echoes, had rendered the words unintelligible. No reply followed. The steps continued with a softness inconsistent with a booted man. Moreover, their rhythm was peculiar: not the alternating tread of two feet, but a confused succession of four slight impacts, occasionally interrupted by a harder click.

Hope diminished; apprehension succeeded it.

I knew that certain beasts had occasionally found entrance to the outer chambers of the cavern. The thought of a wolf occurred to me, though I believed wolves extinct in that district. A mountain lion seemed more plausible. Perhaps the creature had entered through some remote fissure and, lost like myself, survived by preying upon rats, bats, or such lesser organisms as inhabited the depths. My cries had awakened either its curiosity or its hunger.

The sound approached.

Nearer—nearer.

I could discern, in addition to the footfalls, a faint scraping suggestive of claws, and once a low expulsion of breath. There was stealth in the pauses. The creature would advance several paces, stop, and then resume with such caution that I pictured its head lowered, its nostrils testing the stagnant air. Since the passages possessed no current perceptible to me, I could not determine whether my scent had already betrayed my location.

The revolver I habitually carried had been left at my hotel, owing to the guide’s insistence that firearms within the cave were unnecessary and hazardous. I possessed only a stout walking-stick, which I had lost during an earlier descent, and a pocket-knife too small to be serviceable against any formidable animal. Remembering the stones glimpsed by the torch’s final glow, I crouched and felt along the floor until my fingers closed upon one of convenient weight. A second lay near it. I placed both upon my knees and selected several smaller fragments.

This preparation restored some portion of my reason. I inferred that a beast adapted to darkness might be acutely sensitive to sound, and that a missile, even if it failed to injure, could confuse or frighten it. On the other hand, should the animal spring, its leap might be guided by the noise of my throwing arm. I therefore resolved to wait until its proximity afforded the greatest likelihood of a decisive stroke.

Never have I endured minutes of comparable duration. The unseen thing advanced, halted, breathed, and advanced again. At moments it seemed immediately before me; at others the treacherous acoustics transferred it to the rear or placed it above my head. The clicking became distinct. I interpreted it as the contact of long claws with the limestone.

Now close at hand.

Now very close.

A smell reached me—not the hot, musky odour I associated with carnivorous beasts, but something dank, earthen, and indescribably stale, as though a grave had opened after centuries of confinement. My courage departed. All those rational distinctions with which I had fortified myself dissolved beneath the primitive certainty of being hunted.

I flung the first stone.

The missile struck rock and bounded noisily away. At once the footfalls ceased. There followed a silence so complete that I feared the creature had withdrawn beyond hearing and was circling by another route. I listened until the strain became intolerable.

Then, from scarcely twenty feet away, sounded a rapid scuttling rush.

I hurled the second stone with all my strength. A dull impact followed, succeeded by a shriek unlike any utterance of the recognised animal kingdom. It was high, broken, and horribly prolonged. The thing stumbled but continued forward. I cast another fragment, then another, directing each towards the panting and scraping. One struck with a brittle crack. The rush ended in a heavy collapse.

For several seconds I remained crouched with an uplifted stone. The fallen creature moved convulsively. Its limbs beat the floor in an irregular fashion; its claws—or what I still supposed to be claws—rasped against the rock. Presently the struggles weakened to a slow dragging noise.

Terror then accomplished what calculation had failed to do. I fled.

Direction was immaterial. With one hand extended before my face and the other sliding along the wall, I ran through the black passage, bruising my knees and shoulders, stumbling over inequalities, and uttering noises which no cultivated man would willingly acknowledge as his own. Behind me I imagined the beast rising, following, gathering itself for a spring. Every echo became its pursuit. Once I struck a projection and fell, and the resulting shock nearly deprived me of consciousness; but I rose and continued, gibbering entreaties to Providence, Gentry, and even the nameless powers of the cavern.

After what may have been five minutes or fifty, I saw a light.

At first it appeared as a pin-point of dull gold, so feeble that I suspected hallucination. It vanished, returned, and enlarged. Presently I heard my own name, pronounced by a human voice.

The effect upon me was humiliating. I shouted, sobbed, and staggered towards the radiance with both arms extended. Gentry emerged round a bend carrying a powerful oil lantern and a coil of rope. His face, beneath its helmet, displayed astonishment at my disordered condition; yet he wasted no time in reproach. He had searched for nearly six hours, he said, after despatching the others to the entrance with an assistant.

Safety did not immediately restore my faculties. I clung to his sleeve and insisted that we depart without delay, speaking wildly of a lion, a subterranean brute, and perhaps a species unknown to science. Gentry listened gravely. When I described the assault and the direction of my flight, he declared that no large beast had ever been encountered so deep within the cave. Nevertheless, the possibility of an injured creature surviving behind us made retreat unsafe, for our route might approach it by another passage.

“We’d best look,” he said.

Had I been alone, no power could have induced my return; but the broad circle of his lantern altered the cavern’s character. Walls sprang into existence. The floor became measurable. Darkness retreated from an absolute universe into a mere boundary surrounding light. Ashamed of my previous collapse, and increasingly curious regarding the thing I had struck, I consented.

We retraced my course slowly. Blood appeared upon one sharp corner where I had fallen, and scraps of cloth marked another collision. After some difficulty we entered the wider gallery in which the encounter had occurred. Gentry lifted the lantern.

At first we saw only stones scattered across the floor. Then the light touched a pale shape beside the farther wall.

The creature lay upon its belly, with its head turned away and its limbs gathered unnaturally beneath it. Its dimensions approximated those of a small man, though the attenuation of its frame gave an impression of greater length. The skin was white—not the ordinary pallor of illness, but a colourless, waxen whiteness resembling the flesh of organisms bred wholly beyond the influence of the sun. A thin growth of hair covered the shoulders and spine. This too was white, save where darkened by dirt and moisture.

Gentry bent nearer, though I remained several paces behind.

The feet were naked, enormously broadened, and calloused to the hardness of hoofs. The toes curved inward and terminated in nails so elongated that they had become veritable claws. The hands exhibited a similar deformity. Their palms were thick, the fingers crooked, and the arms of unusual length; hence the quadrupedal progression I had heard was readily explained. Whether this posture represented an inherited peculiarity or an adaptation acquired through protracted locomotion beneath low roofs, I could not determine.

The head was grotesquely malformed by emaciation. Its jaw projected; its cheeks had sunk to deep cavities; and its ears lay close against the skull. When Gentry cautiously shifted the lantern, the being stirred and rolled partly upon its side.

I then beheld its eyes.

They were open, but whether they retained sight seemed doubtful. The pupils, prodigiously expanded, occupied nearly the whole visible surface, producing the impression of two black hollows rather than organs of vision. At the light’s approach they contracted feebly, and the creature uttered a hoarse whimper. I perceived upon its temple the wound made by my stone. Another injury had crushed the ribs, and dark blood issued with each shallow respiration.

Despite its hideousness, I could not regard it wholly without admiration. Here, I thought, was some unknown anthropoid, perhaps descended from a species preserved in the cavern since a remote epoch. Its pigmentation, eyes, claws, and mode of progression all testified to an exquisite, if terrible, accommodation to eternal night. Isolation had fashioned it as ocean depths fashion their blind and pallid inhabitants. That a warm-blooded animal of such magnitude could maintain itself there was astonishing, yet not inconceivable; fungi, bats, fish, and subterranean streams might compose a meagre chain of sustenance.

Gentry, less inclined towards speculation, removed his coat and knelt as though to cover it. At this act the creature’s hand moved. The crooked fingers closed weakly around his wrist.

Its mouth opened.

I expected another animal cry, but the sound which emerged was low, deliberate, and burdened with immeasurable fatigue. The accent was strange and archaic, as if preserved from some elder generation; nevertheless, the words were unmistakable.

“Don’t leave me—in the dark.”

The lantern shook in Gentry’s grasp.

All my classifications perished in that instant. The muzzle became a wasted mouth; the paws became hands; the grotesque body, stripped of its imagined zoological difference, revealed the relic of humanity. What years or decades had passed over that victim in the inky confines—what accidental separation, what starvation, what gradual abandonment of speech, posture, reason, and form—I cannot conjecture. Perhaps, like me, he had strayed from some forgotten company. Perhaps the cave had received him as a child and remade him according to its own blind laws.

He drew one final breath. His black eyes turned, not towards us, but towards the lamp.

Then he died.

I had defended myself successfully against no beast. With stones, in ignorance and terror, I had slain a man.

And as I gazed upon that white, ruined countenance, there arose within me no triumph, but a confusion of wonder, awe, compassion, and a horror more profound than fear; for I understood that the darkness beneath the earth does not merely conceal monsters.

Given time enough, it creates them from us.

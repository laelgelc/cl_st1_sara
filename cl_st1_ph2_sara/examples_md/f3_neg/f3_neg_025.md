**A LETTER CONCERNING THE ANTIQUITIES OF COMMUNIPAW**

_To the Editor of the New-York Historical Register._

Sir,

Having observed, with equal surprise and mortification, that certain writers of our infant antiquities have bestowed whole pages upon battles in which no one was killed, treaties which no one observed, and public edifices which have already been pulled down, while they dismiss in half a sentence the venerable settlement of Communipaw, I have deemed it a duty, owing alike to truth, patriotism, and my maternal grandfather, to transmit the following legendary and statistical particulars.

I write, Sir, not as a dealer in idle tales, but as one who has examined tombstones, interrogated weathercocks, compared family tea-spoons, and smoked with the oldest inhabitants. Where written testimony has failed, I have consulted tradition; where tradition has hesitated, I have supplied the deficiency by reasonable conjecture; and where conjecture itself has refused to proceed, I have accepted the account of my aunt Hillegonda, a vrouw of such rigid veracity that she was never known to alter a story after the third recital.

Communipaw, as the learned reader is doubtless aware, lies upon the western shore of that noble bay which New-York has of late years appropriated to itself, much as a prosperous son appropriates the silver buckles, family Bible, and best arm-chair of his neglected mother. Seen from the water in former days, the village presented a low and comfortable appearance. Its red roofs sat among orchards; its peaked gables looked over cabbage gardens; its weathercocks turned deliberately, as if unwilling to commit themselves upon any question of wind; and its inhabitants, seated upon sunny stoops, smoked and smoked, and watched and watched, until evening released them from the labours of observation.

Across the bay towered New-York, thin, noisy, aspiring and perpetually lengthening; while Communipaw remained broad, placid and near the earth. The relation between them was that of a squat little Dutch dame and her overgrown grenadier son, who, having got into a new coat, denies that he was ever carried in petticoats. Yet Communipaw was unquestionably the elder. Indeed, several authorities maintain that New-York sprang from it; and I possess an ancient tile, painted with a blue hen and a long-legged turkey, which, in the judgment of competent antiquaries, is a hieroglyphical representation of that historical fructification.

The origin of Communipaw’s peculiar sanctity must be sought in the melancholy year when the British obtained possession of New-Amsterdam. On that disastrous occasion, while certain faint-hearted burghers accepted the new domination, translated their signboards, and learned to pronounce “good morning” with an asperity offensive to every Dutch ear, a small but heroic band refused submission. At their head was Garret Van Horne, a man of immovable principles, ample breeches, and a countenance resembling a full moon seen through tobacco smoke.

Garret assembled his adherents beneath a pear-tree near the city wall. There were among them Claas Ten Broeck, who had never spoken rapidly; Roelof Van Pelt, who could remember every dinner eaten by his ancestors; Stoffel Brinkerhoff, celebrated for sleeping through three alarms of fire; and the widow Annetje Van Groll, whose opposition to the English arose from their practice of boiling vegetables beyond the limits prescribed by nature.

Garret addressed them in a speech which has unhappily perished, though its duration is known to have been two hours and seventeen minutes. He compared their condition to that of the Israelites, the Trojans, the ancient Batavians, and a cheese beset by hungry mice. He concluded by proposing that they abandon the conquered city, cross the river, and preserve in some secluded spot the language, manners, cookery and tobacco of New-Netherlands.

The proposal was accepted without a dissenting voice, though Stoffel Brinkerhoff, having fallen asleep during the second historical comparison, was afterwards obliged to assent retrospectively. That very night the faithful removed their household gods: beds of mountainous feathers; brass kettles polished like provincial suns; cupboards bearing fleets of delft vessels; clocks which struck according to conscience rather than time; portraits of Hogen Mogens with ruffs like millstones; pipes, warming-pans, spinning-wheels, cradles, skates, sausages, seed-cabbages, and sundry jars whose contents were too precious to be named outside the family.

They landed at Communipaw by moonlight and there established a new commonwealth. Garret Van Horne was chosen first magistrate, chief historian and inspector of chimneys. He caused a mansion to be erected upon a slight eminence, with four enormous chimneys, one toward each quarter of the heavens, in token that Dutch smoke should ascend impartially over the world. This structure became known as the House of the Four Chimneys, and was at once town-hall, archive, fortress, tavern upon public occasions, and private residence of the Van Horne family.

Within its principal chamber were preserved the reliques of the former governors of New-Netherlands: the hat of Walter the Doubter, broad enough to shelter a burgomaster and his council; the shoe of Peter Stuyvesant, of prodigious length and hardness; a fragment of Oloffe the Dreamer’s nightcap; three buttons traditionally detached from the coat of William the Testy; and a pipe said to have gone out at the instant of the surrender.

Having thus secured the palladium of their nation, the settlers enacted wholesome laws against innovation. No English word was to be spoken within the bounds of Communipaw, except during thunderstorms, shipwrecks, or bargains with persons incapable of Dutch. No foreign garment was to be worn until thirty years after its introduction elsewhere. No chimney might be altered merely because it smoked; no chair discarded merely because it lacked a leg; no courtship concluded in fewer than seven winters; and no opinion adopted until it had been enjoyed by at least two generations.

These ordinances produced an admirable tranquillity. News from New-York was held to be contraband, and any traveller arriving from that quarter was detained at the landing until the dangerous novelty of his intelligence had evaporated. The great events of the world reached Communipaw softened by age. A European war was commonly first discussed after the peace; a revolution became credible when its leaders were dead; and a new fashion was never accepted until it had become ridiculous in every other country.

The calendar likewise remained pure. At Paas the children hunted coloured eggs under the fruit-trees; at Pinxter the negro fiddlers drew music from instruments of immemorial discord; at Christmas the hearths were garnished, the cupboards opened, and St. Nicholas expected with that confidence which belongs only to children, creditors and ancient communities. The good saint was regarded as special patron of the village, and his image, carved in wood, stood in the House of the Four Chimneys with one hand raised in benediction and the other holding what some antiquaries call a bishop’s crozier, though my aunt Hillegonda maintained it to be a sausage.

For a time all prospered. The orchards bore; the cows ruminated; the burghers increased in circumference; and marriages between Van Hornes, Van Pelts, Brinkerhoffs and Ten Broecks produced a population so thoroughly interrelated that no man could quarrel with his neighbour without offending a cousin.

But isolation, though favourable to purity, is hostile to commerce. The settlers would neither trade with New-York nor admit foreign merchants. Their wooden shoes wore out; their kettles grew thin; their last imported pipe-stems were smoked down to perilous brevity. Worse still, the soil, fatigued by generations of cabbages, began to yield heads scarcely larger than buttons. The oyster beds failed; the fish, offended perhaps by the solemn silence of the fishermen, removed to livelier waters.

Poverty entered Communipaw—not with a trumpet, as it visits cities, but softly, in patched breeches, diminished dinners and pewter spoons. Yet no one proposed intercourse with the English. The elders met daily at the House of the Four Chimneys, where they sat in a circle, smoking and smoking, considering and considering, while their wives at home practised a stercoraceous economy upon the exhausted gardens.

At length came a disastrous cleaning of the relic-room. Grietje Van Horne, a young housemaid of uncommon industry and no antiquarian sensibility, was directed to remove the dust, which had accumulated until several documents of state had become indistinguishable from grey cushions. In her zeal she cast Walter the Doubter’s hat upon a manure heap behind the mansion, and flung Peter Stuyvesant’s shoe toward the shore, supposing the one to be a ruined basket and the other a species of military boat.

That night was the eve of St. Nicholas. Snow lay upon the roofs; the bay shone beneath a frosty moon; and every honest inhabitant slept, except old Garret’s grandson, Baltus Van Horne, who remained awake from hunger and family anxiety. Near midnight he heard a trampling overhead, followed by a sound resembling a large person descending a narrow chimney in ceremonial robes.

Baltus arose, wrapped himself in a quilt, and entered the relic-room. There stood St. Nicholas, ruddy, grave and powdered with snow, examining the vacant shelves. The saint said nothing—for supernatural personages are sparing of words when dealing with local magistrates—but pointed first toward the manure heap, then toward the oyster shore, and finally laid one finger beside his nose, a gesture of profound mystic signification.

In the morning Baltus repaired to the garden. Upon the very spot where Walter’s hat had been thrown there rose a cabbage of such dimensions that three children might have played at blind-man’s-buff beneath its outer leaves. Its crown was broad, flat and venerable; its colour that peculiar green which painters reserve for ancient bronze and municipal furniture. When cut, it displayed within a succession of folds corresponding exactly, according to Baltus, with the doubts and circumlocutions of Walter’s administration.

The villagers gathered round in awe. The oldest men removed their caps; the women crossed their hands upon their aprons; the children were forbidden to touch it until the schoolmaster had composed an inscription. Thus appeared the first of that illustrious race of vegetables afterwards known as Governor’s Head cabbages.

But mark the consequence. When the tide withdrew that afternoon, there was discovered, upon the spot where Stuyvesant’s shoe had fallen, an oyster of enormous size, shaped like a martial foot and closed with a resolution which defied crowbar, knife and municipal argument. Baltus struck it three times with the wooden leg of an old militia stool, whereupon it opened and revealed meat sufficient for a family supper.

This was the first Governor’s Foot oyster; and though certain natural philosophers have attempted to explain it by currents, sediment and saline fructification, their theories are refuted by the circumstance that none of them was present.

These miracles were the salvation of Communipaw. Seed from the great cabbage produced a crop of heads so ample that a single cart carried but two, while a third was sometimes employed as the driver’s seat. The oysters multiplied with corresponding patriotism. They lay in the shallows like paving-stones, and a stranger once mistook an entire bed for the foundations of a new fort.

The inhabitants now possessed abundance, but abundance itself occasioned a constitutional difficulty. They could not consume all that Providence supplied. The cabbages crowded the gardens, rolled into the lanes, blocked cellar doors, and on windy nights were heard knocking against houses like green, homeless spirits. The oysters accumulated until navigation was threatened. It became necessary either to trade with New-York or permit Communipaw to be buried beneath its blessings.

After six months of deliberation, trade was authorised under severe restrictions. Selected burghers crossed the bay at dawn, sold their produce, and returned before sunset, lest they should become corrupted by street lamps, newspapers, theatres, English grammar, or other inventions of metropolitan depravity. They were forbidden to bring home any thing lighter than iron, newer than twenty years, or ornamental without a practical excuse.

The Governor’s Heads and Governor’s Feet soon acquired celebrity. New-York ate them greedily, without suspecting that every dinner increased the wealth of its ancient parent. Communipaw recovered. Its roofs were mended, its kettles renewed, and its pipes lengthened to a safe and dignified proportion. Yet the elders watched the young with unabated vigilance. No unmarried daughter might cross the bay unless attended by two aunts and a married cabbage dealer; no youth might remain in New-York after dark; and any one returning in a fashionable coat was required to hang it in the smoke-house until its insolence abated.

Thus matters continued through several generations. The English, after long contemplation, ceased to be the principal terror. Their language had infiltrated even the Van Horne nursery, though in a softened form; and their descendants had become almost tolerable by sitting still long enough to own property.

Their place was taken by a more restless race—the Yankees—who arrived from the east with measuring chains, pocket maps, improved clocks, sharp noses, and plans for making every spot unlike itself. These men transformed old New-York with alarming rapidity. They widened streets which had been narrow from principle, demolished houses that had offended no one but architects, erected banks where gardens had stood, and named new districts after trees they had cut down.

At last their eyes fell upon Communipaw.

One summer morning a schooner landed six speculators dressed in black, though the day was hot and their intentions hotter. Their leader, Mr. Zephaniah Quickset, announced that he represented the Communipaw Advancement, Improvement, Rectification and Profit Company. He praised the natural beauties of the village with such ardour that the inhabitants immediately suspected he meant to destroy them.

Quickset unfolded a coloured plan upon a barrel. The cabbage gardens were divided into building lots; the orchards were crossed by avenues bearing the names of directors; the oyster beds had become docks; the burial-ground was marked “Future Public Amusement”; and the House of the Four Chimneys, stripped of three chimneys and furnished with a portico, appeared as the First Progressive Bank of Communipaw.

He offered money—ready money, prospective money, paper money and money which would become money upon the completion of certain contingencies. He spoke of population, circulation, elevation, excavation and universal accommodation. Where the villagers saw apple-trees, he saw rental income; where they saw graves, he saw excellent drainage; where they saw the bay, he saw shares.

The Van Hornes listened in silence. Old Hendrick Van Horne sat upon the stoop with a pipe between his teeth, while his sons, nephews and cousins assembled behind him. When Quickset had exhausted improvement, Hendrick removed the pipe and asked one question:

“Where shall we sit?”

The speculator, unprepared for so fundamental an objection, replied that the company would construct a public promenade.

“Where shall we smoke?”

Quickset proposed a pavilion, subject to regulations.

“Where shall our fathers lie?”

Quickset pointed toward a projected omnibus road and began to explain the advantages of accessibility.

At this, the Van Hornes rose. No violence was committed; Communipaw has ever been governed by moderation. Nevertheless, the speculators found themselves conducted to their schooner with a firmness which shortened the interview. Their maps were folded about them; their measuring chains followed; and Mr. Quickset departed seated upon his own barrel, still declaring, as the vessel pushed off, that the village possessed incalculable possibilities.

Communipaw was saved once more. The gardens remained gardens; the oyster beds continued their useful obscurity; and the House of the Four Chimneys retained all four of its venerable columns of smoke. On summer evenings the old men still sat upon the stoops, the cats slept in the windows, the weathercocks deliberated upon the wind, and the bay reflected the red roofs as faithfully as if no company had ever been incorporated.

Yet, Sir, the antiquary must not permit affection to blind his eyes. During my last visit I observed symptoms which filled me with melancholy. One chimney of the great house had cracked; another leaned toward New-York with an inclination almost political. Moss obscured the date upon the gable. Rain entered the relic-room, and the wooden St. Nicholas had lost the hand which held the disputed sausage.

More grievous still, a young Van Horne wore narrow trousers and consulted a watch. Two maidens spoke of a concert in the city; a boy possessed a newspaper of the same morning; and I detected, behind the smoke-house, a small vehicle called a velocipede. The old Dutch clock in the hall still struck seven when it pleased, but no one waited for it. Even the cabbages had diminished, while the oysters, corrupted perhaps by commerce, no longer resembled any governor within authentic memory.

I stood before the House of the Four Chimneys at sunset. Its windows were red with the departing light; its bricks were worn; swallows entered where councillors had once deliberated; and from the kitchen came the reprehensible odour of coffee prepared by a modern machine. Across the water New-York lifted its towers, rattled its wheels, flashed its lamps, and stretched out piers like a many-fingered giant eager to seize the shore.

Whether Communipaw can long resist that embrace, I dare not determine. Nations, villages and breeches alike must in time alter their fashion. The Yankee may yet divide the orchards, number the cabbages, dredge the sacred oyster beds, and convert the old mansion into an institution having a president, a cashier and no chimney at all. The young will cross the bay; the old will cross a quieter water; and tradition, unless speedily written down, will be improved beyond recognition.

I therefore place these facts in your hands, Sir, that the memory of Communipaw may survive its possible advancement. May its four chimneys stand while one Van Horne remains to smoke beneath them; may its Governor’s Heads increase and multiply; may its Governor’s Feet never be trampled by speculation; and may St. Nicholas continue to defend that honest corner of New-Netherlands from all persons who would make it richer, straighter, newer and less worth remembering.

I remain, with antiquarian consideration and hereditary respect,

Your obedient servant,  
**HERMANUS VANDERDONK**

The Last Lightkeeper

By the time Mara Vale returned to Bellweather Island, the lighthouse had been dark for eleven years.

She arrived aboard the morning ferry with one suitcase, a canvas bag of groceries, and a brass key tied to a red thread. The ferry captain did not help her unload. He stood behind the wheelhouse glass, watching the island as if it might suddenly rise out of the sea and swallow them both.

“You staying long?” he called.

“I don’t know yet.”

The captain nodded in a way that suggested this was the wrong answer.

Bellweather was little more than a steep hill of black rock in the northern sea. A cluster of empty cottages leaned along the harbor road. Beyond them stood a chapel without a bell, an abandoned weather station, and the lighthouse on the western cliff.

Mara had been born in the keeper’s house beside the tower. She had left the island at twelve and had not returned in twenty-three years.

The ferry pulled away. Its engine faded into the wind, leaving Mara alone with the cries of gulls and the soft knock of loose boats against the pier.

She looked up at the lighthouse.

Even from the harbor, she could see the shattered windows of the lantern room. Rust streaked the white tower. The roof of the keeper’s house sagged in the middle, and thorn bushes had conquered the path.

Her father had died there six weeks earlier.

Not in the lighthouse, according to the official letter, but at his kitchen table, with a cold cup of tea beside his hand. The island’s postman, who came once a week from the mainland, had found him.

There had been no funeral. Elias Vale had left written instructions that his body be cremated and his ashes scattered from the western cliff. The postman and the ferry captain had done it together.

Mara had been sent the key.

She carried her suitcase up the hill.

The front door of the keeper’s house resisted her at first, swollen with damp. When it finally opened, the smell of old wood, salt, and extinguished fires met her like a memory.

Everything inside was smaller than she remembered.

Her father’s boots remained beside the stove. His gray coat hung from a hook. A chipped blue mug stood upside down on the draining board. The house felt neither occupied nor abandoned. It was paused, waiting for someone to return and finish a sentence.

Mara opened windows, swept the kitchen, and lit the stove. She told herself she had come only to settle the estate. The government owned the tower, but the house and its contents belonged to her. She would sort the papers, sell whatever could be sold, and leave before the first winter storms.

That afternoon, she entered the lighthouse.

The brass key turned smoothly in the lock.

Inside, the tower smelled of iron and wet stone. The spiral staircase climbed into darkness. Mara stood at the bottom, one hand on the cold railing, listening.

As a child, she had believed the lighthouse breathed. On windy nights, air moved through cracks in the masonry and produced a long, low sigh. Her father called it the tower’s voice.

Now she heard only water dripping somewhere above.

She climbed.

The light had been automated when Mara was seven. Five years later, after shipping routes changed, it was decommissioned. The great glass lens had been removed, along with the lamp and most of the machinery. Only a circular platform remained in the lantern room, surrounded by broken panes.

On the platform sat a wooden box.

Mara recognized it immediately. Her father had kept it locked in his desk when she was young. Once, when he caught her trying to open it with a hairpin, he had shouted so fiercely that she had run outside barefoot in the rain.

Now the box was open.

Inside lay twenty-seven notebooks, each bound in black cloth. The first was dated the year after Mara left the island. The final one bore the date of her father’s death.

She opened it.

Most pages contained weather records.

October 3. Wind northwest, force six. Rain before dawn.

October 4. Clear morning. High cloud by evening.

October 5. Light visible at 1:17 a.m. Three flashes, interval of seven seconds. No vessel observed.

Mara frowned and turned the page.

October 9. Light visible at 12:42 a.m. Two long flashes from south by southwest.

October 13. The light is nearer.

She sat on the edge of the platform, reading.

For years, her father had recorded a light at sea. It appeared on moonless nights, always southwest of the island, always beyond the reef. Sometimes it flashed once. Sometimes five times. Occasionally it remained steady for several minutes before vanishing.

At first, Elias had tried to explain it. Fishing boats. Weather buoys. Aircraft. Reflections from the mainland.

Then the entries changed.

It knows the old signal.

It answered tonight.

I should not have answered.

Mara closed the notebook.

Outside, the sun was sinking into a bank of purple cloud. She could see the reef below, a broken crescent of stone around the island’s western side. Her mother’s boat had struck that reef twenty-six years earlier.

There had been no storm that night.

Mara remembered standing at the kitchen window, watching her father light a lantern and climb the tower. The lighthouse lamp had failed, though no one could later explain why. Her mother, returning alone from the mainland, had missed the harbor channel by less than a hundred yards.

By sunrise, the boat was splintered. Her mother’s body was never found.

For five years afterward, Mara and her father had lived together in the keeper’s house, speaking less with each passing season. He became suspicious of machinery, then of ships, then of the sea itself. When Mara received a scholarship to a mainland school, she left without saying goodbye.

She carried the notebooks downstairs.

For the next three days, she worked from morning until night. She made lists of furniture, boxed clothes for charity, and burned spoiled papers in the stove. Yet each evening, she read more of her father’s journals.

He had filled thousands of pages.

Some described ordinary isolation: roofs repaired, birds counted, fevers endured alone. Others returned obsessively to the mysterious light.

In one notebook, Elias had copied passages from old lighthouse manuals. In another, he listed shipwrecks near Bellweather going back two centuries. Beside several names, he had drawn the same symbol: a circle crossed by three wavering lines.

Mara found that symbol carved into the underside of the kitchen table.

On the fourth evening, the weather turned.

Rain struck the house in hard, slanting sheets. The radio issued a gale warning, and the windows trembled in their frames. Mara ate soup beside the stove and tried not to look toward the sea.

At eleven, the power failed.

She lit two candles and placed them on the table.

The storm intensified. Wind moved around the house with the howl she remembered from childhood. Above it came another sound: a deep metallic groan from the lighthouse.

Mara opened the final notebook again.

The last written page was not a weather record.

Mara, if you return, do not light the tower.

Below the sentence, her father’s hand had drawn the circle and the three wavering lines.

A flash filled the kitchen.

Mara looked toward the window.

For an instant, she thought it was lightning. Then it came again, low over the southwest horizon: a pale beam sweeping through the rain.

One flash.

Darkness.

A second flash, longer than the first.

Mara rose so quickly that her chair fell backward.

No ship should have been near the reef in such weather. She reached for the radio, forgetting that the power was out. Her mobile phone showed no signal.

The light flashed again.

This time it remained visible for seven seconds.

Through the rain-streaked glass, Mara saw something beneath it—a black shape on the water, too broad and still to be a fishing vessel.

Then darkness returned.

Mara pulled on her father’s coat and ran outside.

Rain blinded her. She crossed the yard with one hand against the lighthouse wall until she found the door. Inside, the tower groaned and whispered.

She climbed by candlelight, shielding the flame with her palm.

Halfway up, she heard footsteps above.

Mara stopped.

The sound circled down the spiral stair: slow, heavy steps descending toward her.

“Who’s there?”

The footsteps continued.

She raised the candle. Shadows twisted across the stone wall. No one appeared.

The sound passed her.

For one impossible moment, she felt a rush of cold air and smelled tobacco, engine oil, and the pine soap her father had used all his life.

Then the footsteps faded below.

Her candle went out.

Mara stood in darkness, gripping the railing.

A voice rose through the tower.

Not words. A tune.

Her mother used to hum while cleaning fish at the kitchen sink. A simple melody of six notes, repeated until Mara joined in.

Now someone was humming it below her.

“Mum?” Mara whispered.

The humming stopped.

A door slammed at the base of the tower.

Mara forced herself upward.

When she reached the lantern room, rain poured through the broken glass. The wooden box had overturned, scattering journals across the floor. The storm tore pages from them and pressed the pages against the windows like pale hands.

Beyond the reef, the light flashed.

Three short.

Three long.

Three short.

An old distress signal.

Mara searched the empty room. There was no lamp, no lens, nothing that could guide a vessel. Only the circular platform and, at its center, a rusted metal housing where the lighthouse mechanism had once turned.

Another flash swept the room.

For a fraction of a second, Mara saw a woman standing on the outer gallery.

She wore a yellow raincoat. Her dark hair streamed behind her. One hand rested against the glass.

Then she was gone.

Mara stumbled to the gallery door and dragged it open.

Wind nearly tore it from her hands. She stepped outside, clinging to the rail.

“Mum!”

The sea answered with thunder against the cliffs.

The distant light flashed again, illuminating the black shape beneath it. Mara saw masts—three of them—though no vessel with such masts had sailed these waters for a hundred years. Torn sails hung without moving in the gale.

Figures stood along the deck.

Dozens of them.

At the bow was the woman in the yellow coat.

Mara’s mother raised one arm and pointed toward the island.

Not toward Mara.

Toward the dark lighthouse.

Behind her, something vast shifted beneath the ship.

The water rose in a smooth black curve, like the back of an animal, but it did not break. The ship tilted. The figures on deck opened their mouths together.

The wind carried a single word.

“Light.”

Mara ran downstairs.

In the keeper’s house, she tore through cupboards and drawers until she found an oil lantern. It was the same lantern her father had carried up the tower on the night of the wreck. Its brass was green with age, but fuel still moved in the reservoir.

She struck a match.

Her father’s warning lay open on the table.

Do not light the tower.

Mara held the flame above the wick.

All her life, she had believed he had failed to save her mother. Later, she had believed something worse: that in his bitterness or madness, he had allowed the lighthouse to go dark.

But the notebooks told another story.

He had seen the light before the wreck. He had answered it. And something had come to the reef.

Mara lowered the match.

The wick caught.

At once, three knocks sounded at the kitchen door.

She did not move.

Three more.

“Mara,” called her mother’s voice.

It was exactly as she remembered it—warm, impatient, slightly amused.

“Mara, darling, open the door.”

Tears filled Mara’s eyes.

She stepped toward the sound.

The door shook in its frame.

“I’ve been waiting,” the voice said. “Your father kept me out.”

Mara reached for the handle.

Then she noticed the water.

It seeped beneath the door in thin black streams. Tiny shells clicked across the floorboards. The room filled with the smell of deep sea mud.

“Mara.”

Her mother had never called her darling. She had called her starling, because Mara had talked endlessly as a child, mimicking every sound she heard.

The thing outside knocked again.

“Open.”

Mara backed away.

The voice changed.

Her father said, “I’m sorry.”

Then the ferry captain: “Help me.”

Then a child began to cry.

Mara took the lantern and ran for the tower.

The door behind her burst open as she crossed the yard.

She did not look back.

Inside the lighthouse, the air was full of voices. They rose and fell around the stairwell—sailors shouting orders, women praying, children calling for parents. Water streamed down the steps, though the roof was far above.

Mara climbed.

Hands seemed to brush her coat. Once, something seized her ankle, but she kicked free without looking down. She kept her eyes on the circle of darkness above.

At the lantern room, she placed the oil lamp on the central platform.

Its little flame trembled.

It was not enough. A ship beyond the reef would never see it.

Another notebook lay open on the floor. Rain had soaked the pages, but one diagram remained visible: the old lens assembly, with lines drawn from the lamp through the curved prisms.

The lens was gone.

Yet around the lantern room, broken windowpanes caught the flame and divided it into faint golden fragments.

Mara gathered the loose glass.

She cut her fingers lifting the largest pieces, but she barely felt it. She arranged them around the lamp, leaning one shard against another. The flame multiplied. Ten lights became twenty, then fifty, scattered across the walls.

Beyond the gallery, the ghostly ship turned toward the island.

Mara understood.

It did not want rescue.

It wanted a beacon.

The black shape beneath the vessel rose higher. Water poured from a surface smooth as stone. An eye opened—a white oval larger than the keeper’s house.

The voices below began to laugh.

Mara stared at the lamp.

If she extinguished it, perhaps the ship would turn away. But the woman at the bow was watching her.

Not the thing wearing her mother’s face at the kitchen door. Her mother.

Mara knew her by the way she stood: one shoulder slightly lower than the other, chin raised against the rain.

The woman did not call for light now.

She shook her head.

Then she lifted both hands and made the signal she had used when Mara was small and wandering too near the cliff.

Go back.

Mara reached for the lantern.

Before she could touch it, the tower lurched.

Glass slid across the floor. The lamp tipped, spilling burning oil onto the wooden platform. Flame raced in a bright circle around her.

The black eye beyond the reef closed.

The creature moved toward shore.

Mara tore off her father’s coat and beat at the fire. The old wool smothered one side, but flames spread to the scattered notebooks. Twenty-seven years of her father’s words curled and blackened.

The tower filled with fierce orange light.

The ship advanced.

Mara saw the trap at last. Her father had not preserved the journals to explain himself. He had brought them to the lantern room because paper burns.

He had been preparing to destroy the tower.

Mara grabbed an armful of flaming notebooks and threw them down the stairwell.

Fire tumbled through the dark.

The voices below screamed.

She swept more burning pages after them. Flames caught on the dry wooden steps, then climbed the walls where old paint blistered. Smoke filled the room.

The lighthouse beam grew brighter, but not outward. The broken glass turned it back upon itself, folding the light inward until the lantern room blazed like a furnace.

The ghost ship stopped.

The creature beneath it struck the reef.

Stone cracked with a sound louder than thunder.

Mara ran to the gallery. There was no way down; fire roared in the stairwell. The western cliff lay directly below, and the sea boiled against it.

Through the smoke, her mother stood on the ship’s bow.

For the first time in twenty-six years, Mara saw her smile.

Then the woman began to hum six familiar notes.

Mara answered.

The melody passed between them as the lighthouse burned.

One by one, the figures aboard the ship vanished. The vessel became transparent, its masts dissolving into rain. Last of all, Mara’s mother faded, still smiling, until only the yellow coat remained, bright as a scrap of sunrise.

The creature opened its eye again.

The concentrated light struck it.

The sea erupted.

A wave rose over the cliff, taller than the tower. Within it, Mara glimpsed wrecks from every age: wooden hulls, iron anchors, snapped propellers, pale faces behind glass. The wave hung for a breath, impossible and silent.

Then it fell.

Mara jumped.

Cold erased the world.

She dreamed of descending through dark water while a light turned far above her. Her father swam past, young and laughing. Her mother followed in the yellow coat. Between them they carried a little girl, and the girl would not stop talking.

When Mara opened her eyes, she was lying on the harbor road.

Morning sunlight warmed her face.

The ferry captain knelt beside her, shouting into a radio. Behind him, villagers from the mainland moved between the cottages. Someone wrapped Mara in a blanket.

She tried to sit up.

The western cliff was empty.

The lighthouse had fallen into the sea.

Only part of the keeper’s house remained, its front wall torn away. The kitchen table stood exposed to the sky.

“What happened?” the captain asked.

Mara looked toward the reef.

The storm had broken it open. A wide channel now ran through the black stones, straight and deep, offering safe passage into the harbor.

“I turned off the light,” she said.

The captain glanced at the empty cliff but did not question her.

Months later, Mara returned to Bellweather.

She rebuilt the keeper’s house, though there was no lighthouse left to keep. She painted the walls, repaired the roof, and planted grass over the place where the tower had stood. Above the new harbor channel, the authorities installed an ordinary electric beacon on a steel post.

Its signal was simple and constant. Nothing answered from the southwest.

Mara stayed through winter.

On calm evenings, she sat outside with a blue mug and watched fishing boats use the new channel. Sometimes the sailors waved. Sometimes they sounded their horns.

She kept no weather records.

But on the anniversary of the wreck, she woke before dawn and heard someone humming beyond the open window.

Six notes, soft and clear.

Mara went down to the shore.

The sea was smooth. At the edge of the water lay a brass key tied to a faded red thread. Beside it, untouched by salt, was a small piece of yellow cloth.

She picked them up.

Far out beyond the broken reef, the horizon brightened.

For one moment, just before sunrise, Mara saw two figures walking together across the water. One wore a gray coat, the other a yellow one. Neither looked back.

Then morning came, and they were gone.

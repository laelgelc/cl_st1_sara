# The Sons Who Would Not Leave

There are deeds of courage which win kingdoms, and deeds of courage which save armies; yet there are others, performed without hope of reward and sometimes without hope even of success, which touch the heart more deeply than the proudest victory. Such deeds are not always done beneath waving banners, nor witnessed by applauding multitudes. Often they are wrought in smoke and darkness, beside a fallen man, when the shouting hosts have swept onward and only a son remains faithful to his father.

Let us remember three such sons. They lived in distant ages, spoke different tongues, and served beneath standards that were sometimes opposed to those which we ourselves have learned to honor. One was a Roman youth upon an Italian field; one an English nobleman amid the sorrows of civil war; and one a French child on the burning deck of a doomed ship. But in each there burned the same pure flame of filial devotion; and, whether the father was saved or lost, that flame made the act a Golden Deed.

## I. The Young Roman at Ticinus

More than two hundred years before the birth of our Lord, Rome, still a republic, stood in peril before the wonderful genius of Hannibal. That great Carthaginian commander, having sworn in childhood to be the enemy of Rome, led his army from Spain, crossed the Pyrenees, passed through hostile tribes, and came at last to the Alps. Snow covered the frightful heights; precipices opened beneath the feet of the soldiers; men and horses fell by hundreds; and the elephants, strange monsters in the eyes of the mountain people, struggled painfully through ice and broken rock. Yet Hannibal descended into Italy.

The Roman consul Publius Cornelius Scipio had hastened northward to oppose him. Scipio was brave, honorable, and experienced, but he had not yet learned what terrible skill directed the army of Carthage. Near the river Ticinus, in the year 218 before Christ, the hostile forces met.

With the consul rode his son, a youth of only seventeen years. He bore the same name as his father, and was afterwards to become the renowned Scipio Africanus, the conqueror of Hannibal. But no one upon that autumn day could foresee the glory awaiting him. To the veteran soldiers he was merely the consul’s young son—high-born, eager, perhaps untried. His helmet and shield were new; his face had scarcely lost the softness of boyhood; and there must have been many a scarred legionary who thought that such a stripling had better have remained safely in Rome.

The battle was fought chiefly by cavalry. Hannibal sent forward the fierce horsemen of Numidia, who rode without saddles, guided their active steeds almost by a touch, and circled round their enemies like birds of prey. The Roman cavalry met them gallantly. The ground shook beneath the rushing squadrons; spears splintered upon shields; swords flashed above the struggling mass of men and horses; and clouds of dust rolled over the field.

At first the Roman line held firm. Then the Numidians swept round upon the rear. Confusion followed. Horsemen, pressed together and attacked from every side, could neither advance nor withdraw in order. The Roman infantry had not yet formed so as to restore the battle, and what had begun as a combat became a rout.

The consul Scipio fought in the thickest of the danger. A blow struck him down; his horse was killed or borne away in the tumult; and he lay wounded upon the ground while the Roman soldiers were driven backward. Around him rushed the victorious enemy. Dust dimmed the declining light, and through it came the cries of fugitives, the clash of weapons, and the terrible trampling of horses.

Who, in such an hour, could have been blamed for seeking safety? The Roman army was collapsing. The standards were retreating. Even brave men were carried away by the flood of fear.

But the seventeen-year-old son saw his father fall.

He did not look behind him to learn whether others followed. He did not pause to measure the number of the enemy, nor consider that his own young life might one day be precious to Rome. At that moment he was not the future general, not the descendant of a noble house, not even a soldier thinking of victory or defeat. He was a son, and his father lay helpless among hostile swords.

Turning his horse, he forced his way against the stream of fugitives. A companion called to him to return; but the young Scipio rode on. He struck aside one assailant, broke through another group, and reached the consul. The enemy closed around them. The boy sprang down, shielded his wounded father, and by courage so sudden and determined checked for a moment those who expected no resistance.

Other Romans, shamed or inspired by the sight, rallied to him. Together they lifted the consul and bore him from the field. Some accounts tell us that the son placed his father upon his own horse; others that he supported and carried him through the press. It matters little which hand held the rein or upon whose shoulder the wounded man leaned. The glory rests in the instant choice—the turning back when all others fled, the placing of a father’s life before his own.

They reached the Roman camp. There, behind the guarded lines, the consul’s wounds were dressed, and he lived to serve his country again. Rome afterwards offered a civic crown to the youth, that noble wreath bestowed upon one who had saved the life of a Roman citizen. He refused it, saying that the deed carried its own reward.

How worthy was that answer! A service performed for honor may be great; a service performed for love alone is greater. The youth who refused the wreath went on to gather laurels such as few commanders have worn. He conquered in Spain; he carried the war into Africa; and at Zama he overthrew Hannibal himself. Yet among all his victories, none shines with a purer light than that first brave act beside the Ticinus, when, at seventeen years of age, he rode back through a flying army to save his father.

## II. The Earl and His Son at Edgehill

Let us pass now over nearly eighteen centuries, from republican Rome to our own England; from the dust of an Italian field to the cold darkness of an October night in Warwickshire.

The great Civil War had begun. King Charles the First and Parliament had drawn the sword against each other, and Englishmen stood in arms against Englishmen. It was a miserable conflict, for courage, conscience, and honest purpose were not found wholly upon one side. Families were divided; old friends met as enemies; the same village bell that summoned one man to the King’s standard called his neighbor to the ranks of Parliament. We may honor fidelity wherever it was sincere, while still grieving over the pride, distrust, and violence that brought so much sorrow upon the land.

Among the King’s loyal servants was Robert Bertie, Earl of Lindsay. He was no rash courtier who thought war a splendid pageant. He had seen hard service abroad, had fought under famous captains, and understood the stern discipline by which troops must be kept together. Now past sixty years of age, grave, experienced, and resolute, he was appointed Lord General of the Royal army.

But with the King was his nephew, Prince Rupert of the Rhine, young, fiery, and already celebrated for daring. Rupert understood cavalry and possessed that swift courage which can turn an opening into victory. Yet he was impatient of caution, contemptuous of restraint, and too ready to believe that one magnificent charge could decide every contest.

On the morning of Sunday, the 23rd of October, 1642, the two armies faced each other near Edgehill. The Parliamentary forces, commanded by the Earl of Essex, were drawn up upon the plain. The Royalists descended from the heights, their standards fluttering in the chill wind. Autumn lay upon the fields; hedges were thinning; the ground was damp; and over all hung the solemn knowledge that English blood would soon be shed by English hands.

Lindsay advised that the army should be formed according to the tried order of battle which he had learned in the wars of the Low Countries. Rupert and those around him preferred another disposition, swifter and better fitted, as they thought, to the prince’s bold designs. The King rejected the counsel of his Lord General.

Lindsay did not answer with angry reproaches. He had been entrusted with command, and he knew that command without authority was an empty name. If his judgment was not to govern the battle, another must bear the responsibility.

“I will never,” he is said to have declared, “fight under the command of a boy.”

Then he resigned his office.

Yet mark well what followed. Lindsay did not resign his loyalty. He did not leave the field because his advice had been refused. He put aside the staff of supreme command and went to his own regiment, the foot soldiers of Lincolnshire, among whom his name and face were known. Taking a pike in his hand, the white-haired nobleman placed himself at their head as a private captain.

“If I am not fit to be your general,” his conduct seemed to say, “I am still fit to fight beside you.”

With him went his eldest son, Lord Willoughby, a man in the strength of life. The son knew his father’s indignation; he knew also the danger to which the old earl now exposed himself. But there was no argument between them. Where Lindsay stood, Willoughby would stand.

The battle opened with cannon and musketry. Then Rupert’s horsemen charged with tremendous force. They broke the Parliamentary cavalry before them and swept it from the field. But victory intoxicated the pursuers. Instead of halting, reforming, and returning to strike the enemy’s infantry, they galloped onward after fugitives and plunder. Thus the center of the Royal army was left to bear the sternest part of the struggle without the help it needed.

There, pike met pike. The long shafts crossed and thrust; musketeers fired so near that powder scorched faces and clothing; flags rose and sank above the smoke. Lindsay’s regiment advanced with him on foot at its head. Though age had whitened his hair, it had not weakened his spirit. He fought with the steadfast courage of one who had chosen his duty and would not retreat from it.

But the Royal foot were pressed heavily. Men fell around the earl. A musket-ball struck him and shattered the bone of his thigh. He sank upon the ground, unable to rise.

Willoughby saw him fall.

Once more, as long before beside the Ticinus, an army was giving way and a wounded father lay in the path of victorious enemies. Once more a son forgot everything except the sacred claim before him.

Willoughby fought toward the spot. Parliamentary soldiers surrounded the fallen earl. The son could not rescue him by force; no band of loyal comrades remained near enough to carry him away. He had one choice by which he might save himself, and another by which he might remain with his father.

He threw down his weapon and surrendered.

“I am Lord Willoughby,” he said, “and I yield myself, if you will permit me to attend my father.”

His rank made him a valuable prisoner. He might be confined, exchanged, or held for ransom; but he cared for none of these things. He asked only to stay beside the wounded man.

The fury of battle gradually rolled away. Darkness came early, as it does in late October. Across the fields moved lanterns in the hands of those seeking the wounded; here a cry answered them, and there silence told its more dreadful tale. Fires glowed red where soldiers gathered after the long struggle, but many lay beyond their warmth. The wind sharpened, and the cold crept through blood-soaked garments.

Lindsay was carried to a poor shed or barn near the field. No proper surgeon was immediately at hand. The wound was grievous, and he had lost much blood. Upon some scattered straw the old earl lay through the freezing night, while his son remained beside him as a prisoner.

Imagine that vigil. The proud Lord General, who in the morning had ridden before an army, now lay helpless in a rough shelter. The shouts of battle had ceased, but from the darkness came the groans of wounded men and the confused movements of troops uncertain whether they had won or lost. There was little light, little warmth, and no comfort except the presence of the son who had freely given up liberty rather than leave his father alone.

Perhaps Lindsay thought then of the counsel that had been rejected; but if so, no ungenerous triumph escaped him. The day had justified his fears. Rupert’s brilliant charge had scattered the enemy’s horse, yet his wild pursuit had robbed the King of complete victory. The Royal army remained upon the field, but the Parliamentary army was not destroyed. The long war, with its sieges, executions, bereavements, and final tragedy, still lay ahead.

For Lindsay, however, earthly warfare was nearly ended.

Willoughby tried to warm him, supported his head, and spoke to him through the bitter hours. The father knew that his son had become a captive for his sake. Could any triumph have been dearer to that loyal old heart? He had lost command; his body was broken; his cause stood uncertain; but beside him knelt a son who would not forsake him.

When morning came, help came too late. The loss of blood had done its work, and the Earl of Lindsay died.

Willoughby had not saved him, as young Scipio saved the Roman consul. Yet shall we therefore call his devotion less complete? Surely not. We are not masters of the result. Duty belongs to us; success belongs to God. The truest loyalty is not that which remains only while it can conquer death, but that which watches when hope is gone, endures the cold night, and counts captivity a small price for the privilege of holding a dying father’s hand.

## III. The Boy upon *L’Orient*

Our last scene is neither upon a dusty plain nor beneath an English autumn sky. It is upon the blue waters of an Egyptian bay, where the evening sun shone over two mighty fleets and where, before midnight, flame made the sea brighter than noon.

In the year 1798, Napoleon Bonaparte led a French expedition to Egypt. His army escaped the watchful British ships, landed near Alexandria, and defeated the Mamelukes in the Battle of the Pyramids. But the French fleet, upon which the safety of the expedition depended, lay anchored in Aboukir Bay, commonly known to us through the glorious name of the Battle of the Nile.

The French ships formed a line near the shore, their broadsides facing seaward. The greatest among them was the enormous flagship *L’Orient*, carrying one hundred and twenty guns. Her towering sides, crowded decks, and heavy batteries made her appear almost a floating fortress. Admiral Brueys commanded the fleet from her quarterdeck.

For weeks Admiral Horatio Nelson had searched the Mediterranean for the French. Storm had damaged his vessels; imperfect information had misled him; and again and again the enemy seemed to vanish before him. Yet Nelson did not despair. His body had already been marked by war: he had lost an eye and an arm in his country’s service. His slight figure appeared frail beside many of the officers around him, but within it dwelt a resolution which danger only strengthened.

On the afternoon of the 1st of August, the masthead lookouts reported ships in Aboukir Bay.

There lay the French fleet at last.

Some commanders might have waited for morning. The coast was difficult, the shoals imperfectly known, and daylight was already declining. An officer spoke of what might happen if the ships could not enter safely before darkness.

“There is no if in the case,” Nelson answered.

Those few words reveal the man. He had found the enemy; he would attack.

The British ships bore down in line, their sails bright beneath the lowering sun and the white bands upon their dark hulls showing clear across the water. The French believed that the shoal between their anchored vessels and the shore would protect them on one side. But Captain Foley, leading in the *Goliath*, perceived that where a French ship had swung at anchor there must be water enough for a British ship to pass. He steered boldly inside the line. Other vessels followed, while Nelson in the *Vanguard* attacked from seaward.

Thus the French van was caught between two fires.

At half past six the cannonade began. It was no distant exchange, but a close and dreadful struggle. Gun answered gun until separate reports became one continuous thunder. Smoke spread over the bay, hiding ships from one another; through it flashed the red mouths of cannon. Masts crashed; ropes whipped loose; splinters flew like spears; and the decks, washed again and again, were stained anew.

On board *L’Orient* was the young son of Captain Luc-Julien-Joseph Casabianca. The child was only ten years old. Some boys at that age have scarcely spent a night away from home; this boy stood amid the terrible machinery of a line-of-battle ship, with hundreds of men around him and death coming through the wooden walls at every broadside.

His father had given him a post and told him to remain there until ordered away. It was natural discipline upon a ship in battle. Each person must stay where duty places him, for confusion can be as deadly as the enemy. The boy obeyed.

Captain Casabianca was soon grievously wounded. Amid smoke, darkness, shattered timber, and rushing men, the knowledge of his condition did not at once reach his son. The child remained at the appointed place.

As the battle deepened into night, fire broke out aboard the French flagship. At first it may have seemed no more than one blaze among many, for burning wads, overturned lanterns, and sparks from the guns had kindled small fires upon several vessels. But this one spread. It climbed through broken woodwork, seized upon ropes and canvas, and ran upward with dreadful speed.

Men left their guns to fight it. Buckets were passed; water was thrown into the flames; officers shouted commands that could scarcely be heard. But the rigging burned beyond reach, and blazing fragments fell upon the decks. The fire advanced toward stores which no fire must ever touch—the powder magazines deep within the ship.

Someone saw the boy and urged him to leave.

“My father has not ordered me,” he answered.

They told him that the ship was burning.

“My father will call me if I must go.”

There was in this no foolish love of danger. The child believed his father still commanded somewhere amid the confusion. The order to remain had been clear; no order to depart had reached him. Obedience, which is easy in the quiet room and under a kindly eye, held him fast amid fire and cannon.

The flames rose higher. Their light burst through the smoke and threw a quivering glare across the bay, more intense than daylight. The French tricolor could be seen against the burning rigging. The faces of men on nearby ships shone red as they paused in horror. Even enemies watched with pity, knowing what must follow.

Again sailors called to the child.

“Come! There is still time!”

“Not without my father,” he replied.

Then at last he left his post—not to save himself, but to seek the wounded man whose silence he could no longer understand. He ran through smoke that stung his eyes and scorched his breath. The deck sloped beneath wreckage; dead and wounded lay where the battle had struck them; men rushed toward boats or leaped into the sea. Everywhere was uproar, yet through it the boy called:

“Father!”

No answer came.

“Father! May I leave?”

The guns continued firing of themselves as the heat touched their charges. Flame streamed from hatchways. The great ship, which at sunset had seemed unconquerable, had become a furnace afloat.

At length the boy found Captain Casabianca, wounded and unable to escape by his own strength. Whether the father was conscious enough to recognize him we cannot know. The son did not abandon him. Seeing that the boats were gone or overcrowded, he sought another means of rescue. He obtained a spar—a piece of timber torn from the ship—and with a rope attempted to fasten his father to it. If they could reach the water, perhaps the wood would support the wounded man; perhaps the boy, young though he was, could swim beside him until a boat came.

What hope was there? Very little. The sea around *L’Orient* was churned by shot and filled with wreckage; burning fragments fell upon it; boats already struggled beneath too many men. Yet love does not always ask whether hope is great. It asks only, “What can I do?”

The child labored at the rope. His small hands, blackened by smoke and perhaps bleeding from splintered wood, pulled it around his father and the spar. A sailor called once more, offering rescue to the boy alone.

He refused.

He would not leave his father.

Shortly after ten o’clock the fire reached the powder.

Those who witnessed the explosion never forgot it. For one instant *L’Orient* was a mountain of flame. Then came a burst so tremendous that the battle itself seemed silenced beneath it. The whole sky opened in fire; burning timbers, guns, bodies, and fragments of mast were hurled high into the darkness; the sea shook; and ships far away felt the concussion. The sound rolled across the Egyptian shore and was heard at Alexandria.

Then followed a silence more awful than the thunder.

For several minutes the guns of both fleets ceased. British and French alike stood stunned, gazing at the place where the great flagship had been. Upon the black water fell burning wreckage with hiss after hiss. The stars reappeared above the drifting smoke.

Some believed that the boy and his father had reached the sea before the explosion; others thought they were thrown into it when the ship broke apart. Amid the confusion, men glimpsed forms clinging to wreckage and swimmers struggling in the fiery water. But Captain Casabianca and his ten-year-old son were never brought alive from the bay. They disappeared together among the ruins of *L’Orient*.

The battle began again after that solemn pause. By morning the French fleet was destroyed or captured, except for a few vessels which escaped. It was one of the greatest triumphs in the history of the British Navy. Nelson’s genius, the skill of his captains, and the steadfastness of his sailors had won a victory whose influence reached far beyond Egypt. Let us honor them. Let us remember the wounded admiral continuing to direct the fight; the gun crews serving amid splinters and blood; the seamen who risked their own lives to rescue enemies from burning ships.

Yet victory need not make the heart hard. The flag beneath which a noble deed is performed may be hostile to our own, but courage is not therefore changed into cowardice, nor love into something unworthy of reverence. The French boy was an enemy only by the accident of war. In all that belongs to obedience, devotion, and a faithful heart, he is the brother of every brave child in every land.

Scipio rode back and saved his father. Willoughby surrendered and watched beside his father until death. Casabianca remained amid the flames, and when obedience could no longer keep him at his post, love sent him in search of the wounded man. The Roman lived to win immortal victories; the Englishman lived to preserve the memory of a sad and loyal night; the French child passed away before life had properly begun. But all three had learned the same high lesson—that affection proves itself not by words spoken in safety, but by the sacrifice it is ready to make in danger.

Ask not only of armies who conquered, nor of kings what lands they gained. Ask also of the darkened battlefield who turned back; ask of the winter shed who remained beside the dying; ask of the winds that swept Aboukir Bay who stood upon the burning deck when escape was offered.

They will answer with the names of sons who would not leave their fathers.

And longest, perhaps, through the thunder and the flame, we shall hear of the youngest—the child among warriors, the small brave figure beneath the blazing masts, the young faithful heart.

The young faithful heart.

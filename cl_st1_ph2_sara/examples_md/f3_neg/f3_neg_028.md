# THE GUESTS OF GIBBET ISLAND  
### A Communipaw Tradition

**To the Editor of the Knickerbocker Cabinet of Historical Curiosities.**

Sir,

In turning over certain papers lately purchased, by public subscription, from the descendants of Alderman Stoffel Brinkerhoff—who, as every person conversant with our municipal antiquities must know, was once Inspector of Chimneys, Keeper of Stray Swine, and Deputy Surveyor of Sunken Lots—I came upon the following tradition concerning the ancient settlement of Communipaw.

The manuscript was written in three several inks, upon the backs of tavern accounts, tide tables, and a proclamation against the firing of guns on New Year’s Day. It has unfortunately suffered from mice, mildew, and the historical corrections of a young gentleman who appears to have improved the orthography with a red pencil. Where the authority failed, I have supplied the deficiency from the recollections of aged inhabitants, each of whom remembered the matter with perfect distinctness, though no two agreed upon any particular.

The story has been called fabulous. But what, sir, is a fable? Is not history itself a well-dressed rumor, admitted into respectable society under the protection of dates? The learned Professor Van Zandt, in his *Remarks upon the Probable Situation of Things No Longer Existing*, declares that a tavern once stood upon the spot hereinafter described; while the late harbor-master, Captain Jacobus Kip, testified that he had heard, in his boyhood, an old woman assert that her grandfather had known a man who could point out the very place. Nothing, to my mind, can be more conclusive.

I therefore send you the narrative, not as an article of faith, but as a small contribution to that mass of doubtful knowledge upon which our local greatness so securely reposes. Should any critic demand proof, refer him to the archives. Should the archives contradict the tale, so much the worse for the archives.

Your obedient servant,  
**Diedrich Knickerbant, Corresponding Member**  
of the Communipaw Society for the Preservation of Obsolete Facts.

In the golden age of Communipaw—before steam had frightened the fish from the bay, before wharves had elbowed the marshes into the water, and before every rising ground was shaved flat in the name of improvement—there stood, close beside the landing, a low-browed Dutch tavern, yclept **The Wild Goose**.

It was a broad, comfortable building, with a roof that descended almost to the ears of the lower windows, as though pulled down snugly against the north wind. Its bricks had been brought from Holland, its beams had seasoned in three generations of smoke, and its huge chimney, bestraddling the ridge like a burgomaster upon a bench, sent up a blue column visible from every fishing boat in the Kills. Before the door swung a painted sign representing a goose of uncommon ferocity flying through a sky of Prussian blue. Time had worn away one wing and the greater portion of the bill, so that the bird appeared less a goose than a discontented cloud; but the signpost remained tall, crooked, and cross-armed, and certain imaginative persons afterwards remarked that it bore a disagreeable resemblance to a gallows.

The landlord was Teunis Van Gieson, a round, rosy, peaceable man, who had attained that perfection of temper which comes of believing every dispute capable of settlement by a fresh pipe and another mug. He was slow of speech, liberal of measure, and so unwilling to think ill of any man that rogues found his good opinion almost embarrassing. His wife having died early, and his only brother having followed her, Teunis took into his house the orphan boy, Yan Yost Vanderscamp.

Here charity made one of those blunders for which charity is seldom prosecuted.

Yan Yost was, from his earliest breeches, a restless, hard-headed, hard-fisted varlet. He robbed birds’ nests before the birds had finished building them; untied cattle from their stakes; exchanged the dominie’s spectacles for two rounds of smoked glass; and once introduced a live eel into the pocket of the village constable during divine service. No gate could keep him in, no cupboard keep him out, and no lie, however broad, found him without a second ready to support it. The neighbors assured Teunis that the boy would come to the gallows. Teunis replied that boys would be boys, though it was privately observed that no other boy in Communipaw had ever attempted to sail to Staten Island in the dominie’s pig trough.

The indulgent uncle forgave all. When Yan stole apples, Teunis blamed his appetite; when he broke windows, Teunis blamed the wind; when he fought the schoolmaster, Teunis blamed the decline of education. Thus the lad grew like a neglected thorn hedge—sturdy, crooked, and offensive to all who passed near him.

At this period there occurred an event upon which the learned have expended much conjecture and very little light.

One September morning, after a night of violent rain, the fishermen discovered a broken longboat among the reeds below the tavern. A man lay senseless across the thwarts. He was of powerful frame, though gaunt from exposure; his garments were salt-stiffened and foreign in cut; and about his neck hung a little black stone carved in the likeness of a three-headed beast. When revived, he gave his name as Pluto.

Whence he came no one could learn. He spoke Dutch badly, English worse, and several other tongues with a fluency which served only to confuse inquiry. Some said he had escaped from a Spanish vessel; some, that he had been a cook aboard a Guinea trader; some, that he was a pilot from the islands; while old Cobus Doremus, who had read half a book upon classical antiquity, decided that any man named Pluto must necessarily come from the lower regions.

Teunis, having a spare bed and no suspicion, sheltered the castaway. Pluto repaid him by making himself useful. He mended nets, caulked boats, cured fevers with bitter leaves, could tell the approach of weather by the smell of the mud, and handled an oar as if born with one in his hands. He was silent among the burghers, but with Yan Yost he held long conversations in corners, upon the shore, or in the loft above the stable.

No one knew what passed between them. This was sufficient to make everyone certain it was wicked.

From that time Yan’s pranks took a more maritime and enterprising turn. Boats disappeared from their moorings and returned at dawn with strange mud upon their keels. Nets were cut; cargoes were lightened; a tax collector, crossing the bay, was set ashore upon a sandbar with his account books tied around his neck. Yan and Pluto were repeatedly seen together, Pluto rowing and Yan standing in the bow with a red handkerchief about his head, issuing commands to imaginary fleets.

Teunis remonstrated. Yan laughed. The dominie preached upon the Fifth Commandment. Yan observed that uncles were not mentioned in it. The constable threatened the stocks. Pluto looked at the constable until that officer remembered pressing business elsewhere.

At length there came a night when the wind blew fresh from the south and a low, black vessel anchored far out in the bay, showing no light. In the morning Yan Yost and Pluto were gone. So also were Teunis’s best skiff, two silver spoons, a keg of Hollands, three blankets, a pistol, and the painted goose from the tavern sign.

The village drew its conclusions. Teunis drew his pipe across the table and said nothing.

For several years no certain tidings reached Communipaw. There were rumors, indeed, and rumors are the small coin of seaports. A coaster from the Chesapeake told of a broad-shouldered young captain who had boarded him off the Capes and drunk his health while taking half his cargo. A Barbadian trader had seen a man answering Yan’s description in a scarlet coat, with pistols at his belt and a black feather in his hat. A sailor from Curaçao declared that Yan had married a governor’s daughter, murdered a governor, become a governor, and been hanged on three separate occasions. As the sailor had been drunk throughout the greater part of his voyage, his testimony was considered particularly rich in detail.

Poor Teunis meanwhile declined. The great chair beside the fireplace stood empty; the tavern grew quiet; weeds appeared between the doorstones. He still maintained that Yan would return reformed, and kept the boy’s little room ready. But his pipe went out more frequently, his pauses grew longer, and one winter evening, after looking many minutes into the embers, he nodded his head as though agreeing with some distant speaker, and died.

The Wild Goose passed, by will, to Yan Yost Vanderscamp, wherever he might be—though the neighbors charitably supposed his present residence to possess no need of taverns or fireplaces.

Scarcely had the grass settled upon Teunis’s grave, however, when Communipaw was roused at midnight by the firing of guns upon the bay. A rakish schooner swept in under a cloud of sail, rounded to before the landing, and discharged a boatload of the most roaring, swearing, sea-salted desperadoes ever blown ashore by an ill wind.

At their head strode Yan Yost Vanderscamp.

He had grown into a rough, burly, bully ruffian, broad as a hatchway and sunburned to the hue of old mahogany. A cutlass slapped against one thigh, a long pistol against the other; gold rings hung in his ears; his black beard bristled like burnt stubble; and across his forehead ran a scar which, he said, had been given him by a Spanish admiral. Pluto followed, unchanged except for a few streaks of gray at his temples and a more settled gloom in his eyes.

Yan kicked open the tavern door, drank to the memory of his uncle, and took possession.

The Wild Goose now entered upon a second and more illustrious career. Its windows blazed throughout the night. Boats came and went at unlawful hours. Bales of silk, casks of spirits, chests of tea, silver candlesticks, church plate, women’s jewels, officers’ uniforms, parrots, idols, rolls of damask, and once a small brass cannon passed through the back door with an informality which suggested the inconveniences of custom houses had been happily abolished.

Yan’s companions were a choice brotherhood: Red Dirk, who had lost two fingers and was proud of the circumstances; Long Tom Halyard, a lantern-jawed sea lawyer who quoted regulations whenever he broke them; French Jack, whose politeness increased with his murderous intentions; and Sam Bitter, a one-eyed gunner who slept with his head in a punch bowl and awoke in a fighting humor. There were others, too—roistering, swaggering, storm-begotten varlets, with tarry queues, scarred faces, enormous belts, and more weapons than articles of faith.

Their revels shook the peaceful settlement. They drank, diced, sang, fired pistols up the chimney, danced hornpipes upon the tables, and told such stories that the very pewter seemed to blush. When the burghers complained, Yan assured them he and his friends were merchants engaged in a free and spirited opposition to the oppressions of government. This explanation satisfied nobody, but, being supported by six loaded pistols, was allowed to pass without debate.

The old frequenters of the tavern withdrew by degrees. It is difficult to enjoy a pipe when a cutlass is being sharpened upon the hearthstone, or to discuss the price of onions while a man beside you relates how he marooned his last creditor. The burghers therefore assembled at the house of Harmen Suydam, where the beer was weaker but the company less liable to stab one another.

For a season Yan prospered. He rode a black horse through the village; wore French lace on Sundays; gave gold pieces to children and cudgel strokes to their fathers; and treated the law with that hearty contempt commonly found in persons beyond its reach. Pluto remained his constant attendant—pilot, servant, bodyguard, confidant, and, as some whispered, evil counselor. The two understood each other by a glance. When Yan roared, Pluto was silent; when Pluto spoke softly, Yan generally listened.

But government, though slow, possesses a long arm and an excellent memory for unpaid duties. A king’s cutter appeared in the bay. Warrants descended upon Communipaw like autumn leaves. Informers sprouted from behind fences. One foggy morning a troop of redcoats surrounded The Wild Goose.

Yan and Pluto escaped through a trapdoor beneath the cider press, gained the marshes, and vanished in a hidden boat. Red Dirk, Long Tom Halyard, and French Jack were less fortunate. They were discovered in the smokehouse, where Red Dirk attempted to pass himself off as a ham.

Their trial was brief, their defense spirited, and their conclusion perpendicular.

The three were hanged in chains upon a small, rocky island lying off the settlement—a dreary place swept by every wind and visited only by gulls. There their bodies swung from an iron gibbet, turning slowly with the weather, while the chains creaked night and day. The island thereafter received the name of Gibbet Island, which it retained long after better-minded citizens had proposed such improvements as King’s Pleasure, Loyalty Rock, and Prospect Park.

It may be wondered that the good people of Communipaw endured so ghastly a spectacle within sight of their breakfast tables. But habit, that great reconciler, soon rendered it no more remarkable than a church steeple or a neighbor’s washing. Fishermen used the gibbet to judge the tide; children counted the bodies as they counted weathercocks; and old men, smoking at the landing, made observations upon the wind according to the manner in which the dead turned their faces.

Yan Yost was seen no more for seven years.

The Wild Goose stood closed and silent. Its shutters sagged; its sign creaked upon one hinge; rain entered the roof; burdock occupied the yard. Travelers hurried past it after sunset, for lights were sometimes reported in the upper chambers, and more than one fisherman declared he had heard a drinking song within, though the doors were locked and no living soul had crossed the threshold.

Be that as it may, upon a warm afternoon in August a lumbering wagon arrived from the south, piled with trunks, chairs, feather beds, birdcages, kitchen ware, and domestic confusion. Beside the driver sat Yan Yost Vanderscamp, stouter, darker, and somewhat battered by time. Behind him, enthroned among the baggage, sat his wife.

Madam Vanderscamp was a tall, angular, high-colored woman, with a nose like the beak of an admiral’s galley and a voice that could have carried orders through a hurricane. Where Yan had met her he never declared. Some said she was the widow of a privateer; others, the daughter of a jailer at Port Royal; while charitable persons supposed she had been sent upon him as punishment by an offended Providence.

She descended from the wagon, surveyed the tavern, the marsh, the village, her husband, and the universe generally, and pronounced them all abominable.

Pluto came last, driving a cart. He looked older now. His hair was iron-gray; his face had grown lean; and his eyes, which had always appeared to watch some object invisible to others, had acquired a fixed and sleepless brightness.

Yan proclaimed that he had retired from the sea and intended to pass the remainder of his days as an honest landlord. Communipaw received this declaration with the respect due to a man who announces a miracle in his own favor. The tavern was repaired, the goose restored to its sign, and a room fitted up with a carved Spanish cabinet, a Turkish carpet, ivory-handled knives, silver cups, and other humble memorials of commercial enterprise.

For a while all went decorously. Yan drank at his own bar, cursed only upon provocation, and paid for such poultry as could be proved against him. His wife governed the household, the servants, the customers, and Yan himself. She made him attend church twice, where he slept with conspicuous firmness. The burghers began to say that marriage had done what magistrates, gibbets, and king’s cutters could not.

Yet there were circumstances.

Low boats still glided to the tavern on moonless nights. Strange men, muffled in cloaks, conferred with Yan behind barred doors. Wagons left before dawn with casks covered by hay. Pluto was often absent for days, returning salt-stained and silent. When questioned, Yan winked and said he traded in oysters; and when it was observed that oysters seldom clinked like coin, he replied that Communipaw oysters were remarkable.

Thus matters stood until the equinoctial storm of which I come now to speak.

The day had been sultry and unnatural. The bay lay flat and leaden; smoke refused to rise; cattle stood with lowered heads; and the bodies upon Gibbet Island hung motionless against a yellow sky. Toward evening a bank of purple cloud lifted in the west. Thunder muttered beyond the hills. The old women shut their shutters, sailors doubled their moorings, and every weather-wise person predicted destruction with that solemn satisfaction peculiar to those who expect to survive it.

Yan had crossed to a neighboring port early that morning upon business which he described as private and therefore honest. He remained there until late, drinking with two shipmasters, a deputy collector, and a person who claimed to be a Portuguese nobleman but sold secondhand anchors. By the time Pluto came to fetch him, Yan was in the elevated condition wherein dangers appear jokes, enemies become friends, and the whole world seems in want of a song.

They pushed off in a narrow boat.

The first gust struck them midway across the bay. The water whitened; lightning opened the clouds from crown to horizon; and thunder rolled overhead as if barrels were being tumbled across the floor of heaven. Pluto bent to the oars. Yan sat in the stern, wrapped in a sea cloak, singing snatches of old voyages and correcting the storm when it failed to keep time.

Their shortest course led past Gibbet Island.

“Pull wider,” said Pluto.

Yan laughed. “Art afraid of three dry sailors?”

“The tide sets hard upon the rocks.”

“Then let it set. Red Dirk never refused me a berth, living or dead.”

Pluto rowed on without answer. The island rose before them—a black lump in the racing water. Upon its summit stood the gibbet, sharp against the lightning. The three bodies swung violently now, flung outward by the gale, their chains jerking and complaining. One turned upon its hook until its empty face seemed fixed upon the approaching boat.

Yan shaded his eyes.

“Ho!” he cried. “There’s Dirk! And Long Tom! And French Jack, as neat as ever! What cheer, lads?”

The wind shrieked through the ironwork.

Pluto ceased rowing. “Do not hail them.”

“Why not? They were civil shipmates.”

“They have waited long.”

“Then they are the more in need of entertainment.” Yan rose unsteadily, braced one foot upon the gunwale, and lifted an imaginary cup. “Red Dirk! Tom! Jack! Come ashore with me! We’ll broach a cask at The Wild Goose, and the devil may settle the reckoning!”

At that instant the lightning struck so near that the whole island sprang into blue fire. The gibbet, the rocks, the beating gulls, the three black forms—all stood out with dreadful distinctness. Yan fell backward into the boat. Pluto caught him by the collar and dragged him from the gunwale as a wave broke over them.

There came, mingled with thunder, a clatter like loud laughter.

Pluto rowed desperately. Not another word passed until the boat grounded at Communipaw.

Madam Vanderscamp stood in the tavern doorway with a candle sheltered in one hand and indignation blazing more brightly than the wick.

“A proper hour!” she cried. “A proper husband! The roof leaks, the chimney smokes, the servants have fled into the cellar, and you come rolling home full of rum and sea water!”

Yan stumbled past her. “Peace, vrouw. I have invited company.”

“Invited company!” Her voice rose above the wind. “As if the house were not already full of your vagabonds!”

“Only three old comrades.”

“Three!” said she. “Then go attend them yourself. Three long-faced, ill-favored rogues have been sitting in the upper room this half-hour, stamping their feet and calling for drink. I told them the tavern was shut. They stared at me and rattled their chains!”

Yan stopped.

The drunken color ebbed from his face.

“Upstairs?”

“Where else? They would not enter the taproom. They went up without asking, dripping all the way, and one of them left seaweed upon my clean stair. Fine friends you keep!”

Yan looked toward Pluto. The latter stood in the outer doorway, rain streaming from him. His expression could not be read.

“You let them in?” Yan whispered.

“I let in no one,” said his wife. “They were in before I heard them.”

From above came the scrape of a chair.

Then a cup struck a table.

Then three voices, cracked, hollow, and far away, began to sing:

> “Ho, for the cup and the red, red tide,  
> And the rope where the king’s men swing us!  
> The host is warm and the door is wide—  
> Drink, dead brothers, drink beside!”

Madam Vanderscamp folded her arms. “There! Your respectable merchants are thirsty.”

Yan’s knees trembled. Yet pride, drink, and the habit of facing danger before witnesses drove him onward. He snatched the candle from his wife and mounted the stairs.

The passage above was dark. The door of the great chamber stood ajar. Through the crack issued a wavering blue light, not the honest yellow of candle or hearth, but a pale flame like burning brimstone under water. It cast the furniture into monstrous shapes and made the shadows crawl upon the walls.

Yan pushed the door.

Around the table sat Red Dirk, Long Tom Halyard, and French Jack.

Their sea-rotted garments hung in strips; iron collars clasped their necks; lengths of chain trailed across the floor. Red Dirk’s two missing fingers were hooked about a silver cup. Long Tom sat with his head tilted at the angle the rope had given it. French Jack wore the remnants of his old lace cravat and bowed with hideous courtesy.

Before them stood a black bottle.

The blue flame burned from its mouth.

Red Dirk raised his cup.

“Welcome, captain.”

Long Tom grinned. “You kept us waiting.”

French Jack pointed to an empty chair. “Your place is ready.”

Yan backed against the wall.

“I—I invited you to drink,” he stammered, “not to stay.”

“Drink first,” said Dirk.

“Settle afterward,” said Tom.

“The devil keeps accounts,” said Jack.

The three struck their cups together.

> “Ho, for the host who mocks the chain,  
> And calls through stormy weather!  
> He gives the word—we sail again—  
> And all go down together!”

Yan uttered a cry which rang through the tavern. He turned, caught his heel upon the top stair, and plunged headlong. He struck the landing, rolled to the bottom, and lay doubled against the wall.

Madam Vanderscamp held up the candle.

“Drunk again,” said she.

But Yan Yost Vanderscamp was dead.

When the neighbors, alarmed by the cry, ventured into the house, they found Pluto kneeling beside the body and Madam Vanderscamp scolding everyone within reach. Several bold men climbed the stairs. The upper room was empty. There was no blue flame, no bottle, no wet footprints, no guests.

Yet three iron stains marked the floor around the table; and upon the sill lay a shred of tarred rope such as had long ago been used upon the gibbet.

The official verdict attributed Yan’s death to a fall occasioned by intoxication. The village verdict was otherwise; and village verdicts, though not written in Latin, commonly survive the longer.

From that night The Wild Goose was shunned. No traveler would sleep there. Sailors crossed themselves when passing its landing. Children dared one another to touch the signpost after dark, but seldom carried the experiment beyond argument. It was said that on stormy nights three voices sang in the upper room, and that a fourth voice sometimes joined them, louder and more blasphemous than the rest.

Madam Vanderscamp remained in possession. Whether from courage, avarice, or unwillingness to permit ghosts the last word, she refused to leave. She maintained that Yan had died of drink, that the marks upstairs were mildew, and that any person who spoke of spirits in her house might expect to encounter one thrown bodily at his head.

Pluto stayed also.

After Yan’s death his manner altered. He scarcely spoke. He wandered along the shore at night, launched his boat in foul weather, and was repeatedly seen pulling toward Gibbet Island. Sometimes he carried bottles; sometimes a lantern; sometimes nothing at all. When asked his purpose, he looked toward the bay and replied, “The captain left debts.”

This saying was variously interpreted. Some supposed buried treasure; others imagined an oath among pirates; still others hinted that Pluto had never been a natural man, but some familiar of the sea who had served Yan until the appointed hour. The dominie reproved such conjectures as heathenish, though he thereafter took the inland road.

Winter passed. Spring loosened the ice. Summer filled the marshes with reeds and mosquitoes. Then came another September, and with it a storm more violent than the first.

All evening The Wild Goose stood dark, except for one lamp in the upper chamber. About midnight the villagers heard a pistol shot. A second followed. Then came the crash of furniture, the breaking of glass, and the unmistakable voice of Madam Vanderscamp raised in furious contention.

Her words were lost in the gale, but another voice answered—a low, hoarse voice believed to be Pluto’s. There followed a shriek, a heavy fall, and such a clanking, stamping, roaring confusion that the nearest household fled to its cellar and prayed until morning.

At dawn a party of burghers approached the tavern, armed with muskets, boat hooks, a rusty sword, and the village constable, who was placed prudently in the rear to prevent escape.

The door hung from one hinge. The taproom had been ransacked. Casks were split, drawers torn out, floorboards pried up. In the upper chamber the Spanish cabinet stood open and empty; gold coins glittered in corners; and blackened footprints crossed the ceiling, though how any person had walked there was not explained.

Madam Vanderscamp lay beside the hearth, strangled with a length of tarred rope.

Pluto was gone.

His bed had not been slept in. His coat remained upon its peg. The little black stone he had worn since the day of his shipwreck lay broken on the floor. His boat was missing from the landing.

The authorities concluded that Pluto had murdered the widow for treasure and escaped by water. This opinion was sensible, lawful, and therefore received with little enthusiasm. Boats searched the bay. Watchmen were posted. Rewards were proclaimed in type so large that no criminal could reasonably overlook them.

On the third morning a fisherman found Pluto’s skiff overturned among the rocks of Gibbet Island.

His body lay beneath the gallows.

There was no wound upon him, nor any rope about his neck; but his hands were clenched upon three links of ancient chain, and his face wore such an expression of terror that the fishermen covered it before carrying him away. Near the skiff were four cups set upright upon the stones. Three were rusted iron. The fourth was silver and bore the arms of Teunis Van Gieson.

The bodies of the pirates had long since fallen away, but during the gale three chains had broken from the gibbet.

No one could say how the cups came there.

No one could explain the footprints at the tavern, the rope about the widow’s throat, or the singing heard above the storm. Nor did anyone discover the missing treasure, if treasure there had been. The Wild Goose was abandoned; its roof fell in; the marsh crept over its foundations; and at last even the gallows-like signpost toppled into the mud.

Gibbet Island itself has been altered by tide, commerce, and those public improvements which destroy landmarks in order that commemorative tablets may afterward be erected to them. Yet old boatmen, when the wind blows sharply from the bay, still avoid the place. They say iron chains may be heard creaking where no gibbet stands, and four voices sometimes rise above the water:

> “The door is shut, the lamp is low,  
> The drowned tide climbs the wall;  
> Who calls the dead through rain must go  
> When dead men come to call.”

As for Pluto, charitable opinion maintains that he was merely a desperate accomplice overtaken by the storm and punished by natural causes. The constable’s report supports this view; and who shall dispute a document bearing two seals and a coffee stain?

The fishermen of Communipaw, however, preserve a shorter explanation.

Pluto, say they, had ventured once too often to invite **Guests from Gibbet Island**.

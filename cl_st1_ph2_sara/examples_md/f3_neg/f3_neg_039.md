# The Museum of Unfinished Things

Every morning at nine, Mara Vale unlocked the front doors of the Museum of Unfinished Things and waited for someone to visit.

Usually, no one did.

The museum stood at the quiet end of Bellweather Street, between a boarded-up bakery and a narrow shop that repaired clocks. Its stone facade was grander than the building deserved. Four marble pillars held up a cracked pediment, and above the bronze doors, faded gold letters announced:

**THE MUSEUM OF UNFINISHED THINGS**

Inside, beneath glass and dust, rested the abandoned efforts of generations.

There was a symphony without its final movement, donated by the grandchildren of a composer who had died convinced that the missing notes would come to him in a dream. There was a bridge in miniature, built from iron and hope, which had never crossed a river. There were novels ending halfway through a sentence, wedding dresses missing sleeves, inventions lacking essential gears, and one wooden staircase that climbed thirteen steps before stopping in midair.

Mara’s job was to care for them all.

She polished the glass cases. She adjusted the labels. She repaired whatever could be repaired without making anything complete.

That last rule was the museum’s oldest and strictest.

**An unfinished thing must remain unfinished.**

The museum’s founder had written it in black ink across the first page of the staff handbook. No explanation followed.

Mara had worked there for eleven years. She knew every incomplete object as another person might know neighbors. She knew that the headless marble horse in Gallery Two gathered dust fastest around its broken neck. She knew that the half-painted portrait of a laughing woman seemed sadder in winter. She knew that if the west windows were opened during rain, the pages of the unfinished encyclopedia turned by themselves until they reached the unwritten entry for “Home.”

She also knew that the museum was dying.

On a Monday in October, the director called her into his office.

Mr. Rook was a thin man with a silver mustache and the permanent expression of someone listening to bad news through a wall.

“We received a letter from the council,” he said.

Mara sat opposite him. On the desk between them lay an envelope stamped with the city seal.

“They’re withdrawing our funding,” he continued. “At the end of the month.”

Mara stared at him. “Then we’ll charge admission.”

“We tried that in 1987. Attendance fell.”

“We could host lectures.”

“About incompletion?”

“About history. Art. Failure.”

“People prefer success.”

Mara looked through the office window toward the central hall. In its middle stood the museum’s largest exhibit: an unfinished ship built by an unknown carpenter. Its ribs curved upward like the bones of a whale. No mast had ever been fitted. No sails had ever been sewn. Yet Mara had always thought it looked ready to depart.

“What happens to the collection?” she asked.

“Some objects will go into storage. Some will be returned to their donors. Others will be sold.”

“Sold to whom?”

“Anyone who wants them.”

The answer struck her as obscene. She imagined the laughing woman’s portrait hanging above a restaurant table, its blank corner stained with grease. She imagined the symphony’s pages cut apart and framed. She imagined the staircase used as firewood.

“There must be something we can do.”

Mr. Rook removed his spectacles and cleaned them with a handkerchief. “The council has offered us one possibility. If we can attract five thousand visitors before the last day of the month, they’ll reconsider.”

Mara nearly laughed. The museum had welcomed three hundred and twelve visitors during the previous year. Seventy had been schoolchildren who believed they were going to the natural history museum across town.

“Five thousand?”

“Yes.”

“In three weeks?”

“Yes.”

Mr. Rook replaced his spectacles. “I have already begun preparing an inventory for closure.”

Mara stood.

“Don’t,” she said.

“Mara—”

“Not yet.”

She left his office and walked through the galleries.

At ten, an elderly tourist entered, asked whether the museum had a restroom, and departed without viewing the collection.

At noon, rain began tapping the windows.

At three, Mara sat beneath the unfinished ship and listened to the old building creak.

Five thousand visitors.

She considered advertising. The museum had no budget. She considered writing to newspapers, but newspapers wanted scandals, murders, miracles, and royal weddings. An assortment of abandoned projects would interest no one.

Unless one of them were no longer abandoned.

Her eyes moved toward the eastern gallery, where a locked door stood behind a velvet rope.

The door led to the Archive of Dangerous Fragments.

Mara had entered the archive only twice, both times with Mr. Rook. It contained unfinished things judged too unstable for public exhibition: a map of places that did not exist, a mechanical bird that whispered the names of people who would vanish, and a silver mirror that reflected its subjects as they might have been.

There was also, Mara remembered, an unfinished story.

According to museum records, it had been discovered in 1846 inside the walls of a demolished house. The author was unknown. The surviving manuscript consisted of forty-seven handwritten pages and ended with a single incomplete line:

**When the last door opened, she saw—**

Several curators had attempted to study it. Each reported vivid dreams. One disappeared for six days and returned with sand in his shoes, though he had never left the city. In 1911, a copyist tried to complete the final sentence. The words vanished from the page, along with the copyist’s ability to read.

The manuscript had been locked away ever since.

Mara knew the rule.

An unfinished thing must remain unfinished.

She also knew rules were often the polished surfaces of old fears. People remembered the warning and forgot the reason.

At half past five, after Mr. Rook had gone home, Mara took the archive key from his office.

The eastern gallery smelled of stone and varnish. She unhooked the velvet rope, unlocked the door, and descended a narrow staircase.

The archive lay underground. Shelves disappeared into darkness, burdened with boxes, cabinets, and shrouded shapes. Mara switched on a green-shaded lamp and located the manuscript in a locked drawer.

It was wrapped in blue cloth.

She carried it upstairs to the curator’s desk and untied the ribbon.

The pages were yellow but perfectly preserved. The handwriting leaned sharply forward, as if every sentence were running out of time.

Mara began to read.

The story concerned a young woman named Elianor who lived in a city where every door opened to the same room. Her mother had disappeared behind one of those doors, and Elianor believed that somewhere there must be an entrance leading elsewhere. She searched cellars, palaces, prisons, and towers. Each door brought her back to the room: four gray walls, one wooden chair, and another closed door.

The story was strange, repetitive, and impossible to put down.

As Mara read, the museum became very quiet. Rain trembled against the roof. The electric lights dimmed.

Elianor crossed the city. She grew older. Friends abandoned her. Authorities arrested her. Still she searched. At last she found a door beneath the roots of a dead tree outside the city walls.

Mara reached the final page.

**Elianor placed her hand upon the golden handle. All her life had led to this threshold. Behind her stood the city of one room; before her waited everything she had been denied. She turned the handle. The last door opened.**

Then came the unfinished line.

**When the last door opened, she saw—**

Mara held her breath.

The empty space below the words seemed enormous. It pulled at her imagination like a well.

What had Elianor seen?

Her mother? Another world? Herself?

Mara picked up a pencil.

She told herself she only wanted to test the old story about the copyist. She would add one word in the margin, observe the result, and erase it.

The pencil hovered over the paper.

At that moment, somewhere in the museum, a door slammed.

Mara jumped. The pencil struck the final page, leaving a tiny gray mark.

From the central hall came the groan of bending timber.

She hurried out.

The unfinished ship was moving.

Its wooden ribs rose and fell as though breathing. Planks slid from the exhibition platform and fastened themselves along the hull. Nails rolled across the floor, then leaped into place. A mast pushed upward toward the glass ceiling.

Mara watched, horrified, as years of missing work completed itself in seconds.

The ship became whole.

Then it vanished.

It did not fade or dissolve. One moment it stood in the hall; the next, there was only an empty platform and the scent of salt water.

Behind Mara, music began to play.

The unfinished symphony’s pages fluttered inside their case. Invisible instruments filled the museum with a final movement no living orchestra had performed. The melody climbed, beautiful and terrible, toward its conclusion.

When the last note sounded, the pages vanished.

“No,” Mara whispered.

A cracking noise came from Gallery Two.

The headless marble horse had grown a head. It shook dust from its mane, shattered the glass around it, and galloped through the wall without leaving a hole.

All around the museum, unfinished things completed themselves and disappeared.

The laughing woman acquired a painted hand and a blue background, smiled directly at Mara, and stepped backward out of the canvas.

The miniature bridge extended beyond its display table, shrinking into an impossible distance.

The staircase in the western hall grew a fourteenth step, then a fifteenth, rising through the ceiling toward stars visible beyond the roof.

Mara ran back to the manuscript.

The pencil mark had changed. It was no longer a simple line. Letters formed slowly across the paper in Mara’s own handwriting.

**When the last door opened, she saw a way out.**

Beneath that sentence appeared another.

**And everything unfinished followed her.**

Mara seized an eraser and rubbed at the words. The paper tore, but the writing remained.

Upstairs, glass shattered. Wind roared through the galleries. Pages flew like birds.

Mara grabbed the manuscript and raced toward Mr. Rook’s office, where the original handbook was kept in a fireproof cabinet.

She found it beneath a stack of tax records. The leather binding had cracked, and the founder’s warning remained on the first page.

**An unfinished thing must remain unfinished.**

This time Mara turned the page.

There was more.

The ink had faded almost to brown.

**Completion is not perfection. It is departure. Every unfinished work remains among us because it still belongs partly to this world. Finish it, and it must go where finished things go.**

On the next page, the founder had written:

**If the Last Story is ever completed, the boundary will open. Every fragment will seek its ending, and our world, which is itself unfinished, may follow.**

Mara looked toward the office window.

Outside, the city was changing.

Across the street, the clockmaker’s sign gained a final painted letter. The shop vanished. At the end of Bellweather Street, construction workers fled from a half-built apartment block as windows appeared across its bare concrete walls. Once complete, the whole building flickered and disappeared.

It was spreading.

Every unfinished song, every abandoned letter, every half-knitted scarf and interrupted apology would complete itself. And then—

Our world, which is itself unfinished, may follow.

Mara returned to the manuscript. She tried crossing out the final sentence. She tried tearing away the bottom of the page. Each strip of paper vanished from her fingers and reappeared whole.

The story had an ending now. It could not be made incomplete by ordinary damage.

But perhaps it could be continued.

Mara pulled a blank sheet from the printer and placed it after the final page. With shaking hands, she wrote:

**But Elianor did not step through.**

Nothing happened.

The sentence sat on the new paper, separate and powerless.

Mara realized the manuscript would not accept an ending merely placed beside it. The story had chosen its own words from her accidental mark. To change it, she would have to enter it where it lived.

The silver mirror.

She ran to the archive.

Objects were disappearing from shelves. A clock missing its minute hand suddenly chimed, became whole, and winked out. An incomplete suit of armor filled itself with a knight who saluted Mara before dissolving into sparks.

At the back of the archive stood the mirror, covered by a black cloth.

Mara tore the cloth away.

The silver surface did not show her reflection.

It showed Mara as she might have been.

In the mirror, she stood on a brightly lit stage while an audience applauded. Her hair was shorter, her face younger. She wore a black evening dress and held a violin.

Mara stepped back.

She had studied music once. She had dreamed of performing. But at twenty-three, terrified of failing an audition, she had put her violin under her bed. A temporary decision, she had told herself.

The museum had hired her a month later.

In the mirror, the other Mara bowed.

Then the image changed.

She saw herself older, sitting beside a hospital bed, holding her father’s hand. In reality, she had arrived too late.

The image changed again.

A sunlit kitchen. A child laughing. A garden. A red front door. A life completed from choices she had never made.

The mirror offered not truth, but possibility. Unfinished lives, waiting beyond the glass.

Mara placed the manuscript against its surface.

The mirror rippled.

The pages passed through.

She followed.

For an instant, she fell through every life she had not lived.

She was a musician. A mother. A traveler. A woman who had married. A woman who had refused. A woman who had left the city at nineteen and never returned. A woman who had died young. A woman who had become cruel. A woman who had been brave at exactly the right moment.

Then she landed on hard ground beneath a white sky.

Before her stood a golden door.

A young woman held its handle.

“Elianor!” Mara shouted.

The woman turned. She was old and young at once, her face shifting with every year of her long search.

Behind her, through the open door, lay a vast blue sea. The completed ship sailed toward the horizon. The marble horse galloped along the shore. Music moved through the air in visible ribbons.

Thousands of finished things crossed into that shining world.

“It’s beautiful,” Elianor said.

“Yes,” Mara answered. “But you can’t go.”

Elianor’s expression hardened. “I have spent my life looking for this.”

“I know.”

“My mother is there.”

Beyond the doorway stood a woman in a green coat, holding out her arms.

Mara felt the ache of it. Every absence mended. Every regret answered. Every effort rewarded with an ending.

“What right do you have to stop me?” Elianor demanded.

“None.”

“Then let me pass.”

“If you do, my world may disappear.”

“Your world?” Elianor laughed bitterly. “Your world left me searching for forty-seven pages and abandoned me at the threshold.”

Mara looked back.

Behind them stretched the city of one room. Every open doorway revealed the same gray walls and wooden chair. It was a prison made by an author who had never decided what lay beyond it.

“You deserved an ending,” Mara said.

“Yes.”

“But not one chosen by accident.”

Elianor glanced through the golden doorway.

Her mother waited beside the sea.

“What else can I do?”

Mara remembered the blank sheet she had tried to add. A story could not be undone once finished. But an ending did not have to be a wall. Sometimes it could be a door into something larger.

“Choose for yourself,” Mara said.

“That is what I’m doing.”

“No. You’re choosing between the room and the answer someone else gave you. There may be more choices.”

Elianor looked at the city behind her. “There are no other doors.”

“Then make one.”

For a long moment, neither moved.

The white sky began to crack. Through the fractures Mara glimpsed the museum, the city, and darkness gathering at the edges of both.

Elianor released the golden handle.

She walked to the nearest gray wall.

From her coat, she drew a piece of chalk.

“Will this work?” she asked.

“I don’t know.”

“That is not a satisfying answer.”

“It’s the only honest one.”

Elianor smiled.

On the wall, she drew the outline of a door.

The golden doorway trembled. The sea beyond it folded like painted cloth. The waiting mother raised one hand—not in farewell, Mara thought, but in blessing.

Elianor finished the chalk door except for a small gap at the bottom.

Everything stopped.

The sea, the ship, the horse, and the ribbons of music froze. The cracks in the sky ceased spreading.

Elianor placed her fingers against the chalk handle.

“Don’t complete it,” Mara warned.

“I won’t.”

She pushed.

The incomplete door opened.

Beyond it lay neither paradise nor the gray room, but a forest at dawn. A path wound between black trees, disappearing into mist. The destination was impossible to see.

Elianor laughed.

This time, her face did not shift. She looked young, though not as young as when her journey began.

“What’s there?” Mara asked.

“I don’t know.”

Elianor stepped into the forest.

The story closed around her.

Mara woke on the archive floor.

The silver mirror stood above her, cracked from corner to corner. It reflected only her ordinary face.

In her hands lay the manuscript.

She turned to the final page.

The accidental ending was gone. In its place, the last lines read:

**When the last door opened, she saw a way out. But she had learned enough of doors to distrust the ones that promised destinations. So Elianor drew another, left it unfinished, and went through.**

Below that, in smaller handwriting, was one final sentence.

**Where she went next is another story.**

The museum had become silent.

Mara climbed the stairs.

Most exhibits had returned. The ship stood unfinished in the central hall, its bare ribs casting long shadows. The symphony lacked its final movement. The marble horse was headless once more.

A few things were gone forever.

The painting of the laughing woman remained an empty canvas. The miniature bridge now led to a real valley visible through a hole no larger than a coin. And the wooden staircase had acquired one additional step.

Outside, Bellweather Street had returned.

Dawn was breaking.

Mr. Rook arrived at eight and found Mara sitting beneath the ship with the manuscript on her knees.

He listened to her story without interruption.

When she finished, he looked ten years older.

“You broke the rule,” he said.

“Yes.”

“You nearly erased the world.”

“Yes.”

“And you entered a dangerous fictional reality through an enchanted mirror.”

“Yes.”

Mr. Rook sighed. “The paperwork will be appalling.”

At nine, they opened the museum.

The first visitor arrived at nine fifteen. Then came three more. By noon, a crowd filled Bellweather Street.

Someone had filmed the disappearing apartment block. Others had recorded music coming from the museum and a marble horse galloping across rooftops. The videos had spread across the world during the night.

Reporters gathered beneath the gold-lettered sign. Historians demanded explanations. Musicians begged to see the symphony. Five thousand visitors arrived before sunset.

The council restored the museum’s funding the following week.

Mr. Rook created a new exhibition titled **The Night the World Nearly Ended**, though Mara objected to the word “nearly.”

The unfinished story remained locked in the archive.

Years passed. The museum expanded into the old bakery and the clockmaker’s shop. People came from distant countries to see the things that had been begun but never completed.

Some visitors laughed at the exhibits. Others wept.

Many recognized something of themselves.

Mara eventually became the director. On her first day in the office, she opened the founder’s handbook and added a line beneath the old warning.

The new rule read:

**An unfinished thing must remain unfinished—unless it learns how to continue.**

She still cared for the collection. She still polished cases and adjusted labels. But once a year, after closing time, she took out the violin she had left beneath her bed for decades.

At first, she played badly.

Then she played less badly.

She never became the woman in the mirror, bowing before a brilliant audience. That life was gone, if it had ever existed. Her father remained dead. The sunlit kitchen belonged to no one she knew. Some doors, once missed, could not be reopened.

But in the central hall, beneath the ribs of the ship that had sailed once and might sail again, Mara practiced.

She did not play symphonies.

She played scales, fragments, and melodies without names. She began songs and abandoned them. She returned to them months later, changing the rhythm or adding a few uncertain notes.

And sometimes, when she paused with the bow lifted and the last note still trembling in the air, she heard something beyond the museum walls.

Not applause.

Not an ending.

Only footsteps on a forest path, moving deeper into the unknown.

**TO THE EDITOR OF THE METROPOLITAN MONTHLY.**

Sir,

It has been remarked by certain melancholy philosophers—men who take observations from churchyards, ruined mills, and the back windows of stage-coaches—that Poetry, like the Indian and the wild pigeon, retires before civilization. No sooner does a turnpike enter a valley than Romance departs over the opposite hill; the surveyor drives his stake through the heart of Tradition; the locomotive whistles, and every ghost within twenty miles packs up his winding-sheet and removes to parts unknown.

I confess, sir, that I have observed something of this mournful process in our ancient province of Nieuw-Nederlands. The gray farmhouses, whose red roofs once glowed among the apple orchards like embers under green ashes, have given place to white villas, philosophical seminaries, patent laundries, insurance offices, and other monuments of human improvement. The broad fireplaces, where logs crackled between brass andirons and illuminated whole histories upon Dutch tiles, have been bricked up to receive polished grates, wherein a small quantity of black mineral maintains a sullen combustion and exhales the perfume of the lower regions. The high-backed chairs, the mighty chests, the spinning-wheels, the blue earthen platters, and the solemn eight-day clocks have disappeared before sofas, ottomans, piano-fortes, and those little French timepieces which tick as nervously as if pursued by creditors.

Yet there remains, immediately opposite the proud city which has usurped the name and substance of New-Amsterdam, a secluded region wherein the genius of foregone ages still sits smoking beneath his ancestral weathercock. I allude, sir, to Communipaw.

Communipaw!—name of mystery, fragrance, and fat pastures! There the roads wander at their own discretion, scorning alike the compass and the statute-book; there mossy fences sink beneath elder bushes and wild vines; there ancient willows stoop over green ditches, as though searching for the reflections of burgomasters long departed; there the kine deliberate in the highway, geese possess the door-yards by hereditary right, and cabbages, ranged in innumerable battalions, maintain possession of the soil.

The stranger, approaching this venerable spot from the river, beholds low roofs, whitewashed walls, barns of immense capacity, orchards twisted with age, and a church whose bell appears to have been cast expressly for funerals. The air is enriched with the mingled odours of salt marsh, cow stable, wood smoke, frying dough, and oysters recently translated from their native beds. If his bosom be not altogether occupied by railroads and real estate, he will feel that he has stepped backward by two centuries.

But, Mr. Editor, it is not merely to preserve one picturesque nook from the ruthless pencil of the surveyor that I address you. I have a disclosure to make—a disclosure involving the fate of this republic, the restitution of an injured people, and, peradventure, the future complexion of the whole western hemisphere.

There exists, and has existed since the surrender of New-Amsterdam to the English, a vast and hereditary conspiracy among the descendants of the Dutch.

Yes, sir—a conspiracy!

You may smile; the Yankee may sharpen his jackknife and smile; the speculator may unfold his map of corner lots and smile; but beneath that smile I detect uneasiness. Naples reposes beside her volcano; the careless traveller plants vineyards upon buried cities; and thus does modern Manhattan erect banks, theatres, hotels, shot-towers, institutes, and oyster saloons above the smothered fires of Dutch patriotism.

When our stout forefathers were compelled to lower the flag of the States-General, they did not submit in spirit. Their resistance assumed a deeper, quieter, and more formidable character. They retired to their farms; they planted cabbages; they smoked; they multiplied. Their ostensible object was agriculture: their real design was restoration.

This secret, sir, has descended through my family with the silver porringer, the great walnut cupboard, and an iron waffle-maker of singular construction. I received it from my father on the evening of my twenty-first birthday, after a dinner of codfish, potatoes, pickled beets, sausages, and pumpkin pie. Having locked the doors, drawn the curtains, and looked beneath the table to ascertain that no Yankee schoolmaster had concealed himself there, he placed upon his head the cocked hat of my great-grandfather and addressed me in these words:

“Roloff, preserve the blood, distrust wooden nutmegs, and remember New-Amsterdam!”

He then drank three glasses to the confusion of Connecticut and became too much affected to proceed.

My father, I should observe, was a patriot of the ancient school. He regarded the landing of the Pilgrim Fathers as the commencement of all our calamities, including hard winters, short weights, turnpikes, and religious lectures on week-days. Every anniversary of that event threw him into such ill humour that we children moved about the house with the caution of conspirators. If one of us offended, he administered chastisement to the tune of “The Pilgrim Fathers,” thereby associating that celebrated melody with recollections which have prevented me from enjoying it to this hour. He would not eat baked beans; he regarded codfish prepared after the eastern fashion as a political insult; and he once dismissed a hired man merely for pronouncing the word “school” through his nose.

From him I learned the machinery of our great design.

In the first place, intermarriage has been employed as a system of military occupation. The old families—Van Rippers, Van Horns, Van Winkles, Van Tassels, Van Dams, Van Pelts, Van Brunts, and sundry others whose names begin with a patriotic preposition—have spread themselves quietly through the province. A daughter has been stationed upon one farm, a son upon another; cousins have formed alliances along the Hackensack; nieces have taken possession of Bergen; widows have remarried advantageously near Hoboken; until the whole region is intersected by a network of blood relationships compared with which the combinations of European diplomacy are a child’s cat’s-cradle.

An ignorant observer attends a Dutch wedding and sees only tables loaded with beef, ham, doughnuts, crullers, cheeses, pies, and bottles. He hears fiddles; he beholds old gentlemen nodding over their pipes and young people performing dances of alarming energy. He supposes the occasion festive. Miserable blindness! It is a council of war. Every marriage adds another recruit to the national cause; every christening is a levy; every cradle is an arsenal.

In the second place, we preserve our traditions by stated assemblies. At these solemn gatherings, generally held about a nut-wood fire, the elders relate how spacious the city was before it became crowded; how honest the markets were before weights were inspected; how deep the snow fell in their youth; how peaches once grew as large as cannon-balls; and how a man might then walk from the Battery to Haarlem without encountering a lecture-room, a railroad cutting, or a gentleman soliciting subscriptions.

The younger members listen, eat, and inherit.

Our enemies have mistaken this tranquillity for stupidity. It is the tranquillity of the lion before he rises; of the ocean before the storm; of a burgomaster after dinner. The Spaniards once imagined their mountains secure, yet an indomitable people descended from them and reconquered Granada. Shall less be expected of men nourished upon buckwheat cakes?

You will now comprehend the true significance of those institutions which have lately assumed the honoured appellation of Knickerbocker. We have Knickerbocker banks, Knickerbocker ice companies, Knickerbocker stages, Knickerbocker insurance associations, Knickerbocker hotels, Knickerbocker fire engines, Knickerbocker debating clubs, and, for aught I know, Knickerbocker pills. The superficial citizen supposes this merely a fashion. Sir, it is a proclamation. The old name, once whispered around family hearths, now advances in gilt letters three feet high and takes possession of public signboards.

Consider also the Society of Saint Nicholas, whose ostensible object is convivial remembrance. Its members dine; they toast the past; they contemplate portraits of ancient governors; they speak with emotion of the Manhattoes; and they depart at a late hour, supported by descendants of families allied before the Revolution. Is this nothing? Did the Carbonari dine for appetite alone? Did the patriots of antiquity assemble merely to discuss the gravy?

Saint Nicholas himself, that venerable commander of aerial transport, has long served as the genius of our cause. Once a year he passes over roofs without toll, turnpike fee, or legislative charter; he descends chimneys in defiance of modern architecture; he rewards children who preserve domestic obedience and terrifies those inclined toward foreign innovation. His very pipe is an emblem. When his image shall again surmount the public buildings of restored New-Amsterdam, I trust no committee will recommend replacing it with an allegorical figure of Commerce carrying a ledger.

Nor is Communipaw an accidental centre of these operations. The Dutch descendants turn toward it as the captive Israelites turned toward Jerusalem. Citizens who have forgotten the road to their grandfathers’ graves can nevertheless point out Communipaw from a steamboat. Painters sketch it; antiquaries visit it; corpulent gentlemen regard it through spyglasses and sigh. Even the fashionable youth, though ignorant whether Pavonia were a Roman goddess or a patent medicine, feels a mysterious respect as he beholds its roofs across the water.

Why this universal reverence? Blood remembers what the head forgets.

I have myself watched from the shore at sunset, when Manhattan rose opposite in brick, smoke, steeples, masts, and restless vapour. The city roared like a furnace. Ferries churned the river; bells rang; steam-whistles shrieked; warehouses, counting-houses, markets, presses, and exchanges seemed to proclaim the eternal sovereignty of Utility. Behind me, meanwhile, a cow cropped grass beside a buried tombstone; a blue thread of smoke ascended from an ancient chimney; children chased a rooster through a cabbage patch; and the weathercock upon the inn pointed steadily toward New-Amsterdam.

At such moments I have doubted which power was truly the stronger.

Do not imagine, Mr. Editor, that our plan requires violence. Heaven forbid! We shall conquer by endurance, cookery, genealogy, and names. The restless races exhaust themselves in speculation; they build, burn, fail, rebuild, migrate westward, invent machines, establish journals, and quarrel over improvements. We remain. A Yankee may purchase a farm, but presently he marries the farmer’s daughter; his children acquire Dutch grandmothers; his descendants join Saint Nicholas; and in the fourth generation they boast that their ancestor came over in the *Goede Vrouw*, though he actually arrived from Massachusetts in a one-horse wagon.

Thus are the invaders absorbed.

The triumphant catastrophe cannot be remote. I look forward to the day when the ancient flag shall float above the Battery; when Broadway shall resume a respectable width; when every marble chimney-piece shall be removed and every family hearth restored to dimensions capable of roasting an ox; when the mayor shall wear a cocked hat; when aldermen shall be selected according to circumference; when no public business shall begin before dinner or continue after it; and when the city, purified of unnecessary haste, shall once more be called New-Amsterdam.

Then shall Communipaw come forth from her marshes like a queen from captivity. Her cabbages shall be crowned with dew; her geese shall lift up their voices; her oldest inhabitant shall produce the key of some gate which no historian remembers; and the descendants of the ancient inhabitants, assembling from Spank-town, Pavonia, Bergen, Haarlem, and the utmost borders of the Hackensack, shall enter the recovered city amid the ringing of bells, the firing of cannon, and the distribution of crullers.

Laugh if you will, sir. Rome was once a cluster of huts; Troy fell because of a wooden horse; empires have been overturned by shepherds, fishermen, monks, and women carrying water-pitchers. Who, then, shall despise a people fortified by family Bibles, marriage settlements, pickle jars, feather beds, and seven generations of grievance?

I remain, with profound respect for your editorial sagacity, and with unwavering confidence in the approaching restoration,

Your obedient servant and compatriot,  
**ROLOFF VAN RIPPER.**

**P.S.**—Sir, stop the press! Hold back, if possible, the prophetic portion of my communication!

Since sealing the foregoing, intelligence of a most disastrous nature has reached me from Communipaw. A young Yankee—one Obadiah Spry, a surveyor by profession, sharp of nose, scant of baggage, and possessed of that restless eye which measures a landscape before admiring it—has eloped with Katrina Van Dunder, sole heiress of the richest cabbage garden in the village.

They were married yesterday.

This morning Spry appeared upon the ancestral ground with a compass, chain, three assistants, and a printed handbill. By noon the garden—where cabbages have stood in hereditary succession since the administration of Petrus Stuyvesant—was divided into two hundred and forty-seven building lots, intersected by streets bearing the names of Progress, Enterprise, Liberty, and Spry Avenue. The old farmhouse is advertised as a hotel site; the orchard is reserved for a depot; and the family duck-pond, under the magnificent designation of “Lake Katrina,” is offered at an additional price.

Communipaw is in consternation. The elders have assembled, but can agree upon nothing except dinner. The geese have abandoned the lower field. At sunset the ancient weathercock, after revolving violently in a perfectly calm air, fixed its beak toward Connecticut.

Sir, I tremble.

The enemy has entered—not with cannon, but with a marriage license and a map of town lots.

We are betrayed!

**R. V. R.**

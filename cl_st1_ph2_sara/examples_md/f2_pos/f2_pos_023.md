We rejoice to announce—speaking, as we invariably do, for the whole human race, by which we mean, of course, those portions of it wearing our colours, drinking at our public houses, and prepared to knock down anybody who differs from us—that our honourable friend has once more been returned to Parliament as the free and independent member for Verbosity.

There are victories which affect a borough; there are victories which affect an empire; and there are victories which, passing beyond the narrow limits of geography, arithmetic, conscience, and common sense, illuminate the illimitable perspective of mankind. Our honourable friend’s majority of sixty-three, or eighty-seven if we include the dead, the absent, and Mr. Gubbins, who voted four times under a misapprehension, belongs emphatically to the last and noblest class.

It had been maliciously urged against our honourable friend that he had voted both for and against the same Bill. He had. It had been further alleged that he had declared the Bill to be at once the Charter of National Salvation and the Funeral Warrant of English Liberty. He had. It was demonstrated from printed reports that on Monday he regarded the measure as indispensable, on Tuesday as infamous, on Wednesday as inevitable, on Thursday as impossible, and on Friday as a matter upon which no responsible statesman had ever expressed an opinion. All this is perfectly true; but truth, unless directed by experienced persons, is a dangerous and disorderly thing.

Our honourable friend explained it. When he had voted for the Bill, he had supported its principle without approving its provisions; when he had voted against it, he had opposed its provisions without abandoning its principle. When he called it national salvation, he spoke prospectively; when he described it as a funeral warrant, he spoke retrospectively; when he said it was inevitable, he referred to the necessity of preventing it; and when he called it impossible, he meant that nothing was impossible to a united people, except, naturally, the particular thing then under discussion. Thus the whole charge fell to pieces, and many gentlemen who had previously considered black to be black perceived that, viewed from the proper side and by the light of Party, it was not merely white, but dazzling.

The position of our honourable friend’s parliamentary allies was equally simple. There were twelve of them. One demanded immediate action; one opposed all action as premature; one favoured delay until action became unnecessary; one insisted that nothing had happened; one declared that what had happened must never happen again; one wished to abolish the institution in question; one wished to crown it with renewed dignity; one believed it to be foreign; one claimed it as peculiarly English; one had not heard of it; one had written a pamphlet about it; and one, with that superb independence which distinguishes the disciplined statesman, awaited instructions.

These twelve patriots constituted, in the phrase of our honourable friend, “an impregnable phalanx of unanimous conviction.”

It was at the Great Constitutional Meeting in the Upper Room of the Blue Lion that a sullen and objectionable person named Tipkisson attempted to disturb this unanimity. The room was magnificently decorated. Across the platform hung a banner bearing the words OUR HOMES, OUR RIGHTS, OUR EVERYTHING; beneath it stood our honourable friend, three magistrates, five brewers, the chairman of the Water Company, and a gentleman whose precise profession was unknown, but who carried a life-preserver and rendered useful services at elections.

The free and independent electors crowded every corner. Some were free by nature, some had been freed that afternoon from the police station, and all were independent of any consideration except beer. The enthusiasm was tremendous. The landlord had nobly thrown open the house—not the doors merely, but the taps—and patriotism flowed at the expense of the committee.

Our honourable friend rose amid a scene which will never be forgotten by those who remembered any of it the next morning.

“Men of Verbosity!” he began.

“Bravo!” cried a voice.

“Women of Verbosity!”

“Better still!” shouted another.

“And those rising generations upon whose yet unformed brows the solemn finger of Futurity has written the imperishable legend of To-morrow!”

At this a baby began to howl, and was immediately acclaimed.

Our honourable friend placed one hand within his coat, extended the other toward the gas chandelier, and asked whether England was to remain England. The meeting replied that it was. He asked whether the heart of the nation was to beat, or whether it was NOT to beat. The meeting, after a moment’s natural uncertainty, decided by a large majority in favour of beating. He demanded whether the sacred bulwarks of our island constitution were to be surrendered to hireling tools, foreign intrigues, cosmopolitan doctrines, anonymous pamphleteers, paid agitators, and persons who asked questions at public meetings.

“No surrender!” roared the free and independent electors.

Our honourable friend thanked them. He had always known where Verbosity stood. Verbosity stood where it had stood; it stood where it was standing; and there, while one stone remained upon another, one roof sheltered an English family, one lion adorned a public-house sign, or one British voice could cry “Shame!” without knowing why, Verbosity would continue to stand.

It was at this majestic instant that Tipkisson rose.

Tipkisson was a small, dry man who neither waved his hat nor stamped upon his neighbour’s foot. He had the sinister habit of listening to what was said and the almost revolutionary vice of remembering what had been said before. The chairman ordered him to sit down. Tipkisson remained standing. The gentleman with the life-preserver approached him from the rear, but paused when Tipkisson turned his head.

“What,” said Tipkisson, “are you and your twelve parliamentary friends actually driving at?”

There are questions which are not questions, but insults; inquiries which are not inquiries, but daggers; requests for information which strike at the Throne, the Altar, the Domestic Hearth, the Roast Beef of Old England, and the licensed sale of intoxicating liquors. Tipkisson’s was of this description.

Our honourable friend smiled—a sad, forgiving smile, such as Britannia might bestow upon a ratepayer disputing an assessment.

“What are we driving at?” he repeated.

“That is what I asked,” said Tipkisson.

“Vehement cheering,” observed the local reporter, who had prepared his account that morning.

Our honourable friend drew himself up.

“We are driving, sir, at the illimitable perspective!”

The room shook with applause. A plaster ornament fell from the ceiling and injured an undecided voter, who was carried out shouting that he would support our honourable friend to the death.

Tipkisson, with deplorable obstinacy, asked what the illimitable perspective was.

Our honourable friend replied that it was not a thing to be bounded by definitions, cramped by figures, hampered by details, or chained to the miserable cart-wheel of literal interpretation. It was a principle, a movement, an aspiration, an onwardness. It was the soul of progress tempered by the wisdom of caution; the energy of advance restrained by the dignity of permanence; the courage to go forward combined with the resolution not to move until the country was prepared to follow.

“Where?” asked Tipkisson.

The meeting groaned.

Our honourable friend said that he would tell them where. He pointed not to the right, nor to the left, but upward—subject always to the legitimate claims of the centre. He pointed to the broad highway along which a mighty people marched beneath the folds of an unstained banner toward the consummation of its historic destiny.

“What consummation?” asked Tipkisson.

Cries of “Turn him out!” “Traitor!” and “Who paid you?” arose from every part of the room.

Our honourable friend begged that Tipkisson might remain. Let him stay! Let him hear! Let him learn, if learning were still possible to a heart cankered by faction and a mind darkened by facts. Our honourable friend did not fear inquiry. No man who possessed the confidence of his conscience, the approval of his Party, the support of five newspapers, and a majority on the platform need fear anything.

“What do you propose to do?” Tipkisson persisted.

Then our honourable friend’s face changed. The smile disappeared; the jaw hardened; the eye flashed with the ancestral spirit of those island warriors who had never retreated except strategically, never surrendered except conditionally, and never changed sides except upon grounds of national necessity.

“I propose,” he thundered, “to defend the English hearth!”

The cheering was prodigious.

“I propose to guard the English home!”

A chair was broken in patriotic excitement.

“I propose to uphold the English altar!”

The chairman of the Water Company wept upon a brewer.

“Against what?” cried Tipkisson.

Our honourable friend wheeled upon him.

“Against mosques!”

The effect was electrical.

“Against minarets!”

A man who had never seen either object shook both fists at Tipkisson.

“Against Mohammedanism!”

At this magnificent word the meeting rose as one man, with the exception of two who had already fallen beneath the table. Nobody knew how Mohammedanism had entered the discussion, but everybody recognized that it must be got out again, and that Tipkisson was the person who had introduced it.

Tipkisson attempted to say that he had expressed no opinion concerning mosques.

“Then why,” asked our honourable friend, with crushing logic, “does the word cause him discomfort?”

Tipkisson said it did not.

“Observe!” cried our honourable friend. “He refuses even to deny it!”

“I have denied it,” said Tipkisson.

“And now he retracts!” exclaimed our honourable friend.

The meeting saw the enormity. A cabbage struck Tipkisson upon the shoulder. Its presence in an upstairs political meeting was unexplained, but it had arrived at the right conclusion.

Our honourable friend continued. He asked whether Verbosity would exchange its village spire for an Oriental dome; whether the bells of St. Barnabas were to give way to barbaric summonses from gilded towers; whether honest English matrons were to be driven from their tea-tables by scimitar-bearing despots; whether Magna Carta had been signed in vain; whether Alfred had burnt the cakes; whether Nelson had turned his blind eye; whether Wellington had met Napoleon; whether the British Constitution, the British Workman, and the British Pint were to be offered up together upon the smoking altar of Asiatic tyranny.

“No!” cried the meeting.

“THAT,” said our honourable friend, pointing at Tipkisson, “is what he would do.”

Tipkisson denied it with some heat, which naturally confirmed the accusation. The gentleman with the life-preserver and four assistants then removed him in the name of free discussion. His hat was retained as evidence. The reporter entered: “Disturbance caused by a foreign sympathizer, promptly suppressed by the unanimous good sense of the meeting.”

The rival candidate, Mr. Pottle, committed the fatal error next day of stating that Tipkisson was not a Mohammedan. Our honourable friend, with admirable restraint, did not accuse Mr. Pottle of being one. He merely asked why Mr. Pottle was so anxious to conceal the fact, who had authorized him to speak for the mosques, whether his committee funds had been examined, and what understanding existed between him and the Grand Mufti. There was no Grand Mufti within three hundred miles, but distance, as our honourable friend observed, was precisely what made the conspiracy so dangerous.

From that hour the contest was decided.

Verbosity awoke. Flags appeared in windows; drums appeared in streets; money appeared in pockets where money had long been a stranger. A lunatic of independent means distributed sovereigns from a cart, under the impression that he was King John confirming the Charter. Our committee, respecting mental affliction, made no effort to contradict him. Every public house became free; every free man became full; and every full man became an argument for the Constitution.

On polling day our honourable friend was borne through the town beneath a banner inscribed:

HEARTHS AND ALTARS!  
LABOUR AND CAPITAL!  
CROWN AND SCEPTRE!  
ELEPHANT AND CASTLE!

The final words were attributed to an excess of national feeling on the part of the sign-painter, and were generally considered the strongest part of the programme.

The proceedings were marked by that peaceable enthusiasm which is the glory of our institutions. Three windows were broken, six voters persuaded, nine heads bandaged, and one notorious opponent prevented from corrupting the election by being locked in a coal shed until the poll closed. The police maintained strict neutrality, arresting impartially everybody who complained.

Then came the count. Vote by vote, bundle by bundle, disputed paper by disputed paper, the will of the people emerged. Our honourable friend had triumphed. The Mayor declared him duly elected; the bells rang; the brewers embraced; the gentleman with the life-preserver disappeared before inquiries could be made; and the lunatic, having exhausted his gold, was prosecuted for creating a disturbance.

Our honourable friend addressed us from the window of the Blue Lion. He declared that the result was not his victory, but ours; not ours only, but England’s; not England’s only, but Civilization’s; not Civilization’s merely, but Humanity’s—by which he meant, of course, the sixty-three persons composing his majority.

He would go to Parliament unpledged except by every pledge he had given, unfettered except by those obligations which circumstances might impose, and resolved to support the Government whenever it was right, to oppose it whenever it was wrong, and to determine which was which after consulting the Whip. Upon the great question he remained where he had always been: in favour of principle, opposed to excess, friendly to progress, loyal to tradition, and committed beyond recall to the illimitable perspective.

We therefore congratulate Verbosity, England, the Empire, and mankind. Reason has been defeated, detail extinguished, inquiry expelled, prejudice enthroned, and ambiguity returned at the head of the poll. The hearth is safe, though nobody threatened it; the altar is defended, though nobody attacked it; and our honourable friend is again empowered to explain that his yes means no, his no means perhaps, and his perhaps is the trumpet-note of an unalterable conviction.

Long may he represent us. Wherever a plain question is asked, he will be found obscuring it; wherever two principles conflict, he will embrace both and act upon neither; wherever ignorance sleeps, he will awaken it with a slogan; wherever prejudice flickers, he will pour on patriotic oil. And when, amid the solemn councils of the nation, some troublesome person demands what all this noise, loyalty, religion, unanimity, and magnificent language are driving at, our honourable friend will rise—honourable, noble, free, independent, and gloriously incomprehensible—and point once more toward the illimitable perspective.

### THE TRANSLATION OF TARRION

> The Moon commands the hidden ink,  
> As Paracelsus knew;  
> The Brothers of the Rosy Cross  
> Read seals and scarlet too.  
> Yet stronger than the stars above,  
> Or spells in Sanskrit writ,  
> Is one small packet gone astray—  
> If found by Persons fit.

There are three ways of obtaining an appointment under the Supreme Government. The first is to deserve it; but that is troublesome, and in certain seasons unhealthy. The second is to be born related to the man who gives it; but this requires foresight on the part of one’s parents. The third is to know something which the Supreme Government would prefer that no one should know.

Tarrion, who was young, good-looking in a worn and serviceable fashion, and possessed of three ponies, two hundred rupees, and no relations whatever, had tried the first method for six years. He had written Reports upon the Administration of Outlying Tracts, Memoranda upon the Supply of Mule-Transport, Notes concerning the Behaviour of Frontier Levies, and one admirable Precis on a subject which the Head Clerk afterwards confessed he had never understood. His reward was leave to Simla and the assurance that his claims would receive consideration.

Consideration is the cold-storage chamber of Government. Things put into it do not decay visibly, but neither do they come out.

Tarrion wanted a permanent berth in Simla—five hundred rupees rising to seven, a decent house, no marching, and sufficient dignity to make men answer his notes within the week. He did not conceal this ambition. Modesty is useful to those who have influence; the remainder must advertise.

“You want too much,” said Mrs. Hauksbee, tapping her riding-whip against her boot.

“I have had too little for so long that the accounts balance,” said Tarrion.

They were sitting in her drawing-room after a small dinner at which an A.-D.-C., whose uniform was better connected than himself, had attempted to annex both the conversation and a widow from Bombay. Tarrion, by a few questions concerning polo-ponies, had induced the youth to contradict the Commander-in-Chief, misquote the Viceroy, and reveal that the widow’s brother had been promised a place in the Political Department. Mrs. Hauksbee had then repeated the contradiction—but not the promise—in the proper quarter. The A.-D.-C. discovered next morning that social life in Simla can become extraordinarily steep.

“You handled him prettily,” she said. “He went down the Mall like a rusked puppy.”

“He had too many decorations for his age. They jingled and confused him.”

“And what do I owe you?”

“A permanent appointment.”

Mrs. Hauksbee passed the silver knob of her whip slowly between her lips. That meant that her mind was working, and when Mrs. Hauksbee’s mind worked, three departments generally complained of over-pressure.

“You have no uncle?”

“Not one fit for public service.”

“No sister who might marry a Councillor?”

“My sisters have principles.”

“Worse and worse. Can you keep a secret?”

“If it concerns myself.”

“That is the only trustworthy answer. Very well. I will get you something.”

She said it as another woman might have promised a cup of tea; but Tarrion thanked her seriously. He knew the difference between a woman who wishes to help and one who wishes to prove that she can.

Two mornings later Fate, which has no appointment in the Secretariat and therefore conducts business promptly, sent a scarlet-coated chaprassi to Mrs. Hauksbee’s verandah. He carried a long blue envelope, sealed, docketed, crossed, counter-crossed, and addressed in the cramped hand which High Officials reserve for matters of Diplomatic Secrecy.

The chaprassi asked for “Hauksbee Sahib.”

“There is no Hauksbee Sahib here,” said the bearer.

The chaprassi, who had walked uphill in the sun and desired breakfast, pointed to the name.

The bearer pointed to Mrs. Hauksbee.

Thus the Empire turned upon an index-finger.

Mrs. Hauksbee received the packet. She might have returned it unopened; and you may be certain that this was the first course she considered, because she always considered the honest course first. Then she observed that the seal had been softened by heat and had lifted cleanly from the paper.

At eleven Tarrion called.

“At half-past eleven,” said Mrs. Hauksbee, “you will tell me whether I am a criminal.”

“At twelve,” said Tarrion, looking at the packet, “I may ask to become an accomplice.”

They opened it with the care due to Government property—which means that they did not tear the envelope.

Inside were minutes, demi-official letters, a cipher summary, and a memorandum written by one Great Person about another Great Person, in which the second Great Person was described as “lamentably susceptible to domestic representations.” There were also details of a hill-state succession, the proposed transfer of a Political Agent, the true reason for delaying a punitive expedition, and a private note concerning the creation of a new appointment at Simla.

That appointment carried five hundred rupees a month, rising to seven hundred; furnished quarters; and duties so delicately unspecified that they could only be important or nonexistent.

Across the first page somebody had pencilled: “We must find a safe man—not brilliant.”

Tarrion read this twice.

“It is a portrait,” said Mrs. Hauksbee.

“Except in one detail.”

“Brilliance can be concealed. Safety is more difficult.”

They read for an hour. I shall not tell you what State was concerned, nor which Chief had borrowed money from which missionary, nor why three regiments were officially moving for exercise in a direction where no sane soldier would exercise them. You must fill in for yourself. Governments alter; reasons do not.

At the end Tarrion arranged the papers in their former order.

“This was meant for Hauksby of the Foreign Office,” he said.

“Who is at Mashobra.”

“And whose wife spells the name without an ‘e.’”

“Government cannot be expected to know its own servants. What will you do?”

“Return the packet.”

“Certainly. And before returning it?”

“Take the honest course.”

Mrs. Hauksbee smiled. “I knew I could trust you.”

That afternoon Tarrion sent his card to the Strong Man—a member of Council whose opinion upon all questions was final until the Viceroy formed another. The Strong Man disliked soldiers, young men, petitioners, and interruptions; Tarrion was all four.

“I have five minutes,” said the Strong Man.

“I need three, sir. I wish to be appointed Secretary for Special and Unrecorded Inquiries.”

The Strong Man’s face altered no more than a good thermometer.

“No such appointment exists.”

“It will be sanctioned on Thursday.”

“Who told you that?”

“No one, sir. I understood also that the salary would be five hundred, rising to seven; though there was doubt whether quarters should be charged to the Provincial or Imperial allocation.”

The Strong Man put down his pen.

Tarrion continued quietly, dropping each fact as a man drops chlorodyne into a glass—one measure, then another, never spilling.

“I believe my frontier experience might be useful in connection with the Rungabad succession. I know something of mule-transport; and, if the three regiments now ordered toward Jandipur are intended solely for exercise, I could perhaps suggest a healthier route. I am also unmarried and therefore unlikely to make domestic representations.”

The Strong Man looked through Tarrion, behind Tarrion, and, so far as was possible within a wooden office, under Tarrion.

“Are you threatening the Government?”

“Good heavens, no, sir. I am offering it special knowledge.”

“Where did you obtain this—knowledge?”

“If I answered that, it would cease to be special.”

The Strong Man rose. It was a famous rise, used upon native princes, refractory Lieutenant-Governors, and once upon a bishop. Tarrion admired it, but remained seated until he judged the performance complete.

“Young man,” said the Strong Man, “you have made a serious mistake.”

“I have made several, sir. That is why I require a permanent appointment.”

“You suppose Government can be intimidated?”

“I suppose Government can be inconvenienced. Intimidation is a word for newspapers.”

The Strong Man walked to the window. Below him lay Simla, glittering among the pines—the Mall, the clubs, the bungalows, the offices where yearly appointments, and two-yearly appointments, and five-yearly appointments were created to reward men who had spent their lives demanding economy.

“What are your qualifications?”

“I have served where I was sent; written what I was ordered; offended no one powerful enough to remember me; and I know when not to put things on paper.”

The Strong Man turned sharply.

“That last qualification is rare.”

“So I have observed.”

The interview lasted seventeen minutes.

At four o’clock the misdirected packet, resealed and wrapped in a polite apology, arrived at Hauksby’s office. At four-five Hauksby sent for his Head Clerk. At four-ten the Head Clerk sent for the chaprassi. At four-twenty the chaprassi remembered that he had delivered the packet to a Mem. At four-thirty the Mem acquired, in official correspondence, no fewer than six possible identities. By five, Diplomatic Secrecy had locked all doors and begun shouting from the windows.

The Strong Man called upon the Foreign Secretary. The Foreign Secretary called upon the Military Secretary. The Military Secretary called the occurrence “damnable,” which cleared his department of responsibility. A Commission of Inquiry was proposed, abandoned because it would create a record, revived because the absence of a record might look suspicious, and finally converted into a confidential conversation at the Club.

Meanwhile Tarrion dined well and slept better.

For four days the machinery of Government stood stripped of casings, lacquer, paint, and guard-rails. Wheels spun; pistons hammered; clerks carried boxes uphill; Private Secretaries whispered downhill; and every man asked who had spoken, though none dared ask what had been said. A little knowledge is dangerous, but a little uncertain knowledge in Simla is an earthquake with minutes attached.

I could tell you how the Strong Man blamed the indexing system, how the indexing system was defended by a clerk drawing eighty rupees, and how that clerk was transferred for the preservation of official discipline. I could also tell you why the Political Department objected to Tarrion, why the Home Department claimed him, and why Finance, after refusing the salary on principle, sanctioned it under another heading. But these are Cabinet matters, and Cabinet matters become respectable only after everybody concerned is dead.

. . . . . . . . . . . . . . .

On the fifth morning Tarrion received an envelope. He was appointed Secretary for Special and Unrecorded Inquiries, on five hundred rupees a month, rising by approved increments to seven hundred, with furnished quarters and travelling allowance. The post was temporary, but renewable; which, in India, is one form of immortality.

He kissed the paper.

Then he went to Mrs. Hauksbee.

“You have done it,” he said. “There is no woman in Asia fit to hold your riding-whip.”

“Don’t be extravagant. Did they ask where you learned it?”

“They asked everything except the question whose answer they feared.”

“And the packet?”

“Returned intact.”

“So virtue has triumphed.”

“Completely.”

That evening Tarrion was summoned to the Viceregal Lodge. What occurred there cannot be written down; but when he came away he repeated one sentence.

The Viceroy had said: “Mr. Tarrion, a man may intimidate the Government of India once. If he attempts it twice, he becomes the Government’s servant in a district where there is neither society nor ice.”

Tarrion bowed and replied that one successful experiment should satisfy any scientific mind.

Later, at the Club, he declared that his appointment was due wholly to Mrs. Hauksbee’s genius, courage, political instinct, beauty, and unparalleled knowledge of men. This was excessive, but gratitude generally is when it costs nothing.

Mrs. Hauksbee accepted the tribute, and allowed Simla to believe she had shaken the Supreme Government to its foundations. Then she returned home, took off her gloves, and passed the knob of her riding-whip once between her lips.

“The packet was Luck,” she said. “Tarrion was vanity. Government was fright.”

She considered the three forces, and laughed softly.

“What fools men are!”

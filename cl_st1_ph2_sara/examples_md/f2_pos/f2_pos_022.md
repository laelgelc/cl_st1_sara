### A Considerate Proposal for Relieving the Kingdom of Its Superfluous Infants, and for Rendering the Poor Profitable to Their Country

It is a spectacle capable of disturbing the most composed traveller, when he walks through Dublin, Cork, Galway, or any country town of this afflicted kingdom, to behold the lanes crowded with women in rags, each followed by four, five, or six children, all bare-legged, importunate, and of no visible use to themselves or the public. These mothers, instead of earning an honest livelihood, are forced to employ their whole time in begging sustenance for their helpless offspring, who, as they advance in years, commonly become thieves for want of work, enlist in foreign service, sell themselves to the plantations, or cross the sea, where they may enjoy the privilege of starving under another flag.

All parties agree that this prodigious multitude of children, carried upon backs, dragged by hands, or left crying at cabin doors, is an additional grievance to the nation; and whoever could discover a fair, cheap, and easy method of making them sound and useful members of the commonwealth would deserve a statue erected at the expense of every parish.

My intention is not confined to the children of professed beggars. It extends to all infants of a certain age whose parents are as little able to support them as those who solicit alms in the streets. I have bestowed many years upon this subject, consulting merchants, graziers, physicians, victuallers, one clerk of the revenue, and a gentleman who hath travelled as far as Lisbon; and I find the schemes hitherto offered to be defective in calculation, troublesome in execution, and founded upon an extravagant opinion of human charity.

For we have been told that the poor should be employed, though no employment is provided; that children should be taught trades, though their masters demand premiums; that rents ought to be moderated, though landlords cannot be expected to diminish their own consequence; and that the hungry may live by industry, which is as reasonable as advising a man without legs to improve his fortune by dancing.

I shall therefore take the matter from the beginning, and proceed with all the exactness that so grave a subject requires.

The number of souls in this kingdom is commonly reckoned at somewhat above two millions. From these I subtract the persons of quality, the clergy, officers, merchants in tolerable circumstances, farmers who possess more than three cows, and such artificers as eat meat once in a fortnight. I further subtract barren women, unmarried ladies of reputation, children lost before baptism, and mothers who, by prudence or weakness, bring forth no living infant. There remain, according to my computation, two hundred and forty thousand couples whose chief estate consists in their fertility.

Of these couples, thirty thousand may perhaps maintain their children after some fashion, although I confess I never saw the fashion except at a distance. There remain two hundred and ten thousand breeding women. Allowing for miscarriages, fevers, accidents, and the laudable carelessness of nurses, I reduce the annual produce to one hundred and sixty thousand infants.

The first year may be supported with little charge, the child subsisting principally upon the mother’s milk, with additions of potatoes, thin oatmeal, and whatever may be obtained by lamentation at a gentleman’s gate. I have been assured by a parish nurse, a woman of great experience and no tenderness to mislead her judgment, that a child may be maintained during this period for less than seven shillings, including rags, physic, and burial insurance; and often for considerably less, when the mother is fortunate in begging.

The question therefore is how these infants, after completing their first year, may cease to be a burden upon their parents and parish, and become instead a support to thousands.

They cannot be generally employed in agriculture; neither can they build houses, dig bogs, spin sufficient yarn, or steal with any profitable dexterity until they are at least six years old. Even at that age the value of their thefts seldom repays the cost of keeping them, for a child must consume many potatoes before he becomes expert enough to carry away a silver spoon. Their sale abroad hath also declined, several plantations preferring full-grown labourers, whose teeth and limbs may be inspected before purchase.

Having weighed these circumstances, I shall now humbly offer my own thought, which I hope will not be liable to the least objection.

I have been assured by a very knowing American, lately returned from his country and now residing near Smithfield, that a young healthy child, well nursed and exactly one year old, is a most nourishing and wholesome food, whether stewed, roasted, baked, boiled, or dressed in a savoury pudding; and I make no doubt that it would equally serve in a fricassee, a pie, or a ragout.

I therefore propose that of the one hundred and sixty thousand infants already computed, twenty thousand be reserved for propagation, whereof not above one fourth part need be males; for this proportion is allowed by all experienced breeders to be sufficient, especially since these children are seldom born within the restraints of marriage, a circumstance which our common people regard as a ceremony rather than a necessity. The remaining one hundred and forty thousand may, at one year old, be offered for sale to persons of fortune throughout the kingdom, the mother being advised to give the infant plentiful milk during the final month, that it may appear round, fat, and well-coloured at market.

A child of that age will weigh, if reasonably nourished, about twenty-eight pounds; and may, when dressed, yield twenty pounds of excellent meat, allowing for bone, skin, and those inward parts which cooks of invention will know how to employ. It will provide two dishes at an entertainment of friends; and when the family eats alone, a fore or hind quarter will make a sufficient dinner, seasoned with pepper, salt, and a little sage, and will keep four days in winter.

I grant that this provision may be somewhat dear, and therefore particularly proper for landlords, who, having already consumed the substance of the parents, seem to possess the best title to the children.

The price of a good carcass may reasonably be fixed at twelve shillings. Of this, two shillings will compensate the broker, examiner, and market fee; while ten shillings remain to the mother, who will thereby obtain a clear profit of three shillings after the expense of the infant’s yearly maintenance. She will moreover be prepared to begin again, and may, with ordinary diligence, produce another commodity before the former hath entirely disappeared from the tables of the gentry.

The purchaser will have twenty pounds of meat of a quality at once tender and uncommon. Nor should the expense alarm any family accustomed to imported wine, French lace, English coaches, Italian music, and lawsuits concerning precedence. A dish whose rarity recommends the host, whose delicacy rewards the guest, and whose consumption benefits the nation ought not to be judged by the rules applied to beef or mutton.

Children would be in season throughout the year, though more plentiful at certain periods, which divines may explain by examining the calendar of fasts. There would likely be a considerable supply in the month following the harvest, when marriages and other accidents are frequent among labourers. Thus provident tavern keepers might publish the expected varieties in advance, as they now advertise venison, oysters, and foreign wines.

I recommend that the markets be established in the principal towns on two mornings each week, with private contracts permitted in remote districts. No slaughterhouse need be built at public cost, for every butcher of moderate skill will perform the operation at home; although I confess it would be better to purchase the children alive and dress them immediately, as is commonly done with sucking pigs. This will preserve the flesh from spoiling and prevent fraudulent dealers from substituting the offspring of older families.

A certain gentleman, eminent for improving whatever belongs to the table, hath communicated several receipts to me. He proposes that the breast be baked beneath a crust with onions; that the legs be larded and roasted before a quick fire; that the hands, being delicate, be served in a white sauce; and that the head, with suitable herbs, will make a broth restorative to elderly politicians exhausted by the public service. He further believes that twins might be stuffed together for a civic feast, though I apprehend the appearance would be too affecting for ladies at their first trial.

The skin, carefully removed, may be dressed into admirable gloves for women of quality and summer boots for fine gentlemen. Smaller pieces may cover pocket books, card cases, and devotional volumes; by which happy economy the same infant may furnish both the appetite and the morals of its owner. The bones, polished and divided, would make handles for fans and fruit knives; but this branch of manufacture should be attempted cautiously, lest an abundance lower the value.

I have heard an objection that the flesh of such infants as die naturally could be preserved and sold at a cheaper rate. Yet this cannot be depended upon, because the bodies of the poor are commonly too lean, and are often injured by fever, hunger, damp lodging, or excessive exposure. Commerce requires uniformity. A merchant must know what he buys, and no prudent cook would risk the honour of a dinner upon an infant whose manner of death is uncertain.

Another projector, a person of active genius, proposed to extend the scheme to boys and girls between twelve and fourteen, whereof there are great numbers in every county and little demand for their labour. But I could not approve his advice. The males are generally tough and stringy from wandering the roads, and their exercise gives the flesh an unpleasant firmness. The females would indeed be tenderer, but their destruction would lessen the future stock; besides, some persons of delicacy might censure the practice as bordering a little upon cruelty.

My American acquaintance further observed that young persons of eighteen might serve in time of scarcity; but I am informed that their meat is coarse, especially among the males, and that the women, though often well covered, are more profitable as breeders. I am therefore unwilling to disturb the simplicity of my original design by introducing articles of doubtful quality.

As to the aged, the sick, the maimed, and those worn out by labour, I have long considered how the nation might be eased of that encumbrance; but it is needless to give ourselves anxiety upon their account. They are every day dying from cold, famine, filth, and vermin with all possible expedition. The younger labourers are nearly in the same hopeful condition. They cannot obtain work, and consequently decay from want of nourishment, so that if they are accidentally hired, they lack strength to perform the task. Thus the country and themselves are happily delivered from future inconveniences.

I shall now enumerate the principal advantages of my proposal, requesting the candid reader to observe that I set down only those benefits which are obvious and certain.

First, it would greatly diminish the number of Papists, who multiply with a zeal unknown among prudent Protestants, and who are commonly suspected of favouring the Pretender whenever they possess either a weapon or a dinner. Since their poverty gives them the highest fertility, they would contribute most liberally to the market; whereby the kingdom would be strengthened in religion without sermons, laws, or conversions.

Secondly, poor tenants would possess something valuable of their own, which might lawfully be seized for rent. At present a landlord who enters a cabin finds neither money, grain, furniture, nor clothes worth carrying away. Under my plan, he could accept a child in payment, either alive at the current price or dressed according to agreement. This would introduce certainty into rural accounts and preserve many families from the humiliation of having nothing left to surrender.

Thirdly, whereas the maintenance of one hundred and forty thousand children after their first year cannot be reckoned at less than twelve shillings apiece annually, the public saving would amount to eighty-four thousand pounds, besides the profit of a new dish introduced to the tables of wealthy persons who spend their income at home. The money would circulate among ourselves, the goods being entirely of Irish growth and manufacture, requiring neither foreign seed nor imported machinery.

Fourthly, the habitual breeders would gain three shillings yearly by each sale and be freed from the expense of maintaining the child after twelve months. Those especially industrious might arrange successive births with such skill that an infant would be ready each season, thereby securing a steady income independent of harvest, weather, or the honesty of employers.

Fifthly, taverns would receive a notable increase of custom. Cooks would compete to invent the most pleasing sauces; houses famous for a tender shoulder or a well-seasoned loin would be frequented by all gentlemen who value their reputation as judges of good eating. A skilful host might purchase the infant himself, and the mother, if employed as a wet nurse until the day of sale, could provide useful information concerning its diet and disposition.

Sixthly, this practice would become a powerful encouragement to marriage. Mothers would regard children as yearly profits rather than additional mouths; husbands would value pregnant wives as farmers value cows in calf; and men would cease beating women during the months preceding delivery, for fear of damaging the goods. A wholesome rivalry would arise among mothers to bring the fattest child to market. The father, instead of complaining at the birth of twins, would reckon the double return and invite his neighbours to drink upon his good fortune.

Seventhly, the plan would improve domestic management. Nurses would be more careful, parish officers more attentive, and midwives more exact in recording births. We should require inspectors, weighers, brokers, salters, leather dressers, and clerks, creating employments for many persons who now live merely by examining one another’s accounts. Even disputes would have their usefulness, for lawyers might determine whether an infant promised to a creditor could be claimed by a landlord, or whether twins should be sold separately.

Eighthly, our gentlemen would learn to reside more frequently upon their Irish estates. Since the commodity cannot conveniently be exported alive without waste, and loses tenderness when salted for a long voyage, it ought chiefly to be consumed near its place of production. Absentee landlords, tempted by the freshness of the supply, may return from London for the season; and tenants would at last enjoy the honour of feeding their masters in the most direct manner imaginable.

Ninthly, the visible reduction of beggar children would greatly improve our streets. Foreign visitors, instead of meeting troops of hollow-cheeked creatures at every church door, would observe clean markets, regulated prices, and well-appointed stalls. They would report that Ireland, formerly accused of disorder, had found a method of converting its most melancholy spectacle into a branch of national commerce.

Many other advantages might be named: the relief of parishes; the increase of knives and cooking vessels; the improvement of sauces; the encouragement of marriage settlements; the creation of a new subject for physicians; and the pleasure which persons of quality naturally receive from possessing what common families cannot afford. But I omit these considerations, being desirous of brevity.

Some tender readers may inquire what affection a mother will bear toward an infant intended for sale. I answer that affection will be increased, not diminished, by the prospect of profit. She will guard it from cold, wash it more frequently, and endeavour to preserve its limbs straight and complexion clear. At present, a child brings her only labour and fear. Under this scheme, every ounce it gains adds to her fortune. Interest, which governs senators, merchants, and bishops, may surely be trusted to improve the care of nurses.

Others may say that foreigners will condemn us. This is unlikely; for foreign nations condemn only those practices from which they derive no advantage. If they desire a share, licenses may be granted. Yet I oppose extensive exportation, because the flesh is best eaten fresh, and because we ought not, through excessive generosity, to send abroad the profits of a domestic improvement.

It may also be objected that the population of the kingdom would thereby decrease. I freely acknowledge this, and it is one chief purpose of the proposal. I desire the reader to remember that my remedy is calculated for Ireland alone, and for no flourishing nation where people are fed, clothed, employed, and considered human except during elections.

I can think of no substantial objection unless it be urged that the number of inhabitants hath already been sufficiently reduced by emigration, eviction, disease, and hunger. To this I reply that those methods are irregular, slow, and unprofitable. A family dying in a ditch yields nothing to the landlord, the tavern, or the treasury. My plan introduces order where there is now only waste.

I profess, in the sincerity of my heart, that I have not the smallest intention of offending any person. I seek only the public good: to advance trade, provide for infants, relieve the poor, gratify the rich, and offer some pleasure to our landlords, whose appetites deserve every encouragement after the fatigues of collecting rent.

Therefore let no man speak to me of other remedies: of taxing those who draw Irish revenues while living in England; of refusing foreign luxuries which consume the little money remaining among us; of wearing clothes made by our own workers; of teaching landlords that tenants must eat before they can pay; of rejecting the pride, vanity, idleness, and gambling that devour whole estates; of introducing honesty into merchants, moderation into squires, responsibility into officials, and conscience into those who govern.

Of requiring the wealthy to spend one part of their income among the people from whom they obtain it; of paying labourers enough to preserve their strength; of protecting cottages from arbitrary seizure; of permitting farmers to profit by improvement; of encouraging manufacture without first enriching the undertaker and ruining the worker.

Of curing the spirit of faction; of ceasing to measure loyalty by religion; of loving our country sufficiently to purchase its goods; of treating its people as something more than instruments of rent; of placing merit before patronage; of expecting members of Parliament to remember the condition of those whom they represent.

Of asking a creditor to wait, a landlord to abate, a judge to listen, a merchant to deal fairly, or a gentleman to surrender one bottle of wine that a family may have bread.

Of mercy, justice, restraint, patriotism, and common fellow-feeling.

Let no one mention these desperate fantasies until he can show the least probability that they will be sincerely attempted. For I have conversed too long with men of experience to place confidence in such unsubstantial projects. They require changes of habit, sacrifices of profit, and recognition that the poor possess souls like ourselves; whereas my method asks no man to abandon his luxury, his prejudice, his rent, or his appetite. It accommodates itself to the world as it is, which every practical reformer knows to be the first rule of benevolence.

I am not so violently attached to my own opinion as to reject any proposal equally innocent, cheap, easy, and effectual. But before an answer is offered, I desire its author to consider two points. First, how he will feed and clothe one hundred and forty thousand useless mouths; and secondly, how he will persuade the owners of this kingdom to part with one penny for that purpose when they have already shown greater willingness to part with the people.

Let him further ask the parents whether they would not account it a happiness to have been sold at one year old, and thereby escaped the long succession of miseries through which they have since passed: hunger, nakedness, rents impossible to pay, labour impossible to find, children impossible to feed, and the perpetual expectation of being driven from the cabin where they were born. I make no doubt that many would declare my proposal an act of retrospective mercy.

For my own part, having wearied myself in service of the public without hope of reward, I may claim at least the credit of disinterest. I have no infant by whom I can gain a single shilling; my youngest child being nine years old, of a lean constitution, and therefore wholly unfit for the table, while my wife is past the age at which she might supply the market.

I have consequently no motive but the welfare of my country, the ease of the poor, the improvement of trade, and the satisfaction of those persons whose prosperity hath always been nourished by our want.

# The Quiet Frequency

At nine minutes past five, Owen Hale came up from the basement of Hale Radio & Appliance with a burnt tube in one hand and copper dust on his sleeves.

He had been below since lunch.

The basement was a low concrete room under the shop, built as a storm shelter by Owen’s father after the big tornado of 1927. No windows. One steel door. Shelves of Philco tubes, condensers, coils, wire, solder, batteries and cardboard cartons of parts that no longer fit anything people owned. The walls were thick enough to stop radio reception. Owen knew because customers’ sets went dead when he carried them downstairs.

He had spent four hours repairing a police-band receiver for Sheriff Danton. The trouble was a cracked socket and a hairline break in the coil. Nothing mysterious.

At five, Owen replaced the back panel, washed his hands in the deep sink and started up the stairs.

The shop was empty.

That was ordinary. Lila Watts, his clerk, left at four-thirty to get supper started for her husband. Owen usually closed at six. He liked the quiet half hour when the school traffic had passed and the farmers had gone home.

But the quiet was not ordinary.

No automobile horns. No screen doors slamming. No voices from Fennel’s Drug Store next door.

Nothing.

He pushed open the front door and stepped onto Main Street.

Millhaven lay warm and yellow in the late September sun. The courthouse clock said five-eleven. Cars stood at the curb. The red Rexall sign turned slowly above Fennel’s. A delivery truck idled in front of Morgan’s Market, breathing pale exhaust into the air.

Owen relaxed.

The door burst open.

Two state troopers entered with guns drawn.

Owen fired at the ceiling. They ducked. He overturned the desk and ran into the engineering booth.

The woman engineer caught his arm.

Her mouth opened.

A clear filament slid between her lips.

Owen struck her with the revolver. She fell against the control panel.

He saw banks of glowing tubes, switches and meters. The transmitter. The signal carrying his voice over three counties.

Also carrying the low note.

The modulation needle moved steadily though nobody spoke.

They had used him.

His warning had opened radios everywhere. People had turned up the volume to hear. Farmhouses. Diners. Filling stations. Patrol cars.

Through WHRW, the note was spreading.

Owen grabbed the master switch.

A trooper shot him.

The bullet struck high in his back. He fell against the console.

His fingers remained around the switch.

He pulled.

The room went dark.

The low note stopped.

For three seconds there was silence.

Then an emergency generator coughed to life.

Red lights returned.

The note resumed.

Owen lay on the floor. He could not feel his legs.

The troopers stood over him.

One was Lieutenant Ames, a narrow-faced man with silver hair. His eyes were clear.

Too clear.

“You almost damaged the transmitter,” Ames said.

Owen tried to lift the revolver. His hand would not move.

The engineer rose behind the console. Blood ran from her scalp. The clear filament withdrew into her mouth.

“You were waiting,” Owen whispered.

Ames crouched beside him.

“We monitor the dead spaces.”

“What?”

“Basements. Tunnels. Mines. Vaults. Places where the signal does not reach.”

His voice was quiet and courteous.

“How long?” Owen asked.

Ames considered.

“Here? Since spring.”

“No. How long have you been doing this?”

The lieutenant smiled.

“Your people have always made sounds for us. Drums. Bells. Horns. Telegraph wires. Radio has made the work easier.”

Owen remembered Sunday bells rolling over fields. Factory whistles. Air-raid tests. School buzzers. Telephone voices.

“You don’t need to replace everybody,” he said.

“No.”

“Just tell them what not to see.”

“Yes.”

“Vickers. The man walking backward. What happened to him?”

“A command failed to settle properly. He continues to obey its final physical instruction.”

“Why didn’t anyone help him?”

“They were told there was nothing unusual.”

The answer was simple.

That made it worse.

Owen coughed. Blood filled his mouth.

“Cal Rusk,” he said.

Ames tilted his head.

“The man on the road?”

“One of yours found him. He was alive for several minutes.”

Owen closed his eyes.

“Is he dead?”

“Yes.”

Ames did not sound pleased. He did not sound sorry.

Just factual.

Owen opened his eyes again. “My family?”

“The youngest male resisted integration. There was some tissue loss. The others are functional.”

Benny.

Owen tried to rise.

Nothing happened.

The announcer had returned to his chair. He arranged the livestock report in front of him.

The red ON AIR light glowed.

“Why keep me alive?” Owen asked.

“To complete the warning.”

Ames nodded to the microphone.

The announcer began speaking.

“We interrupt this program with a bulletin concerning an armed and dangerous man. Owen Hale, age forty-one, of Millhaven, is wanted for murder and attempted murder. Hale may be suffering from a contagious neurological illness causing hallucinations and violent behavior. Residents are advised to remain indoors and keep radios tuned to this station for instructions.”

Owen laughed.

It hurt.

“You can’t control everyone,” he said. “Some won’t hear.”

“No.”

“People in mines. Deaf people. Men at sea.”

“Exceptions occur.”

“You’ll miss some.”

“We expect to.”

Ames rose.

“Then they’ll fight.”

“They usually kill one another first.”

Cal’s body lay again in Owen’s mind. The glass hearing aid in the dust.

Ames looked down at him.

“Fear is extremely useful.”

The note deepened.

Owen felt it enter the wound in his back. It moved through his body like warm water.

His thoughts slowed.

Ruth’s face blurred.

The kitchen vanished.

Benny’s torn mouth became unimportant. Cal Rusk became a dark shape on an empty road. Millhaven became lights beneath a clear web.

Everything quiet.

Everything ordinary.

Owen tried to remember the basement.

Concrete walls.

Steel door.

Silence.

For one instant he found it.

Then the quiet frequency covered him.

At six-twenty the next morning, Miriam Cole emerged from the refrigerated storage room beneath Harrow Memorial Hospital.

She was a laboratory technician, thirty-four years old, unmarried, precise and habitually late for breakfast. A compressor failure had spoiled three shelves of plasma, and she had spent the night transferring the remaining stock into insulated cases.

The storage room had thick concrete walls and a steel-lined door.

Her portable radio had not worked inside.

Miriam climbed the service stairs carrying a clipboard and thinking about coffee.

The hospital corridors were busy. Nurses rolled carts. An orderly polished the floor. Dr. Greaves dictated notes near the elevators.

Everything looked normal.

Outside, the morning was clear and cool.

Miriam crossed toward the diner opposite the courthouse.

A crowd had gathered in the square.

Not a real crowd. People stood in loose groups, talking about weather, work and Saturday errands. Cars moved along Main Street. A newsboy called headlines from the corner.

Above them, from the radio tower on the municipal building, hung a man.

He was suspended by wire beneath the lowest crossbeam. His shirt was dark with blood. His head rested at an unnatural angle. One shoe was missing.

A cardboard sign hung from his neck.

OWEN HALE  
MURDERER  
DO NOT APPROACH

People passed beneath him without looking up.

Miriam stopped in the middle of the sidewalk.

A nurse she knew brushed past.

“Miriam? You all right?”

Miriam pointed.

“There’s a man up there.”

The nurse followed the direction of her hand.

For a second her face remained blank.

Then a low note began inside the municipal loudspeakers.

The nurse smiled.

“What man?” she asked.

### THE LAST TREE

A pine forest is Nature’s expression of solemnity and solitude. Other woods may whisper, laugh, or riot in the wind; the pine forest chants. Its dim aisles stretch away like the nave of some primeval cathedral, its pillars rise blackly against the snow, and from its vaulted roof descends a perpetual murmur, now tender as prayer, now stern as judgment. Men entering there lower their voices without knowing why. Even the axe, that insolent tongue of civilisation, seems for a moment abashed before it begins its sharp, metallic blasphemy.

Luther Dallas had lived among the pines for forty-seven years, and during thirty of them had slain trees with the swift surety of an executioner. He knew the straight-falling pine, the treacherous hemlock, the birch that twisted like a wounded snake, and the stubborn oak that clung to its stump by one last fibre after a dozen blows. Yet from boyhood he had believed that a tree would kill him.

His father had died so. The old man had misjudged a wind-snapped cedar, and they had carried him to camp upon a litter of poles, his beard full of snow and his breast crushed flat. Luther, then a silent lad of fourteen, had looked upon that dreadful repose and accepted it as a prophecy concerning himself.

“Trees remember,” his mother had said afterward, half in grief and half in the northern superstition that grows where winters are long and human voices few. “Your pa cut many. One took him back.”

The saying remained in Luther’s mind as an iron nail remains in a beam long after the house has fallen.

One gray December morning the gang-boss ordered him to fell a great Norway pine standing apart on a rise. It was a magnificent tree, scarred by lightning, forked near the crown, and clothed in snow from root to crest. While its fellows swayed and muttered, it stood immovable, lifting its giant arms against the colorless sky.

Luther examined it, walked round it twice, and spat thoughtfully into the snow.

“Well?” demanded the boss.

“I kind-a left this one t’ the last.”

“What for?”

Luther regarded the trunk. “Don’t like the look of it.”

The men laughed. A lumber camp pardons weakness in a horse more readily than fear in a man.

“Looks like two thousand feet to me,” said the boss. “Get at it.”

“It’ll bring bad luck.”

“Bad luck don’t scale logs. Chop!”

Luther reddened beneath his beard. There are commands which conquer judgment because disobedience would wound pride, and pride is often the only property of a laboring man. He pulled off his mittens, tightened his belt, and swung the axe.

The first stroke rang through the woods. A white chip leaped from the pine as though a tooth had been struck from the mouth of a giant. Again the axe flashed; again it bit. Soon the blows fell in rhythm, deliberate and terrible.

“Hi yi halloo!” cried one of the men from below.

Luther answered mechanically, “Hi yi halloo!”

The notch deepened. Resinous fragrance rose from the wound, mingling with the bitter smell of frost. The tree began to tremble. A faint vibration passed upward through the trunk, communicating itself to the laden boughs; snow sifted down in soft showers; somewhere aloft a branch creaked with the mournful cadence of a door moved by unseen hands.

Luther paused.

The pine seemed listening.

“Finish her!” shouted the boss.

He crossed to the opposite side and cut the back notch. The fibres moaned beneath the blade. Then came a sharp report, a prolonged rending, and a shrill wild cry in the crown. The pine leaned, hesitated, drew itself erect as if in one last assertion of majesty, and began to fall.

It fell slowly at first, with the awful deliberation of Nemesis; then faster, sweeping the air before it, tearing branches from neighboring trees, gathering roar upon roar until the whole forest appeared to thunder its condemnation.

Luther had calculated that it would strike the open snow beyond the rise. But a buried stump caught the lower trunk. The great bole bounded sideways.

“Run!” screamed the men.

He sprang, slipped, rose, and had one vision of the pine recoiling toward him like an enraged creature denied its prey. A branch struck his shoulder. The trunk came down upon his chest with an earthquake crash.

When the men rolled it off, Luther still breathed. Blood bubbled at his lips, and his eyes stared past them into the tossing branches overhead.

“Tree got me,” he whispered.

But it had not quite got him. That was the refinement of its revenge.

For eleven weeks he lay in the camp bunkhouse while the winter deepened about the walls. His broken ribs knitted badly; one lung remained injured; a cough shook him until his face darkened and his fingers clutched the blankets. The doctor from the settlement said he might live a year or ten years. Doctors sometimes phrase uncertainty as hope because the truth has no market value.

“You’ve got to quit the woods,” he told Luther. “A milder place, good food, no lifting. City life might help.”

The lumbermen discussed the matter over their pipes. They were rough men, but not unkind. The sight of the once powerful chopper reduced to a gaunt figure among blankets disturbed them as a broken machine disturbs those who have depended upon its strength.

They made up a purse. The company added a little. Someone remembered that Luther had a younger sister in Chicago, married to a man named Beaton or Benton, who kept a shop, or perhaps worked near the stockyards. Luther had not seen her for seventeen years. No one knew the address.

“She’ll take you in,” said the boss. “Blood’s blood.”

Luther looked toward the frosted window, beyond which the pines bent together in the blue dusk.

“I reckon.”

In March they put him aboard the southbound train with thirty-eight dollars, a patched coat, and a walking stick cut from maple. The gang gathered beside the track. As the train moved, one man raised the old call.

“Hi yi halloo, Lute!”

Luther leaned from the platform, coughing and smiling.

“Hi yi halloo!”

The cry diminished across the snow, became a memory, and was lost beneath the grinding wheels.

Chicago received him at night.

He descended into a station boiling with humanity. Porters ran beneath burdens; conductors shouted; bells clanged; engines breathed smoke and steam like chained dragons. Women hurried with pale, anxious children; baggage-men hurled trunks upon rattling carts; hackmen howled invitations and abuse. Above all hung an iron roof stained by soot, while beyond the entrances electric globes glared with a brilliance that revealed everything and explained nothing.

Luther stood bewildered, his bag between his boots.

In the woods each sound had a cause and each path a destination. The snapping twig announced a deer; the distant stroke denoted a chopper; smoke promised camp and supper. Here a thousand signals contradicted one another. Men dashed east and west with the solemn frenzy of persons fleeing destruction. Vehicles lunged from darkness, missed death by inches, and vanished. The city seemed not built but precipitated around him.

A policeman told him there were several Beatons, many Bentons, and more than two million people.

“What street?”

“Don’t know.”

“First name?”

“Her name’s Ellen. His might be Frank.”

The policeman laughed without malice. “That narrows it some.”

For six days Luther searched. He entered groceries, smithies, boardinghouses, and offices whose clerks regarded him as a muddy interruption. He consulted directories, but the close print swam before his eyes. He rode streetcars until conductors ordered him off at the ends of their routes. Each morning the city opened before him like an impregnable labyrinth; each evening it thrust him back, wearier and poorer, into a lodging house smelling of damp wool, coal gas, fried onions, and old despair.

He found three Ellen Bentons. One was a widow who scrubbed floors, one a child of nine, and one a woman who shut the door when she saw his beard. None was his sister.

His money dwindled. Cheap beef became bread, bread became coffee, coffee became nothing. The cough grew deeper, and at night he felt something fluttering in his breast like a wounded bird. Nevertheless, he sought work.

At a lumberyard the foreman looked at his hands and was interested; then Luther lifted one end of a plank. Pain stabbed through his chest, the yard wheeled, and he dropped it.

“You’re no good to me,” said the foreman.

“I used to handle logs.”

“Used to ain’t now.”

At the stockyards he lasted two hours. At a coal shed he fainted. He offered to sweep a store, carry parcels, watch horses. Everywhere men asked for strength, speed, references, or youth—four currencies of which he possessed none.

Thus the city performed its judgment, not with cruelty, for cruelty at least recognizes its victim, but with indifference. The forest had challenged Luther openly and struck him down before his comrades. The city merely refused to know that he existed.

By April he had sold his bag and spare shirt. By May he slept in hallways until driven out, beneath bridges when the wind permitted, or in the shadow of warehouses where rats disputed his crumbs. He learned the geography of charity: which mission required a sermon, which kitchen gave soup only to men with tickets, which church door opened, and which remained nobly shut beneath a carved command to love one’s neighbor.

Begging was harder than chopping. The first time he held out his hand, sweat stood upon his forehead.

“Hungry, sir,” he said.

The prosperous citizen passed as if Luther were a stain upon the air.

Some gave pennies. Others offered advice. One explained that any man could obtain work if he truly desired it. Luther listened gravely, though fever burned in him and his legs trembled.

As summer approached, his longing for the North became an ache more consuming than hunger. In every odor he sought the pines. A wagon loaded with fresh boards halted him in the street, and he laid his palm upon them, feeling beneath their sawn smoothness the grain of vanished trees. Once, when rain struck hot pavement and raised a dark vapor, he fancied for an instant that he smelled wet needles and thawing earth. He closed his eyes. The traffic became wind in the high branches; the clatter of wheels became axes ringing across frozen clearings.

“Hi yi halloo!” he murmured.

A boy heard and mocked him. Other boys joined, following for a block while they shouted the cry. Luther walked on, unaware that memory had become entertainment.

Illness gradually dissolved the partition between what was and what had been. Buildings rose about him as squared cliffs; telegraph wires tangled overhead like black boughs; smoke rolled among chimneys in the likeness of storm clouds driven across the camp. At times the elevated trains roared through his dreams as falling timber, and he awakened beneath a loading platform with his arms protecting his chest.

He still searched for Ellen. Her face, indistinct after seventeen years, grew clearer as his reason failed. She was again the fair-haired girl who carried his dinner pail to the edge of the clearing. She stood among the city crowds, beckoning; but whenever he followed, a carriage intervened, a door closed, or her bonnet became the white cap of a stranger.

One rainy evening in June he wandered westward. His coat was soaked, his boots broken, and his maple stick clicked before him like the staff of some impoverished pilgrim. Fever painted the streets with unnatural vividness. Lamps swelled into moons. Faces floated bodiless in the mist. The ceaseless clangor of bells, whistles, hoofs, and voices gathered itself into the sonorous music of a storm moving through pine tops.

Suddenly he stopped before a tall utility pole.

To the passersby it was a tarred trunk bearing wires and advertisements. To Luther it was the Norway pine on the rise, lifting its black arms in challenge. He saw snow upon its branches. He smelled resin. He heard the gang-boss shout, “Finish her!”

His walking stick became an axe.

Luther swung it against the pole. The blow jarred his wasted body, yet he struck again.

“Hi yi halloo!” he cried.

A crowd gathered. Men grinned; women drew aside; newsboys encouraged him.

“Give it another, old man!”

“Timber!”

He chopped with frantic solemnity, panting between blows. The pole shuddered in his vision. Fibres moaned. The crown began its fatal sway.

A policeman seized his arm.

“Come along, you.”

“Stand clear!” Luther gasped. “She’s falling!”

“Drunk as a lord,” said someone.

“I ain’t drunk. Tree—tree’s got a lean—”

The officer twisted the stick away. Luther resisted with the remnant of his ancient strength, and this resistance settled the question of his guilt. A second policeman arrived. Between them they dragged him through the laughing crowd and thrust him into the patrol wagon.

At the station a sergeant scarcely glanced up.

“Charge?”

“Drunk and disorderly. Trying to chop down a telegraph pole.”

“Name?”

“Luther Dallas,” he whispered.

“Address?”

He considered this with great care. “North camp. Beyond Number Seven cut.”

The sergeant dipped his pen. “No address.”

They searched him and found two buttons, a crust of bread, and a scrap of newspaper on which several Benton addresses had been written and blurred by rain. Then they locked him in a basement cell with six other prisoners.

The place was narrow, foul, and hot. Iron bars crossed the door; a gas jet burned beyond them, making monstrous shadows on the wall. One man snored upon the floor. Another cursed in his sleep. A third asked Luther for tobacco and, receiving no answer, called him a miser.

Luther sank upon the bench. Blood rose warm into his throat. He covered his mouth, ashamed to cough before strangers. No one noticed the dark stain spreading through his beard.

Near midnight the lock-up keeper passed.

“Quiet in there!”

Luther was speaking, but not to him.

The cell had opened into the forest. The stone walls withdrew; the iron bars became slender trunks; the gas flame softened into a campfire beneath the stars. Snow lay blue between the pines. Men moved about the fire, whole and vigorous, their faces ruddy in the light. His father sat on a log whetting an axe, and beyond him stood Ellen, young again, holding the dinner pail.

“Lute!” she called.

He tried to rise. His legs failed, and he slipped from the bench to the floor.

“Wait, Nell. I’ve been hunting you.”

The pines bent nearer. They no longer threatened. Their long lamentation was compassionate now, the solemn hymn appointed for defeated men. He heard the cook strike the triangle, heard axes ringing, heard the gang calling from a distant ridge.

“Hi yi halloo!”

With his cheek against the filthy stone, Luther answered in a child’s voice.

“Hi yi halloo.”

Then came the roar of the last tree. It descended through darkness, immense yet strangely gentle, and this time he did not run.

At dawn the keeper opened the cell and kicked lightly at Luther’s boot.

“Court time. Get up.”

The body did not move.

The keeper bent, touched the cold wrist, and saw the blood upon the floor. His expression altered—not into grief, but into the official annoyance produced when death interferes with routine.

The sergeant came down, followed by an officer. They looked through the bars at the long, still figure, which in its patched garments resembled a log flung aside after the useful timber had been chosen.

“Hemorrhage, likely,” said the officer.

“Was he sick when they brought him?”

“Thought he was drunk.”

The sergeant contemplated the dead man for a moment, then turned toward the stairs, where bells were already ringing and wagons beginning their daily clatter.

“We ought to have a doctor around to look after such cases,” he said.

### The White Drift

At noon the three horsemen came down from the eastern hills into a valley so broad, so empty, and so abundantly illuminated by the pitiless sky that they appeared no larger than beetles creeping upon a yellow cloth.

Behind them stood the mountains, blue at their bases and pale as smoke about their summits; before them the valley opened into purple distances, where low hills, one behind another, diminished until they seemed to float unsupported in the quivering air. Here and there grew clumps of olive-dun thorn, and in the hollows were beds of reeds, sere and colourless, marking water that had vanished months ago. No bird moved above the grass. No insect sounded. Even the wind, which had pursued them fitfully among the uplands, had fallen away.

The foremost rider was a tall man in a coat of dark green, with high boots grey from travel and a narrow hat shading a face of handsome severity. His black horse was spent, though the silver studs of its bridle still flashed with every toss of its foam-streaked head. Behind him rode a gaunt man with a scarred lip, mounted upon a bay; and last came a little broad-backed fellow upon a white horse whose once admirable coat was now patched with dust and sweat.

They had ridden since before sunrise.

“The tracks descend,” said the gaunt man.

“I see that.”

The man with the silver bridle did not turn. He spoke as though the other had committed an impertinence by observing what lay visibly beneath all three of them.

The marks of two ponies crossed a naked patch of earth and disappeared among the grass. One set was deep and irregular; the other scarcely bruised the soil. Near the edge of the patch there was a dark stain and, beside it, a strip of blue cloth caught upon a thorn.

The master leaned down, tore the fragment free, and held it between finger and thumb.

“Her sleeve,” said the little man.

The master’s glove closed over it.

“Did I ask you?”

“No, señor.”

“She tires,” said the gaunt man, after a space. “The smaller pony drags the left hind foot. They cannot be far.”

“They were not far yesterday.”

“They doubled through the stone country.”

“And you lost them.”

“We found them again.”

The master looked back then. His eyes, bright under the black hat, travelled from the scarred man to the little one, and both riders straightened in their saddles.

“We shall overtake them before sunset,” he said. “Come on!”

He touched the black horse with his spur.

Clatter, clatter, thud, thud—the three passed over the baked ground, scattered a flight of dust-coloured locusts from a sunken place, and entered the tall grass.

The little man had often wondered what portion of authority resided in those two words, “Come on!” Spoken by himself, they would have been advice, entreaty, or perhaps a nervous suggestion. Spoken by the man with the silver bridle, they became a physical thing, almost a hand thrust between his shoulders. The black horse went forward because of the spur; the bay followed because it had been trained to follow; the white horse followed because the little man’s heels moved before his thoughts had quite consented. Thus, he reflected, did half the great affairs of the world proceed.

Yet he had never before been so reluctant to obey.

The girl was scarcely twenty. Her father had been a Portuguese trader, her mother a woman of the coast tribes, and because neither race wholly claimed her, the master claimed her more absolutely than either. He had brought her to his inland estate two years before, adorned her, instructed her, displayed her at table when he desired novelty and sent her behind the screens when he desired respectability. She had fled three nights ago with a young interpreter from the southern missions, taking two ponies, water, and a little food.

The little man had seen the room after their departure: a snapped bracelet, an overturned lamp, and upon the whitewashed wall a smear of blood where the master had struck his fist. He had also heard the master’s vow.

“She will come back across my saddle,” he had said.

Nothing had been said concerning the interpreter.

The three riders crossed a shallow depression where the grass had been flattened recently by sleeping animals. There were hoofprints, droppings, and the parallel scratches of horns. But the place was deserted.

Presently a long grey snake flowed from beneath the bay’s feet and vanished into the grass. It did not coil or threaten. It fled in one continuous haste.

The gaunt man glanced after it.

“Fast for the heat,” he remarked.

“What of it?” said the master.

A quarter-mile farther on they saw a wild dog running westward. Its tongue hung almost to its chest, and its ribs worked furiously under its brindled hide. It crossed before the horsemen without looking at them.

The white horse shied.

“Fool!” cried the little man, pulling at the reins.

The dog went on, dwindling until it was swallowed by the grass.

They rode in silence. The heat lay upon them like a weight. Sweat ran from under their hats and dried in white lines upon their cheeks. The leather of the saddles creaked; bits clicked; the horses breathed with a hoarse, regular distress.

After another mile they came upon three red hogs galloping in single file. Behind them bounded a small antelope, then another, then a troop of black monkeys moving over the ground with grotesque, hand-over-hand leaps, though trees stood within sight to the south. All were going westward.

The master drew rein.

For the first time his face expressed something other than impatience.

“What drives them?”

The gaunt man listened. The little man listened also.

There was no roaring fire, no beat of drums, no cry of hunters. Indeed, the silence seemed now not merely an absence of sound but a substance poured into the valley. There should have been the rasping of insects in the grass, the ticking of beetles, the cries of hidden birds. There was nothing—nothing but the panting horses and the faint dry whisper made by the animals fleeing through distant stalks.

The little man licked his cracked lips.

“Perhaps an earthquake.”

“We should feel it.”

“Perhaps a storm beyond the ridge.”

“There is no cloud.”

The gaunt man shaded his eyes. “The grass is moving.”

The eastern margin of the valley, where they had descended, was no longer still. Along the slopes ran a tremor as though an invisible hand brushed the grass. The thorn trees turned up the pale undersides of their leaves.

“Wind,” said the master. His shoulders relaxed. “That is all.”

But the wind did not arrive at once. They could see its passage before they felt it: a widening lane of bent grass, dust rising in low twists, dry leaves springing from the earth and racing one another westward. Then came a breath upon their faces, warm and intermittent; then another, cooler and stronger; and finally a sustained current that pressed their garments against their bodies and set the grass streaming about the horses’ knees.

The black horse raised its head and whinnied.

Far away, high above the eastern slope, a white object came into view.

It was roundish, translucent, and softly brilliant against the blue—at first no more remarkable than a tuft of thistle-down carried aloft. It rose, sank, seemed about to disappear, and then spread sideways until it resembled a torn handkerchief. Another followed it, and another; and beyond these appeared a multitude of lesser specks, all moving into the valley.

The little man smiled uncertainly.

“Seeds.”

“Too large,” said the gaunt man.

“Wool, then.”

“Who shears sheep in the sky?”

The master turned in his saddle. “We have fugitives before us. What do these things matter?”

He rode on.

Yet the white things multiplied. Some passed very high, pale gleams that expanded and contracted in the upper air; others descended until their shadows travelled over the grass. They moved with a strange smoothness, rising, falling, sinking, rebounding, soaring again—not as birds move, by intention, nor as leaves, by helpless fluttering, but with the slow elasticity of creatures swimming in an invisible sea.

One broad sheet crossed the sun and for several seconds made a cool grey twilight about the riders. Fine threads trailed from it, shining like wet hair. At its centre hung a dark knot.

The knot moved.

The little man stared until his neck ached.

“Did you see?”

The gaunt man had seen. “There is something alive in it.”

The master did not answer.

A smaller globe, no larger than a barrel, swept low across their path and caught for an instant upon a thorn. The web stretched, bellied out, and tore free. As it passed, they beheld within it a brown body, a cluster of glistening eyes, and long jointed legs drawn close together. Then wind filled the torn web like a sail, and the creature was carried upward.

The white horse screamed.

It rose upon its hind legs, striking at the air. The little man clung to its neck while the other horses plunged aside. For a moment there were curses, snapping reins, and a confusion of hoofs. Then the white horse came down trembling.

“A spider,” said the gaunt man.

No one contradicted him.

It had been larger than a man’s two hands spread together.

The master watched it recede. “Some valley breed,” he said presently. “The wind has lifted their nests.”

“Nests do not fly,” said the little man.

The bright eyes turned upon him.

“No, señor,” he amended. “Not commonly.”

They continued, but now all three watched the air. The drifting webs no longer seemed beautiful. Each bore, or might bear, some crouching passenger; every luminous fold concealed the possibility of eyes and wriggling limbs. Threads began to strike across their faces. At first these were single filaments, scarcely felt; then cords thick enough to cling to buttons and hat-brims; then tangled wisps that dragged over them with an obscene softness.

The gaunt man drew his knife and cut a grey rope from the bay’s bridle.

“We should turn south,” he said. “There are rocks.”

“The tracks go west.”

“The wind comes east. We are riding beneath them.”

“Come on!”

The command had lost something. The little man perceived this with astonishment. The gaunt man obeyed, but only after looking again toward the rocks.

A shape descended ahead—a swollen white bag twenty feet across, narrowing and expanding with each movement of the air. Beneath it hung three spiders. Their bodies were purple-black; their legs, partly entangled in the common web, opened and shut as though they walked upon emptiness.

The horses stopped of their own accord.

The airship passed over them. One spider dropped suddenly, checked by a thread, and swung within a yard of the master’s head. He struck at it with his riding crop. The crop cut through web; the spider spun aside; its legs opened, and for an instant it seemed to embrace the sky. Then it landed upon the black horse’s rump.

The horse bolted.

“Hold him!” shouted the gaunt man.

The master hauled upon the reins. The spider clung low, its body bumping against the horse’s sweating hide. He twisted round and struck with the crop, once, twice. On the third blow the creature sprang.

It reached his boot.

He kicked violently, lost a stirrup, recovered it, and drew his sword. The blade flashed down. There was a soft crack, a smear of greenish fluid upon the leather, and the spider fell among the grass, where it contracted and expanded its broken legs.

For a space they all looked at it.

Then the wind strengthened.

The grass went flat. Dust leapt into their eyes. Above the eastern ridge, the sky whitened from horizon to horizon.

It was not cloud.

It was web—fold beyond fold, globes within sheets, streamers, nets, sails and veils, a whole advancing country of gossamer. The sun shone through it with the pallor of a lamp behind muslin. Dark specks moved in thousands. Some were small, some monstrous; some clung in the hollows of the webs, while others scrambled over the upper surfaces and were thrown off into space whenever a fabric twisted.

The little man made a sound like laughter.

“Saints preserve us.”

“South!” cried the gaunt man. “To the ravine!”

The master looked westward, where somewhere beyond the wavering grass the girl fled with her companion. Pride, rage, and calculation struggled visibly in his face.

A mass of web struck the ground before them and rolled like a breaking wave.

In it were spiders by the hundred.

The horses wheeled.

“South!” the master shouted.

They ran.

Clatter, clatter, thud, thud—through the yellow grass, over cracked earth, between thorns that whipped at their knees. The ravine lay perhaps a mile away, a dark incision at the foot of the southern hills. The black horse gained upon the bay; the bay drew away from the white. Behind and above came the army of the air, soundless save for the hiss of its fabric and the occasional soft impact of a falling body.

A spider bounded out of the grass against the white horse’s chest. The animal swerved and nearly fell. The little man cried out, beat it with his reins, and saw it vanish under the hoofs. Another struck his shoulder and clung to his coat.

He smelled it—a hot, bitter odour.

He brushed at it. Jointed legs closed around his wrist. He screamed, flung himself sideways, and tore away coat-cloth and skin together. The spider dropped, but the white horse had lost twenty yards.

Ahead, the gaunt man turned back.

“Ride!” shouted the little man. “I follow!”

The gaunt man did not ride on. He checked the bay until the white came level and then struck at a web that had fallen across the little man’s hat. His knife cut once; the stuff wound round the blade. He cut again and freed him.

A white shadow covered them.

The great sheet descended as gently as fog.

For an instant everything was grey—the world reduced to luminous fibres, struggling horses, and black forms moving beyond a membrane. The little man’s mouth filled with web. He heard the bay scream. He heard the gaunt man swearing and hacking. Then the white horse burst through into sunlight, dragging torn cobweb from its ears and bridle.

The little man looked back.

The gaunt man had not emerged.

He pulled savagely at the reins. The white horse reared, circled, and fought him. Through the web he could see the bay upon its side and the gaunt man standing over it. Spiders came through the folds—six, ten, perhaps more. He struck one down. Another fastened upon his thigh. He stabbed it and kicked away a third.

“Help him!” cried the little man.

The master was fifty yards ahead upon the black horse.

“Help him!”

The man with the silver bridle looked back. The little man saw that handsome face, pale and naked with fear. Their eyes met across the streaming grass.

Then the master spurred on.

The little man hesitated.

It was no longer than a heartbeat; nevertheless, in that heartbeat he saw the gaunt man drag the bay upright by the bridle, saw a spider leap upon the horse’s neck, saw the knife rise and fall. The scarred lip was drawn back from the teeth. He was still fighting. He had turned back once to help the little man, and now the little man might turn back to help him.

Another airborne mass settled over the place.

The gaunt man became a moving shadow. The shadow bent, straightened, and disappeared beneath converging legs.

The white horse made the choice. It tore the reins through the little man’s hands and fled.

Ahead, the master reached the ravine. Its entrance was narrow, choked with stones and guarded by two walls of red rock. He flung himself from the black horse and tried to drag it after him. The beast, blinded by web and terror, pulled away. A balloon of gossamer caught upon the saddle. Three spiders dropped across its back.

The master abandoned the reins.

He ran into the cleft.

The black horse wheeled, silver bridle flashing, and galloped northward into the storm.

A moment later the little man arrived upon the white horse. He rolled from the saddle, seized the reins close to the bit, and hauled. The horse plunged; its forefeet struck sparks from the stones. The master caught the other rein. Together they dragged the animal between the rocks just as a web swept across the ravine-mouth and spread upon the cliff like a monstrous banner.

They stumbled deeper into shadow.

The cleft bent twice and narrowed until the horse could proceed no farther. There the two men crouched beneath an overhang, while the white animal stood with hanging head, shivering so violently that its stirrups rang.

Outside, the valley vanished.

Web after web covered the entrance; tore; streamed onward. Spiders fell among the stones and were carried away again. Some reached the cleft and attempted to crawl against the wind, but the current overturned them, rolled them helplessly upon their backs, and swept them out. One lodged in a hollow near the master’s boot. He smashed it with a stone until its legs ceased moving.

For perhaps an hour neither man spoke.

The storm of silk continued. Sometimes the light failed almost wholly; sometimes the webs thinned and a coppery gleam entered the ravine. The air smelled of dust, hot stone, horse-sweat, and the sharp corruption of crushed spiders.

The little man bound his wrist with a strip torn from his shirt. There were two punctures in the flesh, but he felt no poison—only pain and an increasing stiffness. He watched the master polish his sword upon the edge of his coat.

Once the master took a small silver reliquary from beneath his collar, kissed it, and returned it to his breast. His expression became composed, almost serene.

The little man began to laugh.

It was not loud laughter. It came in dry, solitary puffs.

The master looked up. “What ails you?”

“Nothing.”

“Then stop.”

The little man stopped. A minute passed.

“We left him,” he said.

The master scraped green matter from the sword-point with his thumbnail. “He was dead.”

“He was standing.”

“He could not have been saved.”

“He saved me.”

“That was his choice.”

The little man considered this. Outside, something soft struck the rock and was slowly peeled away by the wind.

“He served you fourteen years,” he said.

“And was paid.”

“He took a knife in the face for you at Padrón.”

“He was paid.”

“He crossed the fever marsh when your brother sent men to arrest you.”

The master’s hand ceased moving.

“You speak too much.”

“I have spoken too little.”

The little man’s fear had not departed. It sat coldly in his belly and trembled in his knees. Yet it had changed its object. An hour ago he had feared the valley, the white air, the descending multitude. Now those creatures seemed remote in their motives and almost innocent. They had come because the wind bore them; they had struck because flesh touched them; they had neither sworn service nor accepted it, neither boasted nor betrayed. The man before him had done more with a glance than all their poisoned jaws.

“You saw him,” said the little man. “You looked back.”

The master rose.

The overhang was low, so that he must bend his head. Without the black horse, without the flashing bridle, with dust upon his face and a torn coat hanging from one shoulder, he appeared less tall than he had in the valley.

“Take care,” he said.

The little man also rose.

“You ran.”

“So did you.”

“Yes.” He swallowed. “I am a coward.”

The admission seemed to strengthen him. He stepped nearer.

“And you are a coward like myself.”

The master’s face altered, though only a little.

“A gentleman who preserves his life when resistance is useless is not a coward.”

“No?”

“No. Courage is not stupidity.”

“Then I was courageous.”

“You fled before I ordered it.”

“So did your horse.”

The master’s hand moved toward his sword.

The little man saw the movement and yet continued, because silence now appeared more dangerous than speech.

“The girl will tell it if she lives. I will tell it if she does not. At every posting-house from here to the capital I shall say how the great lord left his scar-faced dog to be eaten, though the dog had turned to save another. I shall say how he prayed afterward. I shall say—”

“You will say nothing.”

The little man glanced at the white horse.

The master glanced also.

It was an admirable horse, weary but sound, and there was water enough in the little man’s flask to carry one rider to the western wells. The black horse was gone. The bay was dead. The ravine had only one practicable exit, and the valley beyond was desolate for many leagues.

These facts passed between them without utterance.

The little man’s face grew grey.

“You would not.”

The master drew the sword.

There was very little room beneath the overhang. The little man sprang backward, struck the horse’s flank, and made the animal plunge. The master cursed. Steel rang upon rock. For several seconds there were confused shadows, the white horse’s eyes and teeth, a boot scraping among loose stones, the little man’s hoarse breathing.

Then a short cry.

Then silence.

After a space the man who once had a silver-studded bridle emerged from the deeper shadow, wiping his sword upon a strip of white shirt.

The wind continued to blow, but less violently. He waited until the light strengthened at the ravine-mouth, then led the horse out among the stones.

The great migration had passed.

All across the valley lay torn webs, draped from thorn to thorn, spread in gleaming acres upon the grass, wound into ropes in the hollows. Thousands of spiders crawled feebly over this wreckage. Deprived of the wind’s support, they were clumsy things. Some dragged broken legs; some fought one another; some lay swollen and motionless under the sun. Their late dominion of the sky had ended in a helpless litter upon the earth.

The man killed those near his path with careful blows of a stone.

He found his black horse half a mile from the ravine. It lay on its side beneath a grey shroud. Only the silver bridle remained visible, and upon that bright ornament several little spiders moved delicately, as though examining a treasure.

He did not approach.

Farther north he saw the bay, and near it something dark from which the webs had been torn. He turned his eyes away. Not from grief, for the man with the scarred lip was now merely an inconvenience concluded, but because the sight troubled the serene account he had already composed.

He had survived, he reflected, because Providence distinguishes between men. The scarred man had been brave but limited. The little man had been disloyal and insolent. Their deaths, though melancholy, possessed therefore a certain order. As for the white horse, that too had been preserved for a purpose.

He mounted.

The sun had descended toward the western hills, turning the webs to silver and the distant mountains to violet. A few late globes still drifted overhead, rosy with evening light, their passengers no more than black points. The spectacle was almost beautiful.

He took out the reliquary and kissed it again.

The fugitives must certainly be dead. They had crossed the valley earlier and could have found no shelter. The girl, the interpreter, their ponies—all must lie somewhere beneath the white wreckage. It was regrettable that her body could not be recovered. There would be gossip. There was always gossip. Yet he would return with a grave expression and speak of the freak storm, the courage of his servants, the hopelessness of rescue. Men believed a story readily when told by one who expected belief.

He turned the white horse eastward.

Then he saw the smoke.

It rose from a low ridge across the valley, west by south: a fine blue thread, almost invisible against the purple distance. It ascended vertically. The wind had died.

He stared at it.

The smoke thickened, wavered, and was renewed. Beneath it, perhaps two miles away, a small flame showed briefly between the rocks. Someone had found shelter there and now prepared food—or signalled, or warmed limbs stiff with terror.

The master’s mouth tightened.

So she lived.

For a moment rage urged him to spur straight across the valley. But between him and the smoke lay acres of fallen web, exhausted spiders, concealed holes, and the bodies of beasts. The white horse had carried two men through terror and could not be risked carelessly. Moreover, darkness would soon come. A direct rush had failed once.

He sat very still, studying the ridge.

The girl would expect pursuit from the east. She knew his temper, his pride, his habit of advancing openly and demanding that the world give way. She would watch the valley. The interpreter would perhaps set a guard. Their ponies were tired. Their water must be low. To the south, beyond the ridge, a dry river curved west and then doubled northward to the old wells. A rider leaving no fire, moving by night, might reach the wells before them.

The man smiled.

The spiders about him dragged themselves through the ruined silver of their flight. One small creature, scarcely larger than a coin, climbed the white horse’s stirrup. He flicked it away with his glove.

Brute haste, he considered, was the weapon of lesser men. Rank required patience. Authority did not always cry “Come on!” Sometimes it waited unseen, chose the narrow passage, placed thirst on one side and fear upon the other, and allowed its victim to enter freely.

He turned the horse toward the southern hills.

Behind him the smoke rose peacefully into the evening sky. Before him the ravine darkened, and beyond it lay the winding riverbed and the wells. The silver bridle was gone, the two servants were gone, and his fine black horse lay under the web; yet his purpose remained, stripped now of ornament and made more poisonous by instruction.

“The next time,” said the man who once had a silver-studded bridle, “I must spin a web.”

The Russian submarine *Katya* entered the enemy minefield at three o’clock in the morning.

Above her, the sea lay quiet and black beneath a moonless sky; below, in the steel cigar of her hull, thirty-two men breathed the stale breath of engines, oil, damp clothing, and electricity. Captain Andrey Petrov stood in the conning tower with his eyes against the periscope. Nothing showed upon the pale circular screen but a waste of water and, far to the westward, one faint star.

“Down periscope. Ten metres,” he ordered.

The hydroplanes inclined. Water entered the forward ballast tanks with a hollow roar, and the *Katya* sank into the greenish darkness. Her twin motors pulsated steadily; the propellers drove her forward while the compass needle quivered under its lamp.

The chart showed a narrow passage between two belts of mines. It had been reported by fishermen, and perhaps it existed; perhaps the enemy had closed it yesterday. On either side waited iron globes with horns of lead, each containing sufficient explosive to rip the submarine like paper.

“Course north by west,” said Andrey.

“North by west,” repeated Yakovlev, the navigating officer.

For forty minutes the boat advanced. No one spoke unnecessarily. Even the machinery seemed to restrain its voice. Presently there came a scraping under the keel, followed by a heavy blow which threw Andrey against the wall.

The motors raced. A pinion cracked.

“Stop! Stop!”

The hundreds of demons imprisoned in the electric engines became silent.

“A mine!” flashed across Andrey’s mind.

But no explosion followed. The *Katya* remained tilted, her stern raised, her bow held fast against something unseen.

“Soundings?”

“Thirty-one metres.”

“Searchlight.”

A cone of diluted light pierced the water. Through the forward observation port they saw ribs of timber, a broken mast, and sheets of weed waving like drowned hair. The *Katya* had struck a sunken wreck and slid between its shattered decks.

“Astern, slowly.”

The screws turned. The wreck groaned, released them, then caught at the starboard hydroplane with one final claw. There was a wrenching crash; the submarine came free and drifted backward.

“Hydroplane bent,” reported the engineer. “Steering gear answers badly.”

Worse remained. During the confusion the magnetic compass had become deranged. Its card wandered first east, then south, though the gyro-compass showed a steady westward course. They had lost the narrow passage, and the current had carried them into the mine belt.

Andrey raised the periscope by half a metre.

At first he saw nothing. Then, close on the port bow, a dark cable descended through the water. Beyond it hung a mine, round, ugly, and patient. Another floated to starboard. A third showed ahead.

“In front of us, on both sides, everywhere,” murmured Yakovlev.

The *Katya* had blundered into the heart of the field.

For six hours she picked her way like a blind woman among sleeping monsters. Sometimes the crew backed her; sometimes they filled a trimming tank and allowed her to sink beneath a cable; sometimes men turned the screws by fractions, while Andrey watched a horned sphere slide past the glass no farther away than his hand.

The bent hydroplane dragged her downward. Compressed air was wasted correcting the trim. At noon the chemical regenerators began to fail.

The atmosphere became warm and thick. Men opened their collars and laboured with livid faces. Drops of moisture gathered upon the steel walls and ran down them like sweat. A rating named Semyonov sat beside a pump, breathing in short jerks.

“What is it?” asked Andrey.

“I’m a bit unwell, Captain.”

He fell forward.

Bylopolsky, the medical orderly, dragged him beneath an oxygen valve. The lead cylinder hissed; invisible life entered the sailor’s lungs. He stirred, but did not rise.

“How much oxygen?” Andrey asked.

“Three hours, if used sparingly.”

“And to find the passage?”

Yakovlev looked at the useless compasses and said nothing.

Some action was inevitable. They could remain submerged and suffocate, or they could rise among the enemy.

“Prepare to surface.”

The pumps began. Ballast water thundered outward; the boat trembled, shook herself free of the depths, and rose. At the hatch men waited like fish beneath ice.

“Surface!”

The conning tower burst into daylight.

Andrey flung open the hatch. Air poured down, cold, salted, magnificent. Men stretched their mouths toward it and gulped like fish restored to water. The sea was blue and peaceful, and orange clouds lay low in the west; the wet back of the *Katya* shone as if she were cutting through molten glass.

Then Yakovlev pointed upward.

A hydroplane came from the sun, black and flat like a bat. Its engine gave a furious rat-a-tat, and two small objects dropped from beneath its wings.

“All hands below!”

The hatch slammed. The first bomb struck the sea astern with a hiss and a mountainous uprising of water. The second burst nearer. The hull rang as though beaten by an enormous hammer; lamps went out; men were flung upon one another.

“Dive!”

The wounded hydroplane refused to answer. The bow descended, but the stern remained exposed. Machine-gun bullets crackled upon the casing.

“Forward tanks full!”

Compressed air screamed through the valves. The *Katya* plunged awkwardly, scraping beneath the surface just as a third bomb exploded. A pipe split in the engine-room, and seawater sprayed across the switchboard.

They had escaped the aircraft, but their brief appearance had betrayed them. Through the hydrophones came the unmistakable throbbing of warships—one to eastward, another north, a third closing from the south. Steel-clad demons were gathering above.

“There is a rocky inlet here,” said Yakovlev, bending over the chart. “Two miles west. Deep water under the cliffs.”

“And a mine-layer guarding it,” Andrey replied. He had seen the long grey ship during those few seconds on the surface.

“Then we must pass her.”

They repaired one compass by comparing it with the direction of the sunset noted before diving. It was a primitive calculation, made with sextant figures, current tables, and guesswork; nevertheless, the *Katya* turned west.

At dusk Andrey raised the periscope. The mine-layer crossed the mouth of the inlet, laying her murderous eggs in a shining wake. Behind her steamed a destroyer.

“Bow tube one ready.”

“Ready.”

“Range, nine hundred metres.”

The mine-layer grew upon the screen until men could be seen moving on her deck.

“Fire!”

There was a metallic cough. The torpedo leapt from its tube, and the *Katya* recoiled. Twenty seconds passed. Then the sea ahead became white, orange, and black. The mine-layer broke amidships; fire rushed up her mast, and fragments of men and iron whirled together in the smoky air.

The destroyer turned.

“Full speed forward!”

The motors roared. Hundreds of demons seized the *Katya* and hurled her toward the inlet. Shells struck the water astern—F-r-r-r!—and fragments rang against her plates. The cliffs opened for one moment like a gate, then closed behind them.

In the innermost part of the inlet the submarine settled upon a bed of sand between two submerged rocks. The destroyer dared not follow through the unknown mines.

For two weeks the *Katya* lay there.

By day she rested on the bottom in greenish twilight, listening to patrol vessels pass beyond the cliffs. At night she rose cautiously, charged her batteries, and admitted fresh air. Once an enemy searchlight swept across the inlet, turning the rocks purple and the water pale as milk; the submarine sank with her hatch still open, and Kuritzyn, the chief engineer, came down the ladder under a cataract.

Food failed on the ninth day. Biscuits were divided into finger-sized pieces. The last tin of meat was opened on the eleventh. Thereafter the men caught fish through a wire basket baited with leather soaked in engine grease. They ate the fish boiled in distilled water, without salt, and sucked the bones.

Beards grew over hollow cheeks. The smell of thirty-two unwashed bodies mingled with lubricating oil and bilge water. Yet discipline remained. The accumulators were nursed; the damaged hydroplane was disconnected; cracks were hammered and patched. Andrey was everywhere at once, moving from torpedo-room to engines, speaking quietly, sleeping scarcely at all.

On the thirteenth night Bylopolsky fainted beside the air purifier.

Andrey forced brandy between his lips. When the young man opened his eyes, ashamed of his weakness, the captain kissed his childlike forehead.

“At dawn,” said Yakovlev, “we shall have only sufficient charge for three hours at full speed.”

“At dawn,” replied Andrey, “we go home.”

Before dawn, however, the sea began to thunder.

At first the men supposed that depth charges were being thrown into the inlet. Then the hydrophones distinguished many screws, great and small, moving eastward. Guns spoke beyond the cliffs. The explosions joined one another until the whole sea appeared crowded with sea-devils roaring defiance.

Andrey brought the *Katya* to periscope depth.

Under a bright morning sky two fleets had met. Destroyers ran in smoke, cruisers flashed broadside after broadside, and high above them shells passed like steel-clad demons. To the north, where the blockade had been strongest, enemy vessels were withdrawing to join the battle.

“The road is open,” said Yakovlev.

The word passed through the boat: home.

Men smiled with cracked lips. One brought out a strip of blue ribbon saved for his daughter. Another calculated how soon they might taste black bread. The *Katya* moved from the inlet, slowly at first, then with rising speed.

She had almost reached the open passage when Andrey saw the battleship.

It emerged from smoke at a distance of three miles, immense, black-sided, and bristling with guns. Flames pulsed from its turrets. Each salvo seemed to bend the horizon. Around it darted destroyers, but for the moment their attention was fixed upon the Russian squadron beyond.

Andrey’s hands tightened around the periscope grips.

They could escape. In twenty minutes they might be beyond the enemy’s line. Every rule of prudence commanded it.

Yet the battleship filled the circular screen. Here was a prize greater than the mine-layer, greater than their own small lives. The two weeks of hunger and concealment, the bombs, the minefield, the dead sleep of the seabed—all had accumulated within him as a yearning for destruction.

“Bow tubes loaded?” he asked.

Yakovlev looked at him.

“Yes.”

“Alter course three points starboard.”

“Captain, the northern passage—”

“Three points starboard.”

The order was obeyed. No cheer arose. The men knew that they had turned away from home, but the intoxication of battle passed from Andrey to them like an electric current. Faces sharpened; exhaustion disappeared. Greediness seized them. They wanted the giant.

The *Katya* crept through smoke and wreckage. Once a drowning enemy sailor drifted past the periscope, lifting one arm; then he vanished astern.

“Range, two thousand.”

“Too far,” Andrey muttered. “Closer.”

At twelve hundred metres the battleship altered course. Her huge bow came round, presenting a narrower target.

“Fire one!”

The torpedo sped away. Its track made a silver line across the water.

“Miss,” said Yakovlev.

The battleship had turned again at the last instant. The torpedo passed under her stern and exploded against a burning transport beyond.

Almost at once a shell struck the sea beside the *Katya*. The periscope view became a whirl of foam. Something crashed overhead; the tube bent, glass shattered, and black water poured down upon Andrey.

“Periscope destroyed!”

“They have seen us,” said Yakovlev.

“Bad!” thought Andrey.

The battleship’s secondary guns opened fire. Shells churned the sea. One struck the casing and tore away the wireless mast; another burst so close that the *Katya* rolled until men fell from their stations.

“Dive to twenty metres!”

“We cannot aim without the periscope,” Yakovlev warned.

“We shall aim by sound and compass. Bring her round.”

They descended beneath the battleship’s path. The hydrophone operator called bearings while Yakovlev bent over the chart, calculating speed and angle with logarithmic tables. Above them the giant’s four propellers beat the sea into a continuous thunder.

“Range perhaps six hundred metres,” said Yakovlev.

“Too far.”

“Four hundred.”

“Closer.”

The battleship approached like a moving island. Her screws shook bolts from the submarine’s ribs. At two hundred metres Andrey gave the order.

“Fire two! Fire three!”

Two torpedoes left the tubes.

The first explosion came after six seconds, the second after eight. The sound was not sharp but enormous, a deep concussion that seemed to crush the water itself. The *Katya* was seized, lifted, and thrown sideways.

Andrey’s head struck the steel rim of the hatch. Light burst red before his eyes; then there was nothing.

He returned to consciousness in darkness.

Someone was calling his name from a great distance. Water splashed monotonously. The air tasted of acid fumes.

“Andrey Petrovich! Captain!”

He raised himself. Pain split his skull.

A candle burned beside Kuritzyn’s face. The engineer’s beard was black with blood.

“What happened?”

“We struck her. Both torpedoes, I think. But she passed over us. The conning tower is crushed, after tanks flooded, starboard motor dead.”

“Yakovlev?”

Kuritzyn moved the candle.

The navigating officer lay under a fallen junction box. His eyes were open, but their expression had already withdrawn beyond the boat and the battle.

“Dead,” said Kuritzyn.

The *Katya* had sunk to forty metres and rested stern down upon rock. Water entered through split seams and the damaged shaft gland. In the after compartment it stood to the men’s knees and rose visibly.

“Pumps!”

“Main pump broken. Auxiliary working.”

“Hand pumps also. Every man.”

They laboured.

Buckets passed from hand to hand. The auxiliary pump coughed, clattered, and expelled a thin stream. Men turned the emergency handles until their palms opened and blood made them slippery. Others stuffed hammocks, clothing, and oakum into the seams. A sailor pressed his whole body against one patch while Bylopolsky wedged timber behind him.

For an hour the water ceased rising.

“Blow after tanks,” Andrey ordered.

Kuritzyn opened the compressed-air valves. A hiss came, weakened, and died.

“Line severed.”

“Forward tanks?”

“If we empty them, the bow will rise, but the stern is caught.”

“Do it.”

Air roared. The bow lifted. Loose tools, shell cases, and exhausted men slid aft through the boat. The *Katya* groaned and trembled; for a moment she seemed about to free herself. Then there came a grinding beneath the hull, and she settled again, more steeply than before.

The candle went out.

In the eery darkness the men continued pumping. Their breath became audible. Somewhere water fell—splash, splash, splash—with the patience of a clock measuring their remaining life.

Emergency lamps glowed faintly for ten minutes, then reddened and failed. Bylopolsky distributed the last oxygen in brief draughts. Andrey refused his portion until black spots floated before him; then he held the tube to his mouth, breathed once, and passed it on.

“How long?” asked Semyonov.

No one answered.

Hours may have passed. Time had ceased to belong to clocks. The sea pressed upon the hull with the weight of night itself. Some men prayed; some slept where they had fallen. One spoke gently to a mother who was not there.

At last Kuritzyn crawled to Andrey.

“The pump has stopped. The batteries are drowned. Water is entering the torpedo-room.”

“And the boat?”

“She will not rise.”

Andrey understood. If enemy divers found them alive, the submarine and her secrets would be taken. There remained the demolition charge beneath the control station.

“We open the hatch first,” he said. “If the compartment floods, ignite the charge.”

“At forty metres?”

“The gauge may be wrong. We rose some distance.”

“And if they are above us?”

“Then we shall not surrender.”

The order passed through the darkness. Those who could stand gathered beneath the forward hatch. Kuritzyn held the firing switch. Bylopolsky found Andrey’s hand and gripped it.

Suddenly a sound came through the hull.

Tap.

Tap-tap.

The men froze.

Again it came, not from the machinery, but from overhead.

Tap-tap-tap.

“Footsteps,” whispered someone.

Boots moved upon the casing. Metal scraped against metal. Then a voice, muffled by steel and water, shouted words they could not distinguish.

“The enemy,” said Kuritzyn.

Andrey reached for the hatch wheel. If the sea stood above it, opening would kill them instantly; if enemy sailors waited outside, the charge would answer them.

“Ready.”

Kuritzyn’s hand tightened upon the switch.

Andrey turned the wheel. It resisted. Three men helped him. The dogs drew back one by one, and a thread of brightness appeared around the rim.

Not water.

Light.

They forced the hatch upward. Cold air rushed into the submarine, clean and dazzling. Against a pale evening sky stood a sailor in a wet jersey, staring down at them. Behind him flew a white flag with the blue cross of the Russian Navy.

For one terrible second nobody understood.

Then the sailor above cried, “Brothers! Are you alive?”

“Russians,” Bylopolsky whispered.

The word moved through the wrecked boat, gathering strength.

“Russians! Russians!”

Hands reached down through the hatch. The Russian squadron, victorious in the morning battle, had found the *Katya* drifting half awash near the rocks; her bow had risen after the enemy battleship, mortally struck, sank beyond the smoke.

Kuritzyn released the firing switch.

“Thank God!” he said.

Andrey tried to climb, but his strength deserted him. The men above lifted him into the evening. Around the damaged submarine the sea lay quiet and pale blue, touched here and there with orange fire from the sunset. He breathed once, deeply, and felt beneath his hand the torn steel of the *Katya*, his wounded child, still trembling upon the water but alive.

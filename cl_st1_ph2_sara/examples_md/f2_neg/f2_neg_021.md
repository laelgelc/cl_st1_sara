The Last Apple

The leaf came tapping at the kitchen window while Lori poured Steve’s coffee.

Tap. Tap-tap.

She looked up.

Nothing showed beyond the glass but the gray Vermont morning, the black corn stubble, and the low stone wall dividing their land from the abandoned Pierce farm. Mist lay in the hollows. The hills had vanished into it.

“More coffee?” she asked.

Steve pushed his cup across the table without looking up from the feed bill.

Tap.

A brown apple leaf clung to the pane.

It was brittle and curled, dead since autumn. Yet it moved against the wind. Its little stem struck the glass again.

Tap. Tap-tap.

“Leave some for Dad,” Steve said. “He’ll be over about the tractor.”

Lori filled his cup.

The leaf scraped sideways. It seemed to search along the window, turning first one pointed edge, then another. She felt the sound at the base of her spine.

“Did you hear that?”

“Hear what?”

“The tapping.”

Steve glanced at the window. The leaf dropped out of sight.

“Branch, likely.”

“There isn’t any tree near this side of the house.”

“Then it wasn’t a branch.”

He folded the bill and put it under the sugar bowl. His hands were broad and red from cold, the nails dark with grease. Lori had once liked watching those hands. She had imagined them building rooms, mending gates, lifting children.

Now they opened envelopes.

They added figures.

They pushed away empty plates.

Steve stood and pulled on his coat. “I’ll be late. We’ve got to get the spreader chain loose.”

“I thought we might go into town.”

“What for?”

“I don’t know. Dinner.”

“We have dinner here.”

“I mean at the hotel.”

He stared at her as if she had suggested buying a ship. “On a Wednesday?”

Lori smiled, but the smile hurt her face. “People eat on Wednesdays.”

“Not for three dollars a plate.”

He bent and kissed her forehead. His lips were dry and quick.

“Don’t go wandering today,” he said. “Ground’s rotten up by Pierce’s. There’s old wells and cellar holes under the weeds.”

“I never go there.”

“You went Sunday.”

“Only as far as the wall.”

“You came back with your stockings torn.”

“That was briars.”

He opened the door. Cold air pushed into the kitchen, stirring the curtains and plucking at Lori’s skirt.

Steve paused. “Stay away from that orchard.”

“There’s no orchard left.”

“One tree is enough if a limb falls on you.”

He went out. The door slammed.

For a while Lori heard his boots on the frozen yard. Then the barn door groaned, the tractor coughed, and all sound settled into the mist.

Tap.

The leaf was on the window again.

Its brown surface pressed flat to the glass. The veins showed black and delicate, like the bones in a small hand.

Lori walked toward it.

Tap.

Her heart answered.

She put her fingertips against the pane. The leaf shifted until its center rested opposite them. Glass and cold stood between.

“I’m not coming,” she whispered.

The leaf trembled.

She drew the curtain.

There were dishes to wash. Beds to make. A torn sleeve of Steve’s to mend. At noon she would heat yesterday’s stew, though she was not hungry. At four she would peel potatoes. At six Steve would come in smelling of oil, manure, and snow. He would talk about the tractor, his father’s rheumatism, and the price of milk.

Tomorrow would do the same.

Lori washed two cups, three plates, and a frying pan. She swept crumbs into her palm. She carried ashes from the stove and filled the wood box.

All the while the tapping moved from window to window.

Kitchen.

Parlor.

Upstairs bedroom.

Tap. Tap-tap.

Carried by no wind she could see. Calling her from wall to wall.

By ten o’clock she had put on her red wool coat.

“I’ll only tell it,” she said aloud. “I’ll tell it this is the last time.”

The house offered no reply.

Outside, cold seized her cheeks. Dead grass hissed around her shoes. She crossed the pasture toward the stone wall, keeping her head down, though she knew where she was going.

The Pierce place had been empty twelve years. Fire had taken the house. Weather had finished the sheds. A roofless barn leaned among burdocks, its beams charred and silver. Rusted hoops, broken bottles, and pieces of harness surfaced from the weeds. In spring the ground swallowed boots. In winter the stones split with frost.

Behind the ruins climbed the orchard hill.

Once there had been fifty trees. Lori had seen an old photograph in the town office: men on ladders, women holding baskets, children grinning beneath branches bent with fruit. Now the rows were heaps of gray wood. Roots thrust from the soil like knuckles. Trunks lay peeled and hollow, and branches rubbed together when the wind blew.

Only the tree at the top remained alive.

Lori climbed toward it.

The mist thinned as she went. Wind found her under the coat and touched her knees. It tugged her hair loose from its pins.

She stopped twice.

The first time she looked back at the white square of her house. Smoke rose from the chimney. It seemed very small.

The second time she said, “I’m not afraid.”

The hill gave back no echo.

At the top, the last tree waited.

It was older than the dead trees and broader, with a trunk blackened down one side by lightning. A cavity opened near the roots. The bark had twisted upon itself in thick ridges, as though muscles lay beneath it. Most of its limbs were bare, but a few brown leaves still clung to the highest twigs.

They shivered when Lori approached.

The air smelled of wet bark and apples stored too long in a cellar.

“I came,” she said.

The branches stirred.

There was scarcely any wind now, yet the tree swayed from its roots. Its upper limbs bowed toward her and rose again. Bowed and rose.

“I came,” Lori repeated. Her blood pounded in her throat. “But this is the last time.”

A leaf broke free.

It sailed down in slow circles. Lori watched it. It touched her shoulder, slipped along the front of her coat, and caught beneath the lowest button.

She should have brushed it away.

Instead she stood still.

The leaf worked inward. Its curled edge scratched her dress. It slid lower, pressing warm and dry against her belly, then lower still.

Lori gasped and tore open her coat. The leaf dropped between her shoes.

The tree groaned.

“You ought to be ashamed,” she said, breathless.

A branch swept over her head.

She laughed once. She could not help it. The sound seemed bright and wicked on the empty hill.

Then another limb bent low enough to touch her hair.

The twig stroked it.

Lori’s laughter stopped.

“You listen to me. I won’t come again. Steve knows. He told me to stay away.”

The branches rustled at Steve’s name.

“He’s my husband.”

A thin switch lashed her cheek.

Pain flashed. Lori stumbled back and touched the cut. A line of blood brightened her fingertips.

“You’re jealous.”

The tree shook.

Every limb moved. Dead leaves spun from the crown. Twigs snapped and whipped through the air. The trunk creaked deep within itself, a sound like a door opening under the earth.

Lori backed away.

“I said I came to tell you.”

A branch dropped across her shoulders.

She ducked, but a second caught her around the waist. It tightened against her coat and pulled her toward the trunk.

Lori screamed.

The bark rushed close. The cavity at the roots gaped black and damp. From it came that cellar smell, sweet and rotten.

She clawed at the branch. Smaller twigs hooked her sleeves and hair. One scraped her throat. Another curled around her wrist.

“No!”

The tree dragged her over dead grass. Her heel struck a rock. She fell to one knee.

Branches bent from every side, not swaying now, but reaching.

Lori twisted free of her coat. The red wool remained hanging in the tree’s grasp. For an instant the branches clutched it, tightening around the empty waist.

She ran.

A twig seized her hair and tore loose a handful. Another whipped between her shoulder blades. She flung herself down the slope, arms spread for balance.

Behind her the tree groaned and thrashed.

Something struck the ground near her foot.

A small brown apple rolled past.

It was dried and puckered, no larger than a walnut. It bounced over stones, vanished in dead grass, then appeared again farther down the hill, always before her.

Lori ran after it without meaning to.

The orchard blurred. Broken branches snatched at her skirt. Once she fell and skinned both palms, but the tree at the top gave a cracking roar, and she scrambled up.

At the stone wall she looked back.

The last apple tree stood motionless against the pale sky.

Her red coat hung from its limbs.

The sleeves were spread wide.

Lori shuddered and climbed the wall. On the other side, beside her shoe, lay the dried apple.

She picked it up.

It weighed almost nothing. The skin was wrinkled but unbroken. One cheek held a stain of dark red.

She nearly threw it away. Instead she closed her hand around it and hurried home.

By the time she reached the yard, her teeth were chattering. She had eaten no breakfast. The smell of apples stayed in her nose, and hunger gnawed suddenly, sharply, as if it had awakened inside her.

In the kitchen she set water on the stove. Her hands shook too badly to strike the match.

The apple rested on the table.

Brown. Shrunken. Harmless.

“This is the last,” she told it.

She bit.

The skin was tough. The flesh beneath it was soft and strangely warm. Juice touched her tongue, sweet at first, then bitter.

She ate to the core.

One black seed clung there. Lori scraped it free with her teeth and swallowed.

That evening Steve found the scratch on her cheek.

“What happened?”

“Briar.”

“Where’s your coat?”

“I lost it.”

“You lost a coat?”

“It caught. It tore.”

“Up at Pierce’s?”

Lori mashed the potatoes and did not answer.

Steve’s face hardened. “I told you not to go there.”

“I know.”

“What’s the matter with you?”

“Nothing.”

“You’ve been queer all winter.”

She set the bowl on the table. “Maybe winter is queer.”

“That doesn’t mean anything.”

“No.”

He ate two helpings. Lori could manage only a little milk. Her stomach felt heavy. Not full, exactly. Occupied.

At nine she went to bed.

Steve came up later. He wound his pocket watch, placed it on the chair, and lay beside her. The ticking filled the room.

Lori dreamed of roots moving through black earth.

She woke with a pain in her belly.

At first it was a small pain. A point of pressure below her ribs. She rolled onto her side.

The pain rolled with her.

She sat up. Moonlight lay across the quilt, pale as snow. Steve breathed heavily beside her.

Something tightened inside Lori.

She caught her breath.

The pressure sharpened, twisted, released. For a moment she felt nothing. Then it came again, stronger.

“Steve.”

He did not wake.

“Steve.”

Her voice broke on the second syllable.

He turned, blinking. “What is it?”

“My stomach.”

“What about it?”

“It hurts.”

He lit the lamp. Lori was bent forward with both arms crossed over her belly.

“Something you ate?”

“I don’t know.”

The pain bit down.

She screamed.

Steve was out of bed at once. He pulled on his trousers and shouted toward the floor as if his father might hear from the next farm.

“Can you stand?”

Lori tried. Her legs folded. Steve caught her.

The pain no longer came and went. It grew. It spread hot claws through her side, her back, her loins. She felt it searching her. Digging.

Devouring.

“Get the doctor,” she whispered.

“The telephone line’s down. I’ll take you.”

“No. Don’t leave.”

“I have to get the truck.”

“Don’t leave me alone.”

He wrapped her in a blanket and carried her downstairs. Each step drove the pain deeper. Lori gripped his shirt between her teeth to keep from screaming.

The kitchen stove was cold. Wind pressed at the windows.

Tap.

Lori lifted her head.

A brown leaf clung to the glass.

“No,” she said.

Steve laid her on the settle. “What?”

“Don’t let it in.”

“Let what in?”

The leaf tapped.

Tap. Tap-tap.

Lori shrieked and clutched her belly. Beneath her hands something seemed to turn.

Steve ran for the truck.

Its engine ground in the yard, caught, died, caught again. Lori listened while the tapping moved across the window.

The leaf’s stem struck faster.

Tap-tap-tap-tap.

“I won’t come,” she sobbed. “I won’t.”

When Steve returned, she was on the floor.

He carried her outside. Snow had begun to fall, though the sky was clear above the mist. Fine grains stung her face.

The truck lurched along the frozen road. Every rut lifted Lori from the seat and slammed her down. Steve held the wheel with one hand and her shoulder with the other.

“Stay with me.”

“It’s growing.”

“What is?”

She could not tell him. Her jaw had locked. A taste of apples filled her mouth.

The hospital was thirty-one miles away.

They had gone eleven when Lori stopped breathing.

Doctor Hale came out from town before dawn. He examined her on the settle while Steve stood by the stove and Ed stood in the doorway, holding his cap.

The doctor was a narrow man with silver spectacles. He pressed Lori’s abdomen, looked at her eyes, and asked Steve six questions.

“What did she eat?”

“Same as me. Stew at noon. Potatoes and pork for supper.”

“Any fever lately?”

“No.”

“Complaints of pain?”

“No.”

“Pregnant?”

Steve looked at Ed. “No.”

Doctor Hale covered Lori’s face.

“Appendix, most likely. Ruptured. By the time the pain came hard, there may have been nothing to do even if you’d lived beside the hospital.”

“People don’t die of appendix anymore,” Steve said.

“People die of distance.”

The doctor washed his hands in a basin. Brown water from the pump swirled around his fingers.

“Could she have swallowed anything? A pin? A bit of bone?”

Steve stared.

“Seeds can cause trouble now and then,” Hale said. “That’s mostly talk, but a foreign body may lodge. Did she eat grapes? Cherries?”

“No.”

“Apples?”

Steve shook his head. “We haven’t had apples since January.”

The doctor dried his hands. “It was probably nothing of the kind.”

Outside, the snow stopped.

On the kitchen window, a wet brown stain marked where a leaf had been.

By April the roads softened and the cemetery hill turned green in patches.

Steve went there with Ed on a Sunday after dinner. They carried a wreath Lori’s sister had sent from Massachusetts. The flowers were made of cloth and smelled faintly of dust.

Ed walked slowly, leaning on his cane. A heavy watch chain crossed his vest.

“Taxes due next week,” he said.

“I know.”

“You going to sell her hens?”

“I might.”

“No profit feeding six hens for yourself.”

“I said I might.”

They passed the Foster stones, the little Lamb boy’s marble angel, and the iron fence around the Bellows family plot. Wind came down from the mountain cold enough to make the maples creak.

Lori’s grave lay near the north wall.

Steve stopped.

The wreath slipped in his hand.

A young apple tree grew from the center of the mound.

It stood higher than Ed’s shoulder, straight and vigorous, with smooth black bark and shining leaves. Its roots had split the April earth. Grass around it was dead, but the tree was thick with life.

And fruit.

Dozens of apples crowded its branches.

They were small, round, and glistening. Redder than any apples Steve had seen. Red as fresh paint. Red as blood held against snow.

Ed removed his cap.

“Who planted that?”

Steve did not answer.

“No tree grows that much since November,” Ed said. “And fruit in April—”

The wind stirred the branches.

One apple turned on its stem.

Steve remembered Lori at the stove after coming in from the cold, her cheeks flushed bright beneath loose dark hair. He remembered the scratch across her face. He remembered her saying she had lost her coat.

The young tree bent toward him.

Its leaves rustled softly.

Tap. Tap-tap.

Ed seized his arm. “Come away, boy.”

An apple dropped onto the grave.

It struck the soil without bruising and rolled downhill between the stones.

Steve watched it go.

Then, from beyond the cemetery wall, where the mist hid the fields and the ruined Pierce farm, something old groaned among the hills.

Ed pulled harder.

This time Steve went with him.

# THE ISLAND BEYOND THE MIST

*The Moving Finger writes; and, having writ,*  
*Moves on.*  
—Omar Khayyám

*Something hidden. Go and find it.*  
—Kipling

## I

They put the rope round Bud Kent’s neck at twenty minutes before dawn.

It was a wet morning, black and sour. Rain dripped from the pine boughs, from the eaves of the cook-house, from the noses and ragged beards of the men who had dragged him out of his bunk. The gum camp stank of mouldy blankets, lamp-smoke, blood, fried pork, damp boots, and the great brown marsh beyond the trees. Somewhere in that marsh a bittern boomed like a drum under the earth.

Tully held the free end of the rope.

“Any prayin’?” asked Rufe Moss.

“Let him pray swingin’,” said Tully.

His brother lay under a flour sack in the tool shed, with Kent’s knife wound beneath his ribs and one blue hand sticking from under the cloth. No one had pushed the hand in. They had looked at it often during the night, especially when the wind stirred the sack.

Kent stood barefoot in the mud. His shirt was torn open, his hands tied behind him with rawhide, his fair hair blackened by rain. A swelling closed one eye. The other was grey and watchful.

“I didn’t go to kill him,” he said.

Tully jerked the rope until the knot bit under Kent’s ear.

“But ye killed him.”

“He came at me with the axe.”

“Ain’t no axe in him.”

“My knife was quicker.”

“Then the rope’ll be quicker still.”

The men laughed, though none laughed well. They were gum diggers, lumbermen, trappers, river thieves, and three-day saints when the preacher’s boat came up from the settlement. They knew the law chiefly as a distant thing which collected taxes, took witnesses away at planting time, and hanged a poor man after feeding him for half a year. Their own justice wasted neither provisions nor daylight.

A pine leaned over the clearing. They had thrown the rope across its lowest limb. The other end passed through Tully’s hands, hairy and red-knuckled, and thence to Moss, Lanky Pell, the Frenchman called Little Baptiste, and six others who had spent the night drinking Kent’s whisky while deciding which of his possessions each should take.

“His boots won’t fit ye,” Moss had told Pell.

“I kin stuff ’em.”

“His rifle’s mine.”

“Who says?”

“I do.”

“Wait till he’s dead,” Little Baptiste had suggested. “It is not polite, dividing the property when the owner listens.”

Now the owner listened to the rain.

Beyond the pines lay the river, swollen and slate-coloured, with long strips of yellow foam twisting down to the bay. Kent could not see it, but he heard it. Water chuckled among roots. Water slapped the landing piles. Water whispered under the camp, under the trees, under the world.

“You boys,” he said, “are making a mistake.”

“Shore,” said Tully. “We’re makin’ it too slow.”

He struck Kent in the mouth.

Kent reeled; Tully struck again; then Rufe Moss kicked the backs of his knees. He went down. Mud splashed over his face. They hauled him up by the rope, choking him until his heels danced and his neck burned with a white, tearing fire.

“Ready?” called Pell.

The bittern boomed again.

Something happened then—not courage, for courage is a clean thing and what entered Kent was black, blind, clawing. It was the fear beneath fear, the beast that lives where thought cannot follow. It seized his heart and squeezed. It drove its claws into his throat beside the rope.

He flung himself backward.

The sudden weight dragged Tully forward. Kent twisted, kicked, caught Moss under the chin with his bare heel, and went down among them. Men cursed. The rope slackened. Kent rolled twice, grinding his bound hands against a broken bottle half buried in the mud. Glass bit his wrist. He sawed once, twice, thrice. The rawhide parted.

Tully was over him.

Kent caught Tully’s thumb, bent it backward, heard the joint crack, and drove his forehead into the man’s teeth. Blood leaped. Tully screamed through it. Kent snatched the rope with both hands, swung the loose coil, and struck Little Baptiste across the eyes. Moss sprang upon his back; Pell gripped one ankle; bodies wallowed and thrashed in the mire.

“Hold him!”

“Knife him!”

“Don’t knife him—he’s to hang!”

Kent heaved upright with Moss clinging to his shoulders. He rushed backward against the cook-house wall. Moss’s breath burst out in a grunt. Kent struck again. The wall shook; a frying pan fell inside; someone yelled that the building was coming down.

Pell crawled for the rifle propped against a stump.

Kent saw him. He sprang. The rope trailed from his neck, coiling and uncoiling like a pale snake. Pell’s fingers touched the stock. Kent stamped on them, snatched up the weapon, and fired from the hip.

The shot tore bark from the pine above Tully’s head.

All men stopped.

Smoke mingled with rain. Kent stood panting, one foot bare, the other sunk ankle-deep in mud, the rifle wavering across the ring of faces.

“Next one,” he said, “gets it in his belly.”

No one moved.

Behind them dawn had begun to spoil the darkness. The marsh showed a dull metallic gleam. Wet trunks rose like pillars in a drowned church.

Kent backed toward the landing.

Tully spat out a tooth and blood.

“Ye’ll come back,” he said.

Kent backed another pace.

“Not here.”

“Ye’ll come back somewheres. A man carries his road inside him.”

“Shut up.”

“My brother’ll walk beside ye. Every step.”

“Shut up!”

“An’ I’ll be at the end.”

Kent fired again.

This time the bullet ploughed the mud at Tully’s feet. The men scattered, slipping, sprawling, cursing. Kent turned and ran.

At the landing lay three dugouts and a cedar canoe. He chose the canoe because it was light, kicked two paddles and a patched blanket into it, and shoved off. Moss ran down behind him with an axe. Kent swung the rifle. Moss stopped so sharply that he fell seated in the shallows.

The current took the canoe broadside. Kent dropped the rifle, seized a paddle, struck left, struck right, struck left again. The slim craft wheeled between drowned roots and entered the middle stream.

From the bank Tully shouted after him.

“The bay’ll eat ye! The fog’ll blind ye! There ain’t nothin’ past it but Dead Man’s Island!”

Kent paddled.

“Ye hear me? Nobody lands there!”

Kent paddled harder.

“Ye’ll come back!”

The words followed him down the river. They came thinly through rain, then fainter through the trees, then seemed to issue from the water itself.

Ye’ll come back.

The river widened. Pines sank behind reeds; reeds gave place to tidal flats where white birds rose in screaming clouds. The rain ceased, but the sky remained low and iron-grey. At last the mouth opened before him, and beyond it the sea rolled under the morning like an immeasurable sheet of hammered lead.

Far out, where sea and cloud were one, a wall of fog waited.

Within that wall lay the island which fishermen saw in autumn and denied in spring; the island around which compasses wandered; the island whose beaches were sometimes visible at sunset, pale as the brow of a drowned woman. Three boats had gone toward it in forty years. One returned empty. One returned with a madman who bit his own hands and cried that the trees had spoken. One did not return at all.

Kent looked back.

On the distant shore, at the last point before the river vanished among pines, a man stood watching. Even at that distance Kent knew the great shoulders and crooked hat.

Tully lifted one arm.

Kent turned toward the fog.

The paddle swung and fell, swung and fell. Water slid from its blade in silver chains. The canoe rose upon the first long swell, dipped into a valley green as bottle glass, climbed again. Wind strengthened. Seaweed caught at the bow and streamed behind like drowned hair.

By noon the coast was gone.

By evening the sun appeared for one crimson instant beneath the clouds. It looked across the waste with a red, unwinking eye, then sank. Darkness came up swiftly.

Kent paddled on.

He had no food. He had no water save what rain remained in the bottom of the canoe. The rifle held one cartridge. The rope was still around his neck, its cut end dragging across his knees.

He tried to pull it off. The swollen knot would not pass over his head.

Thus he voyaged—bruised, bleeding, crowned with a noose—toward the hidden island and the night.

## II

At sunrise the sea became beautiful.

It had no right to beauty. It had tormented him through the hours of darkness, lifting him toward cold stars and dropping him into black hollows where pale foam clawed at the gunwales. Yet dawn clothed it in pearl and sapphire. Each wave carried a rosy flame along its crest; each hollow opened like a bowl of blue crystal. Gulls appeared from nowhere and hovered over him, their white wings catching gold.

Kent’s hands had blistered and broken. His shoulders burned. Thirst had swollen his tongue until it seemed a stone in his mouth. Still he paddled.

Ahead, the fog stretched north and south without end. It was not vapour as ordinary men know vapour, but a continent of whiteness, founded upon the sea and towered into the heavens. Its cliffs advanced and withdrew. Valleys opened in it and closed. Now it resembled marble; now wool; now the shroud drawn over the face of the world.

The gulls would not enter.

They followed until the canoe reached the first pale tendrils, then wheeled away, crying.

Kent dipped the paddle.

The fog received him.

At once the sun was extinguished. The sea lost colour. Sound altered: every splash became near and hollow, every breath returned to him from an invisible distance. Drops gathered in his hair, on his lashes, on the rope about his neck. The canoe moved through a white nowhere.

He paddled for an hour, or a day.

Sometimes he heard surf far to the right. He turned toward it, and the sound moved left. Sometimes a bell rang beneath the water. Once a child laughed close behind him. He looked back so violently that the canoe almost overturned, but there was only mist twisting over his wake.

The fear of fear returned.

It sat behind him.

He knew that if he looked once more he would see Tully in the stern, holding the rope and smiling with his broken teeth. Kent fixed his eyes upon the bow. His arms continued their labour though feeling had gone from them.

Swing and sink; draw and rise. Swing and sink.

The sea whispered against the cedar shell.

Toward afternoon the paddle struck sand.

Kent stared. Beneath the canoe lay clear green water and a floor of shells. The fog thinned. A luminous arch appeared overhead; then another. Through them shone a strip of beach, white as powdered bone.

A wave lifted the canoe and carried it forward.

The mist opened.

The island stood before him.

Cliffs rose at either horn of the bay, purple-black beneath crowns of cedar. Between them curved a beach where every grain glittered. Beyond the sand grew ferns high as a man, flowering vines, red-barked trees, and pines whose trunks ascended straight and silver into a green-gold dusk. Birds crossed the opening—blue, scarlet, amber—flashing like gems tossed from hand to hand.

Kent tried to shout. Only a cracked sound came.

The canoe touched shore.

He fell out on hands and knees. Warm water ran around his wrists. He crawled above the reach of the waves, dragged the rifle after him from habit, and lay with his cheek upon the sand.

The beach breathed. Each little wave came lapping, lapping, and withdrew with a silken hiss. In the forest something called twice—low, sweet, questioning.

Kent slept.

When he woke, night had come. The stars above the bay were enormous. They hung so near the black tree-tops that he imagined fruit might be shaken down among the ferns. A moon, thin and yellow as a wildcat’s eye, looked over the eastern cliff.

His thirst was torment.

He rose, fell, rose again. The rope dragged across his chest. He pushed into the forest, clutching leaves and branches. Perfume thickened around him: pine, moist earth, crushed mint, unseen blossoms. Small luminous insects drifted between the trunks. Once a pale deer stood before him. Its antlers were hung with strips of moss. It watched without fear, then stepped aside as though showing him a path.

Kent followed.

The path descended to a pool beneath a fall of water. Moonlight trembled there. He plunged his face into it and drank until cold struck his teeth and skull. Then he was sick, drank again, and lay shivering among flowers.

Morning found him still there.

For two days he lived on water and sour berries. He tried to strike fire from stones; sparks jumped and died. He rubbed sticks until his palms reopened. He searched the tide line for shellfish and found only empty shells, lavender within and rough as old teeth without. The rifle cartridge he dared not waste. Weakness entered his legs, then his arms, then his thoughts.

On the third day he caught a crab. It pinched his thumb. He laughed, smashed it with the rifle butt, tore it apart, and swallowed the flesh raw.

On the fourth, rain fell. He crouched beneath the cedar canoe, listening to drops drum upon the hull. The whole island seemed to whisper.

On the fifth, he saw Tully among the trees.

The man stood with a rope over one shoulder. Water ran from his hat.

Kent lifted the rifle and fired.

The report shattered the valley. Birds erupted from the forest. Echo rolled from cliff to cliff, dwindling into a hundred little shots.

When the smoke cleared there was no man, only a cedar trunk with a vine hanging from it.

The last cartridge was gone.

Kent cursed, then wept. The tears surprised him. He had not wept when his mother died, nor when winter took his first trapping partner, nor when Tully’s brother folded around the knife with a foolish look of astonishment. Now he wept because a vine had become a rope.

That evening he climbed the western cliff in search of birds’ eggs. Halfway up, his fingers slipped. He fell through brush, struck stone, and rolled into a hollow between roots.

He did not rise.

Twilight gathered, violet and slow. Moths fluttered over him. The pale deer came and smelled his hand. A fox sat upon a rock, wrapped its tail around its feet, and waited. Far out beyond the island the sea turned from opal to lead, from lead to ebony.

Kent heard singing.

At first he thought it wind in the needles. Then the tones descended and rose again, clear and human, though the words were unknown. A figure passed through the trees carrying red fire.

It was a young woman.

She wore a cloak of soft skins over one shoulder, and her black hair fell to her knees. Rain, or dew, jeweled it. Her feet made no sound among the leaves. She came near, saw him, and stood motionless.

Kent tried to speak.

The woman bent over him.

Her face was brown and oval, grave in repose, with dark eyes that reflected the flame. She touched the rope mark on his neck. Her fingers were cool.

Kent said, “Water.”

She answered in a language of flowing syllables.

“Water,” he repeated, pointing to his mouth.

Understanding moved across her face. She lifted his head and held a little cup of bark to his lips. The water tasted of smoke and cedar.

When he had drunk, she drew back and looked at him for a long while—not fearfully, but as one might regard a strange bird flung exhausted from a storm.

Then she smiled.

The fox leaped from its rock. The deer moved away. Night descended through the branches.

The young woman lifted her torch.

“Ihó,” she said softly. “Inâh.”

Kent did not know whether the words meant *come*, or *live*, or *poor lost creature*.

He followed her light into the forest.

## III

Her name, as nearly as his tongue could shape it, was Aiyana.

She lived in a house built beneath an overhanging rock, where cedar poles, woven reeds, hides, and flowering vines made a dwelling warmer than any lumber cabin Kent had known. Smoke escaped through a crack in the stone. Bundles of dried herbs hung from rafters. There were bone needles, baskets, fishhooks of shell, knives of chipped black glass, and wooden bowls polished by years of use.

Whether others had once lived there he could not discover. Sometimes she spoke toward the empty places, pausing as if an answer came. Sometimes she laid food upon a flat stone outside the door. Yet no human being appeared.

For many days Kent lay on a bed of fern and fur while she fed him broth, berries, roasted roots, and flakes of white fish. She washed the cuts on his wrists. She rubbed a bitter green salve into his shoulders. The rope she removed by wetting and patiently loosening the knot. When at last it fell away, Kent snatched it from her and hurled it into the fire.

The fibres blackened, writhed, glowed red.

But the mark remained around his throat.

Aiyana touched it and frowned.

“No,” Kent said.

She imitated him. “No.”

He pointed to himself. “Bud.”

“Bût.”

“Kent.”

“Ken.”

He pointed to her.

“Aiyana.”

“Aiyana,” he repeated.

She laughed, because he spoke it badly. Her laughter shook the dusk like a little string of silver bells. He said her name again, worse on purpose, and she laughed until she hid her face in her hair.

Thus their language began.

They possessed at first perhaps twenty words: water, fire, fish, sleep, sun, rain, yes, no, come, wait, danger. For the rest there were hands, eyes, smiles, and silence. Aiyana’s voice made even rebuke musical. Kent, who had heard men swear in five countries and pray in three, found that tenderness required no grammar.

When he could walk, she taught him the island.

She showed him how dry cedar fibre might be nested around a spark struck from black stone; how to bend over it gently, breathing life rather than blasting it with impatience. Flame appeared between her hands, small and blue, then yellow. Kent stared at it as at a miracle.

“Fire,” he said.

She touched her breast. “Fire.”

Then she touched his.

He looked away.

She taught him which berries nourished and which brought fever; where mussels clung below the tide; how fish could be trapped in woven fences when the sea withdrew; how a deer must be thanked before its flesh was taken; how roots could be softened in ashes, hides smoked, cordage twisted, hooks baited, shelters thatched against the eastern rain.

Kent had lived half his life in wilderness, but he knew it as an enemy knows an enemy—by wounds, ambushes, hungers, and cold. Aiyana knew it as kin. She spoke to the cedar before cutting bark. She warned ants when hot water would be poured. She called ravens thieves and then fed them. To the moon she gave a different name at every phase.

The animals knew her.

A red fox came each morning for scraps and allowed her to comb burrs from its tail. Two otters followed her canoe, rolling and chirruping. A stag with one broken antler waited beyond the pool at twilight. Birds perched on her shoulder and pecked seeds from her lips.

Kent named them in his own fashion: Gentleman Fox, Old Crookhorn, Mrs. Bluewing, the Two Idiots. Aiyana repeated these names solemnly, though she laughed when he was not looking.

Autumn coloured the island crimson and gold. The maples flamed; berries shone like drops of blood; wild geese passed over the cliffs in long black letters written across sunset. Kent built a second room against the rock house. He carved shelves, mended Aiyana’s canoe, and made a bow after three failures. She watched the failures with patience and the success with exaggerated wonder.

At night they sat by the fire.

Sometimes Kent told stories she could not understand. He spoke of snow camps, cattle towns, ships, sawmills, cities where streets glittered after rain and a man might walk all day without touching earth. She listened to his voice rather than his meaning. When he imitated a drunken cook fighting a stove, she laughed. When he described his mother, she seemed to know, and placed her hand over his.

He did not speak of Tully’s brother.

Winter came softly. Frost silvered the upper meadows, but snow touched the island only twice, lying one white morning upon the cedars before dissolving into rain. Storms attacked the outer cliffs. Waves burst upward in walls of green glass and fell in white ruin. Yet within the bay the water merely breathed.

Kent’s strength returned. Flesh covered his ribs; colour entered his face. His hair grew to his shoulders. He forgot the taste of whisky, the slap of greasy cards, the cook-house smoke, the musty bunk. He forgot men’s laughter when it is cruel.

He did not forget Tully.

At odd hours he would look toward the sea and see, beyond the bay, the fog.

It encircled the island through every season. Sometimes it lay low upon the water, a white serpent biting its tail. Sometimes it towered in walls. On clear days Kent climbed the cliff and searched for the mainland, but there was no mainland, no sail, no smoke—only whiteness and the small breathing circle of sea within it.

Once he proposed that they paddle through.

Aiyana understood before he finished. Terror entered her face.

She seized his wrist. “No.”

“I came that way.”

“No.”

“There are people.”

She shook her head violently.

“Men. Houses.”

“No, Ken. No.”

She pointed into the fog, then touched his closed eyes, his heart, and the earth. She lay one hand flat and covered it with the other.

Death. Sleep. Burial.

Kent looked again at the white barrier. A little wind moved along it, shaping towers and faces. One face had a crooked hat.

He turned away.

The second spring was a procession of wonders. Blue flowers rose through old leaves. Silver fish crowded the streams. The deer descended with spotted young. Every branch seemed to hold a nest; every nest, a mouth lifted toward heaven. Rain passed in warm curtains, and afterward the forest glittered from root to crown.

Aiyana swam in the pool beneath the fall. Her black hair spread upon the water. Sunlight trembled over her brown shoulders.

Kent stood among ferns, unable to move.

She saw him and called.

He shook his head.

She called again, laughing.

There was neither shame nor boldness in her; there was only the innocent certainty of sun, water, skin, and joy. She lifted both arms. Drops fell from them like diamonds.

Kent entered the pool.

The water was cold. She splashed him. He caught her wrist. She twisted free, swam beneath his arm, surfaced behind him, and pushed wet hair across his eyes. He turned. Their laughter ceased.

For a moment they heard only the fall, the rustle of leaves, the pit-a-pat of drops returning to the pool.

Aiyana touched the scar on his throat.

Then she kissed it.

Love did not come upon them like lightning. It had been there already in bread divided, wounds washed, fish nets lifted together, and long evenings of incomprehensible speech. Yet that kiss gave it body. The invisible became visible; the unspoken found a mouth.

They returned through the forest hand in hand.

That night the moon was full. The bay shone like a shield of pearl. Flowers opened after dark, pouring perfume beneath the trees. A night bird called, and another answered from the cliffs.

Aiyana said words Kent had never heard.

He answered with words she could not know.

Neither misunderstood.

## IV

There followed a year which afterward seemed to Kent not a measure of time but a single golden day.

Morning: Aiyana grinding roots while sunlight threaded the smoke. Noon: the two canoes gliding side by side above green depths where fish hung like scraps of silver leaf. Evening: Kent carrying wood, Aiyana returning with berries, their shadows meeting before their bodies did. Night: firelight on the rock walls, her hair across his breast, rain whispering outside.

They built a larger house in a meadow above the bay. Kent set cedar posts deep and strong. Aiyana wove mats patterned in black, red, and yellow. He fashioned a table, a cradle—though neither yet spoke of a child—and a door that would not fit until she showed him the frame was crooked.

He blamed the frame.

She blamed the builder.

By then their language was a strange, bright fabric woven from both their tongues. His rough nouns stood beside her fluid verbs. A look altered a sentence; a touch completed it. For *I love you*, they had many forms: *My fire knows you*. *My feet return to you*. *The night is little when you are here*. The simplest was her first phrase to him:

“Ihó. Inâh.”

Come. Live.

So Kent believed.

Aiyana told him fragments of the island’s history. Long ago, people had dwelt there in summer, fishing the bays and gathering the red berries that grew upon the northern slopes. Then came sickness from beyond the fog. Canoes departed and did not return. Children slept and could not be awakened. Fires went out in house after house.

Her own people—whether parents, grandparents, or ancestors seemed uncertain in the telling—had remained when others fled. At last only a girl and an old woman survived. The old woman taught the girl the speech of birds, the proper names of water, and the songs that keep mist from entering a house.

“And the old woman?” Kent asked.

Aiyana pointed to the earth.

“How long ago?”

She counted seasons on her fingers, lost count, laughed, and shook her head.

Kent sometimes wondered whether she had been alone five years or fifty.

Such thoughts could not live long in daylight. Aiyana was warm beside him, her pulse quick under his fingers. She grew weary that summer, and one evening she placed his hand upon the gentle swelling of her body.

Kent stared.

She watched his face anxiously.

“A child?”

She nodded.

Something immense and tender broke within him. He dropped to his knees and pressed his cheek against her. He felt nothing at first. Then, faintly, from the secret dark, came a movement like a fish turning under water.

Kent laughed. He lifted her, though she protested, and carried her around the house. Gentleman Fox barked at them. Ravens flapped from the roof. Aiyana clung to his neck, laughing and scolding.

That night Kent could not sleep.

He went to the bay. Stars burned in the water. The fog stood far off, white beneath the moon.

A child.

His child.

The words opened doors he had kept shut. Behind the first was the gum camp. Behind the second lay the tool shed. Behind the third was Tully’s brother with astonishment on his face and Kent’s knife under his ribs.

The dead man had been named Abel.

Kent remembered that now. Abel Tully, who cheated at cards, struck when drunk, sang hymns badly, and had come at him with an axe because Kent called him a liar. The axe had glanced from a post. Kent had stepped in with the knife. He might have stepped back. He had not.

A man alone can outwalk memory. He can cross mountains, rivers, frontiers. He can change his name and sleep in rain. But a father stands still. Behind him is his child. Before him comes the thing he fled.

Kent touched the scar at his throat.

The fear of fear crept from the forest and sat beside him.

From that night he began to prepare.

He kept a spear by the door. He climbed the cliff daily. He strengthened the canoe, stored dried fish, examined the fog. At any crack in the brush he turned, expecting Tully. When driftwood landed on the beach, he searched it for axe marks. When ravens cried, he heard men calling, “Hold him! Rope him!”

Aiyana perceived the change. She asked what hunted him.

Kent tried to tell her.

He drew two men in the sand. One struck with an axe. The other used a knife. He drew blood. He drew a third man holding a rope. Then he drew a canoe crossing water.

Aiyana studied the figures.

“This one dead?” she asked, pointing to Abel.

“Yes.”

“You killed.”

“Yes.”

“This one follows?” She touched Tully.

“Yes.”

She looked toward the fog.

Kent waited for judgment. He desired it suddenly. Let her recoil. Let her name him murderer. Let Eden close its gate.

Instead she erased the figures with her foot.

“Gone,” she said.

“No.”

She placed her palm over his heart. “Here.”

“Yes.”

Then she placed it over her own. “Here too.”

He bowed his head.

Aiyana took his face between her hands.

“The earth alone lasts,” she said in her language, and made him repeat it until he spoke correctly.

Their son was born on a night of rain.

The labour began at sunset. By midnight the wind had risen; by dawn the island shook beneath the storm. Kent tended fire, heated water, obeyed commands, misunderstood half of them, and suffered ten deaths before the small wet creature came wailing into his hands.

Aiyana laughed weakly.

“Chaské,” she whispered.

The boy opened dark eyes.

Thunder rolled from the sea. At once his crying ceased. Every flame in the house bent toward him.

Kent saw it. Aiyana saw it. Neither spoke.

Chaské grew swiftly. At three months he watched birds with such grave attention that they ceased singing and watched him in return. At six months he crawled beyond the doorway, where Gentleman Fox lay waiting as if appointed nurse. At nine months he spoke—not *mother* or *father*, but the liquid call of the blue bird nesting above the pool. The bird answered.

He was beautiful. His skin held the warm brown of Aiyana’s; his hair was Kent’s sunlit gold darkened by a copper shade. His eyes changed with the hour, grey at dawn, green at noon, nearly black by firelight.

And he cast a white shadow.

Kent first noticed it beside the stream. The sun stood overhead, and every fern laid a dark shape upon the ground. But Chaské’s small shadow shone pale, a little brightness moving over stones.

Kent blinked. It remained.

At night the child glimmered in his cradle. The light was not strong; it resembled moonshine caught beneath clear water. Moths entered and settled on his blanket. Aiyana brushed them away gently.

“What is it?” Kent asked.

She sang an old song in answer.

“What is he?”

“Our son.”

“Is he—”

“Our son,” she repeated.

Kent loved the boy with a passion that frightened him. He lifted him at dawn and showed him the sea; carried him through rain beneath his coat; carved animals from wood; spoke the names of tools, trees, stars, and sins. He promised protection against cold, hunger, beast, man, and spirit.

Yet each promise increased his terror, for love is the key that opens grief. Before Chaské, Kent had feared death. After Chaské, he learned there were things worse than death.

The boy approached his first year.

Mist began to enter the bay.

## V

It came first in threads.

At sunrise a white ribbon lay across the beach and vanished when Kent approached. At noon little vapours drifted between cedars, though the sky was blue. At night the windows filmed over from outside, and unseen fingers traced circles on the glassy skins stretched across them.

Aiyana sang more often.

She sang while cooking, while gathering wood, while Chaské slept. Her voice had changed. Beneath the melody ran a farewell.

Kent worked furiously. He sealed cracks in the house. He kept fires burning at every side. He cut back brush so no man could approach unseen. The labour made his palms bleed, but when he paused, the fog waited at the meadow’s edge.

One morning Chaské stood for the first time.

He took three uncertain steps toward Kent, his white shadow preceding him. Kent knelt, laughing, and caught him.

From the forest came a dry snap.

Kent wheeled.

A branch swayed. Beyond it, between two trunks, hung what seemed a loop of rope.

He thrust Chaské into Aiyana’s arms and ran. The loop became a vine. He tore it down, hacked it with his knife, stamped it into the earth.

When he returned, Aiyana was watching.

“Tully,” he said.

She shook her head.

“I saw the rope.”

“Mist.”

“It was rope.”

“Mist,” she repeated.

That evening the fog covered the beach. By midnight it reached the meadow. It moved against the wind, flowing uphill in long pale streams. Flowers disappeared beneath it; then stones; then the lower trunks of trees. The house stood like an ark upon a silent white flood.

Kent lit torches.

Flame drove the fog back a little, colouring it rose and gold. He planted torches in a circle. Aiyana sat inside the house with Chaské, singing.

“Ihó! Inâh!” rose the refrain.

Come. Live.

The night deepened. Kent paced from flame to flame. Beyond the circle something paced with him.

Step.

Pause.

Step.

Leaves did not stir. Branches made no sound. Yet he knew the tread of a heavy man.

“Tully!” he shouted.

The fog returned his voice.

Tully—Tully—Tully—

A torch guttered. Kent relit it. Another went out.

Aiyana’s song ceased.

He ran inside.

She stood with Chaské in her arms. Both were luminous. The child’s face shone against her shoulder, and her long hair floated though there was no wind.

“We go,” she said.

“No.”

She pointed toward the forest.

“No.”

“The old path.”

“There’s no path.”

“For me.”

Kent seized her hand. It felt cool, almost without substance.

“You stay.”

She smiled, and the sorrow in that smile seemed older than the island.

“I stayed.”

“What does that mean?”

“I waited.”

“For whom?”

“For you.”

The fog entered under the door. It curled about her ankles.

Kent caught Chaské and pulled. For an instant he held the child between them. Chaské did not cry. He touched Kent’s throat, precisely where the rope had marked him.

“Father,” he said for the first time.

Then the little fingers became white mist within Kent’s hand.

He screamed.

Aiyana drew the boy to her breast. Fog climbed around them. Her feet vanished, then the folds of her garment, then her body to the waist.

Kent wrapped both arms around her. He felt her heartbeat, slow and tender. He pressed his mouth to hers.

“Ihó,” she whispered.

“No!”

“Inâh.”

“Don’t go. Don’t leave me. Aiyana!”

She touched his face.

“The earth alone lasts.”

Her eyes disappeared last.

Kent clutched empty fog.

He fell, striking the floor. For a while there was no sound but his own breathing and the faint hiss of mist over embers. Then, far out in the whiteness, Chaské laughed.

Kent sprang up.

He ran from the house, carrying a torch. The circle of fires had died. Fog filled the meadow to the height of his breast.

“Aiyana!”

A voice answered to the left.

He plunged after it. Branches struck his face. The forest had altered. Familiar paths ended at cliffs; cliffs opened into pools; trees stood where yesterday there had been sky. The pale deer appeared and vanished. Gentleman Fox trotted ahead, but when Kent followed, it became a red leaf tumbling through vapour.

“Chaské!”

A white radiance moved between the cedars.

Kent pursued it. He reached the pool. The waterfall fell upward, silver beads climbing into darkness. On the opposite bank Aiyana stood with the child.

He waded in.

The pool deepened. Water rose to his waist, his breast, his throat. He swam. When he reached the other side, there was only moonlight on stone.

He searched until dawn should have come, but no dawn came.

Time compressed into wandering. He called until his voice broke. He found the house again and found it ruined, roof fallen, timbers grey with age. He found his canoe rotted upon the beach. He found the wooden animals he had carved for Chaské buried beneath moss.

Years had passed.

Or one night.

The mist thickened.

Kent returned to the meadow. Grief walked behind him now. Unlike fear, it did not threaten. It merely followed, patient as a shadow, knowing he had nowhere left to go.

At last a red light appeared among the trees.

“Aiyana?” he whispered.

The light came nearer, bobbing at the height of a man’s shoulder. Kent staggered toward it.

A torch emerged from the fog.

A broad hand held it.

Then came an arm in a wet wool sleeve; a shoulder; a hat with a crooked brim. The face beneath was swollen and pale. Blood darkened the mouth where teeth were broken. Rain dripped from the beard, though no rain fell upon the island.

Tully stood beside him.

Not across the meadow. Not approaching.

Beside him.

In his left hand he carried a coil of rope.

Kent could smell the gum camp: mould, whisky, blood, damp boots, rotten leaves. He heard men laughing beneath the pines. He heard the river whispering under the world.

Tully raised the torch.

“Found ye,” he said.

Kent looked at the rope. Its noose was already tied.

“Are they dead?” Kent asked.

“Who?”

Kent turned toward the ruined house.

For one instant the fog opened. He saw Aiyana beneath the flowering trees, young and dark and smiling. Chaské stood beside her, bright as a star at morning. Around them gathered the deer, the fox, the otters, and birds of blue and scarlet and gold. Beyond them shone the island as it had on the first day—water of emerald, beach of pearl, cedars crowned with sun.

Chaské lifted his hand.

“Father.”

The vision closed.

Kent faced Tully.

“I killed Abel,” he said.

“Yep.”

“He had an axe.”

“Yep.”

“I could have stepped back.”

“Yep.”

Kent touched the old scar about his throat.

“Will it hurt?”

Tully considered. His terrible face softened—not into mercy, but into the bleak fellowship of men who have travelled the same road from opposite ends.

“Not long.”

He slipped the noose over Kent’s head.

The rope settled into its former mark as water enters an ancient channel.

Out beyond the cliffs, the sea rose. Waves turned, turning, under the white enclosure. They lifted pearl backs to a moon no eye could see and broke upon the beaches with the sound of innumerable wings. Forests bent; birds migrated through darkness; flowers opened their perfumed throats and perished before dawn. The island breathed beneath mist, remembering or forgetting—it was all one.

Tully took up the slack.

Kent heard Aiyana singing.

“Ihó! Inâh!”

Come.

Live.

Or perhaps the words had always meant something else.

The torch flared crimson. The rope drew tight. For an instant Kent saw all fires as one fire: the spark between Aiyana’s hands; the hearth beside which she had laughed; the light upon his newborn son; the torch in the fist of death. He saw every white thing become another—the gull’s wing, the curl of foam, the child’s shadow, the fog, the bone beneath flesh, the blank page upon which no man may write twice.

Then flame and fog went out together.

The sea continued its lapping.

The trees continued their whispering.

Morning, rosy and indifferent, opened over empty water. Where the island had stood there was only a circle of mist, slowly thinning beneath the sun. A gull crossed it and gave one lonely cry. For a moment its shadow upon the sea was not dark but white.

Then that too vanished.

The earth alone lasts.

The earth alone—and even the earth dreams of ending.

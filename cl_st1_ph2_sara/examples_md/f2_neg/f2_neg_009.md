When the sea withdrew from Bellweather Bay, it left the boats standing upright in the mud.

At first, no one was alarmed. The tide had always gone out farther in autumn, revealing black sand, tangled ropes, and the ribs of ships too old for anyone to remember. Children ran between the stranded fishing boats, collecting coins, buckles, and green fragments of glass. Gulls stabbed at silver fish trapped in shallow pools.

But by sunset, the sea had not returned.

The people of Bellweather gathered along the harbor wall. Lanterns appeared in windows. Fishermen stared toward the dark horizon, where the last strip of water shone beneath the sky like a blade.

Mara Venn stood among them with her hands in her coat pockets. At seventeen, she was the youngest apprentice in the lighthouse, though there had been no official keeper since her grandfather died. She polished the lens, trimmed the wick, recorded the weather, and lit the lamp each evening because someone had to.

“Has this ever happened before?” asked a boy beside her.

His father pulled him away before Mara could answer.

Everyone in Bellweather knew the story of Mara’s mother, who had walked into the sea twelve years earlier and never returned. Some said grief had taken her. Others said madness ran through the Venn family like a crack through glass.

Mara looked past the abandoned boats. Far out on the exposed seabed, something flashed.

Once.

Twice.

Then darkness swallowed it.

She climbed the lighthouse hill alone.

The lighthouse stood on a cliff above the town, a white tower weathered gray by storms. Inside, the circular stairs smelled of oil and salt. Mara reached the lantern room, struck a match, and touched it to the wick.

The flame rose.

She adjusted the brass housing until the great lens began to turn. Its beam swept across Bellweather, over rooftops and the church spire, then outward across miles of empty seabed.

On the third rotation, the light found a road.

Mara gripped the railing.

A road of pale stones ran from beneath the harbor toward the horizon. It was too straight to be natural and too broad to have been built for ordinary carts. Along its edges stood black pillars crusted with barnacles. Between them lay broken statues, arches, and the roofs of drowned houses.

Bellweather had not been built beside the sea.

It had been built on top of a city.

The beam continued its sweep. For one instant, it illuminated a tall gate in the distance. Something stood beneath it.

A woman in a red coat.

Mara’s breath stopped.

Her mother had owned a red coat.

By the time the beam returned, the gate was empty.

Mara did not sleep. Before dawn, she packed bread, a flask, spare lamp oil, matches, and her grandfather’s brass compass. She took a coil of rope from the storehouse and tied his old storm lantern to her belt.

When she opened the lighthouse door, Elias Thorne was sitting on the step.

He was a year older than Mara and had spent most of his life mending nets in his father’s shop. His hair was the color of sand, and his left hand bore a white scar from the night he had tried to rescue a seal from a hook.

“You’re going out there,” he said.

“No.”

“You’re wearing boots and carrying enough bread for two days.”

“I get hungry.”

Elias rose. “Then I’m coming.”

“No.”

“Mara, the whole town thinks the sea will rush back any moment.”

“Then stay here.”

He studied her face. “You saw something.”

Mara looked toward the harbor. In the gray morning, townspeople moved across the mud, dragging stranded fish into baskets. Beyond them, the stone road disappeared into mist.

“I saw my mother,” she said.

Elias did not laugh.

They left before the church bell rang six.

The road was slick with weed but otherwise perfectly preserved. Strange carvings covered the pillars: open eyes, crescent moons, hands holding flames. The drowned buildings grew taller as Mara and Elias walked. At first, only chimneys and rooftops emerged from the mud. Farther out, whole houses stood exposed, their windows empty and their doors furred with moss.

Crabs filled the rooms.

In one house, plates remained arranged on a table. In another, a rusted cradle rested beside a cold fireplace. A schoolroom held rows of stone desks, each carved with names no one in Bellweather could read.

Elias ran his fingers over the marks.

“Imagine living here,” he said.

“People did.”

“What happened to them?”

“The sea.”

“That isn’t an answer.”

Mara’s compass needle trembled. It had not pointed north since they stepped onto the road. Instead, it pointed forward, toward the gate.

Around noon, they found the first footprints.

They were narrow and fresh, pressed into the mud between the stones. Whoever had made them wore boots with a broken heel.

Mara knelt.

Her mother’s red boots had made a clicking sound when she walked because one heel had split. Mara remembered lying awake as a child, listening for that uneven rhythm on the stairs.

Click. Step. Click. Step.

“She’s here,” Mara whispered.

Elias crouched beside her. “After twelve years?”

“She’s here.”

He did not argue, but fear crossed his face.

They followed the footprints deeper into the drowned city.

The air changed as the afternoon faded. Though no water covered the road, Mara began to hear waves. They rolled somewhere overhead, vast and heavy. Schools of translucent fish drifted through the air between buildings, vanishing whenever she looked directly at them.

At a crossroads stood a clock tower. Its face had no numbers, only symbols matching those on the pillars. Its single hand turned backward.

Beneath the tower, an old man sat behind a wooden desk.

Unlike the city, he was dry. His black suit was neatly pressed, and a blue flower bloomed in his buttonhole. On the desk lay a ledger, an inkpot, and a silver bell.

“Travelers,” he said. “How unusual.”

Elias grabbed Mara’s arm.

The old man smiled. “No need for alarm. I am only the registrar.”

“Of what?” Mara asked.

“Arrivals and departures.”

He opened the ledger. Its pages stirred though there was no wind. Columns of names filled the paper. Mara glimpsed dates spanning centuries.

“Name?” he asked.

“We’re not staying,” Elias said.

“Everyone says that.”

“We’re looking for someone,” Mara said. “Liora Venn.”

The registrar’s smile faded.

He turned several pages, then stopped.

“Liora Venn entered at midnight on the seventh day of November,” he said. “Twelve years ago.”

Mara leaned across the desk. Beside her mother’s name was a blank space.

“What does that mean?”

“No departure.”

“So she’s alive?”

The old man dipped his pen in ink. “The drowned are not dead in the usual sense. Nor are the living entirely alive while they remain here. The city keeps poor boundaries.”

“Where is she?”

“At the House of Tides, if she has not changed her mind.”

“Changed her mind about what?”

The registrar looked toward the mist. “About coming home.”

He rang the silver bell.

The sound passed through Mara’s bones.

Every door along the street opened.

Figures emerged from the buildings. Men in ruined uniforms. Women with seaweed in their hair. Children carrying wooden toys. Water streamed from their clothes, yet vanished before touching the ground. Their faces were pale and patient.

“Run,” Elias said.

They ran.

The drowned people did not chase them quickly. They walked, but there were hundreds, and they poured from every street. Mara and Elias followed the compass through alleys and courtyards. The needle spun wildly whenever they turned away from the distant gate.

A little girl stepped in front of Mara.

“Have you seen my brother?” she asked.

Mara swerved around her.

A sailor reached for Elias. “Which year is it?”

Elias pulled free, leaving a strip of his sleeve in the sailor’s hand.

The sound of invisible waves grew louder.

They reached the gate as the first stars appeared. Beyond it stood a palace of black stone with shells embedded in its walls. Water flowed upward from its roof, joining a dark cloud above. Wide steps led to bronze doors.

There, wearing a red coat faded almost pink, stood Liora Venn.

She had not aged.

Mara knew her at once: the gray streak in her dark hair, the small scar beneath her chin, the silver ring on her thumb. She looked exactly as she had on the last morning Mara saw her.

“Mama?”

Liora lifted one hand to her mouth.

“Mara,” she breathed.

The word broke something inside them both.

Mara climbed the steps. Her mother met her halfway and held her so tightly that Mara could scarcely breathe. She smelled of lavender soap and rain, the scent of childhood blankets and warm kitchens.

“You grew,” Liora whispered.

“You left.”

“I know.”

“You left me.”

“I know.”

Mara pushed her away. “Why?”

Liora looked at Elias, then at the drowned people gathering beyond the gate.

“We can’t speak here.”

She led them through the bronze doors.

Inside, the House of Tides was larger than it appeared. Staircases climbed into darkness. Chandeliers burned with blue flame. In the center of the entrance hall stood a round pool, though its water hung vertically like a mirror.

Liora locked the doors.

“They won’t stay outside long,” she said. “The city knows you’re alive.”

“What is this place?” Elias asked.

“A prison.”

“For them?”

“For what’s beneath them.”

A deep thud shook the floor.

Dust fell from the ceiling.

Liora closed her eyes. “It’s waking.”

Mara took out her grandfather’s compass. The needle pointed toward the vertical pool.

Her mother saw it and turned pale. “Where did you get that?”

“It was Grandfather’s.”

“It was never his. He was only keeping it.”

Another thud sounded, followed by a low groan like mountains splitting underwater.

Liora approached the pool. Its surface brightened, revealing not their reflections but Bellweather Bay as it had appeared the previous morning, full of waves and boats.

“Long before Bellweather existed,” she said, “this city was called Nareth. Its people learned to command the tides. They harvested storms, redirected currents, and raised islands from the deep. Then they found something sleeping beneath the seabed.”

In the pool, the image shifted.

A vast shadow curled below the city. It had no clear shape. Mara saw an eye, then a forest of limbs, then a mouth wide enough to swallow the harbor.

“The people of Nareth could not kill it,” Liora continued. “So they drowned their city to keep it asleep. They bound it with the turning of the tides. As long as the sea came and went, the prison held.”

“Why did the sea leave?” Mara asked.

“Because the mechanism is failing.”

“Then fix it.”

“I tried.”

“For twelve years?”

“For me, it has been nine days.”

Mara stared at her.

“Time follows the tide here,” Liora said softly. “It advances, retreats, circles back. I thought I could repair the prison and return before you woke. Then the sea closed above me.”

The bronze doors shuddered as the drowned struck them from outside.

“They want to leave,” Elias said.

“Of course they do,” Liora replied. “But they are part of the binding. If they cross into Bellweather, the thing below will rise with them.”

Mara looked into the pool. “What does the compass do?”

“It finds the heart of the tide engine.”

“And?”

“And it can restart it.”

Hope flared in Mara. “Then why haven’t you?”

“Because restarting it requires a keeper.”

The word settled heavily in the hall.

Mara thought of the lighthouse, its turning lens, its nightly beam. She thought of her grandfather recording winds in careful handwriting. She thought of her mother trapped in the drowned city while twelve years passed on shore.

“A keeper stays behind,” she said.

Liora nodded.

The doors began to bend inward.

“That’s why you never came back.”

“I couldn’t ask someone else to take my place.”

“You could have told us.”

“I believed I would find another way.”

“You always believe that,” Mara said. “Even when everyone else pays for it.”

Liora recoiled as though slapped.

Mara regretted the words, but only because they were true.

A bronze hinge tore loose. Pale hands reached through the gap.

“We need to go,” Elias said.

Liora removed the silver ring from her thumb. Inside it was a groove shaped like a wave. She pressed it into Mara’s palm.

“The engine lies beneath this house. The ring opens the chamber.”

“And then?”

“One of us turns the wheel. The other two return to Bellweather before the sea follows.”

“No,” Mara said.

“Mara—”

“No. I didn’t come here to lose you again.”

The doors burst open.

The drowned flooded into the hall.

Liora seized Mara’s hand and pulled her toward a staircase behind the vertical pool. Elias followed as the dead spread through the House of Tides. They descended past walls that trembled with each impact from below.

At the bottom stood a circular door with no handle. Mara pressed the ring into a hollow at its center.

The door opened.

The tide engine filled a cavern larger than Bellweather.

Thousands of bronze wheels turned in darkness. Chains thicker than tree trunks descended through holes in the floor. Glass pipes carried streams of glowing water in impossible directions. At the center stood a platform and, upon it, a wheel of white stone.

The compass needle pointed directly at the wheel.

Behind them, the drowned began descending the stairs.

Liora ran to the platform. Mara caught her coat.

“Don’t.”

“There is no time.”

“We’ll find another way.”

“I searched for nine days.”

“Then we’ll search for ten.”

Liora touched Mara’s cheek. “You have your whole life waiting.”

“So did you.”

A terrible crack echoed below. One of the enormous chains snapped and whipped through the cavern, smashing gears into fragments.

Elias stared at the machinery. “The keeper turns the wheel, yes?”

“Yes,” Liora said.

“Does the keeper have to turn it forever?”

“As long as the prison must hold.”

“But the lighthouse lens turns by itself.”

Mara looked at him.

“The clockwork,” he said. “We wind it each morning, and the weights keep it moving all night.”

“This is not a lighthouse,” Liora said.

“No,” Mara replied slowly. “But it was built by people.”

She climbed onto the platform and examined the wheel. Along its base were sockets, gears, and a broken axle. The city’s engineers had intended the mechanism to move on its own. The keeper had never been part of the design.

“The engine isn’t asking for a sacrifice,” Mara said. “It’s broken.”

The drowned reached the chamber.

Liora held them back while Elias and Mara searched the platform. The missing axle lay below, snapped in two. One half had fallen into the gears. The other remained attached to the wheel.

“We need something to join them,” Elias said.

Mara pulled the storm lantern from her belt. Its brass handle was too thin. The compass casing was too small. The ring bent when they tried it.

Another chain snapped.

The thing beneath the city opened its eye.

The cavern filled with sickly green light. Everyone froze, living and dead alike. A voice entered Mara’s mind, promising deep kingdoms, vanished knowledge, and the return of every soul the sea had taken.

Then Elias raised his scarred hand.

“The hook,” he said.

Years earlier, the surgeon had set two narrow steel pins in his hand because the seal hook had shattered the bone. Mara remembered him complaining they ached before storms.

“No,” she said.

“They’re the right size.”

“They’re inside you.”

“Yes. Inconvenient, that.”

Liora tore a strip from her coat. Mara used the small knife from her pack. Elias bit down on his sleeve and did not scream until she drew the first pin free.

The steel pins fitted into the axle’s inner channels as if they had been forged for that purpose. Mara bound the joint with brass wire from the lantern. Elias, shaking and white-faced, leaned against the wheel.

“Turn,” he said.

Together, they pushed.

At first, nothing happened.

Then the axle caught.

The white wheel moved.

One tooth engaged the next. The motion traveled outward through a hundred gears, then a thousand. The glowing water surged through the glass pipes. The chains tightened.

The drowned cried out, not in pain but relief.

Above them, the sea began to return.

Liora grabbed Elias and Mara as water burst through the cavern walls. The compass needle pointed upward now, tracing the fastest path home. They climbed while the House of Tides collapsed around them.

Outside, the drowned city was disappearing beneath a wall of black water.

They ran along the stone road.

The dead did not follow. They stood in doorways and windows, watching the sea come home. The little girl waved. The sailor saluted. The registrar closed his ledger.

Mara, Elias, and Liora reached the harbor as the first wave lifted the fishing boats from the mud. Bells rang across Bellweather. People shouted from rooftops and threw ropes toward them.

The water struck.

For a moment, Mara saw the city beneath her, its towers shining in the deep. Then strong hands pulled her into a boat.

When she woke, she was in the lighthouse.

Morning filled the lantern room. Elias slept in a chair nearby, his bandaged hand resting on his chest. Below, Bellweather’s harbor glittered beneath a calm blue tide.

Liora stood beside the great lens.

She had aged during the night. A few lines touched her eyes now, and more gray threaded her hair. Not twelve years, perhaps, but enough to show that time had found her again.

“Will it hold?” Mara asked.

Liora watched the beam sweep across the water.

“For a while.”

“How long?”

“All machines need tending.”

Mara joined her at the window. Far below, the townspeople repaired docks, righted barrels, and told one another stories that would grow stranger with every telling.

Elias woke and muttered that someone owed him two new pins.

Liora laughed.

It was a rusty sound, uncertain from disuse, but it was real.

That evening, all three of them lit the lighthouse lamp. Mara wrote the day’s events in the keeper’s book, though she left out the voice from beneath the city and the promises it had made.

At the bottom of the page, she added a new duty to the list:

Check the tide engine every seventh autumn.

Then she closed the book.

Outside, the sea withdrew from Bellweather Bay, paused at the edge of the harbor, and returned exactly when it should.

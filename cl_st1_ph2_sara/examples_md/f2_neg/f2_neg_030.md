THE HEAT ARRIVES before the jet lag has decided what to do with them. It presses against the airport windows, pale and absolute, and when they step outside it slides beneath their clothes. On the taxi ride Clara watches the fields flatten under the noon light, then the warehouses, the umbrella pines, the ochre walls scribbled with names. Rome appears by degrees, cupolas between apartment blocks, laundry on balconies, the mottled trunks of plane trees like old camouflage.

“You said October would be cooler,” Daniel says.

“It is cooler.”

“Than what?”

“July.”

He lowers the window and warm air fills the taxi. The driver glances at them in the mirror.

“Aria condizionata,” he says, pointing at the dashboard.

“Prego,” Daniel says, though no one has offered him anything.

Clara laughs. She has not slept since Boston, unless the twenty minutes over Portugal count. Her birthday is tomorrow. Fifty. Daniel has said the number often enough that it has become an item in the luggage, something they must remember not to leave in the taxi.

The apartment is on the second floor of a building near the river, above a coffee bar with three metal tables on the pavement. The owner’s niece meets them at the door. She is called Elisa, or perhaps Elissa; Clara cannot hear properly in the stairwell, where a breeze moves downward from an open window and the lift rattles behind its cage.

“You find everything,” Elisa says. “Towels, sheets, washing machine. The boiler is there, but do not touch unless—” She makes a turning gesture. “Hot water.”

“We shouldn’t touch it if we want hot water?” Daniel asks.

“If no hot water.”

“Then touch it.”

“Yes.”

The apartment is larger than it appeared in the photographs. There is a narrow kitchen with blue cupboards, a dining room, a sitting room with French doors onto a small balcony, and a corridor that bends out of sight. The furniture belongs to different decades. A green velvet sofa sags in the middle. Six cane-backed chairs surround a table covered with a plastic cloth printed with lemons. A chandelier hangs low over it, one glass tulip missing, another tilted downward as though listening.

On an upright piano are children’s music books, their covers faded blue and pink. The top three keys have chips in the ivory. A framed photograph shows two girls at the sea, one standing stiffly in a striped bathing suit while the younger digs in wet sand.

“Family place,” Elisa says. “My aunt. She lives now with my cousin outside Rome.”

“Whose children?” Clara asks.

Elisa looks at the photograph. “My mother and my aunt.”

“I thought it was more recent.”

“No, no. Nineteen sixty-eight, maybe.”

“They look like children now,” Clara says.

Elisa smiles politely.

She shows them the bedroom. The bed is made with a white crocheted cover, and on each pillow lies a wrapped mint. In the bathroom there are half-used bottles of shampoo, a hair dryer with a knotted cord, a chipped ceramic cup full of cotton buds. A medicine cabinet contains iodine, aspirin, an elastic bandage, and a packet of tablets expired six years ago.

At the end of the corridor is a door painted cream.

“That room is closed,” Elisa says.

“Closed?” Clara asks.

“Private things.”

“Of course,” Daniel says.

Elisa sets a ring of keys on the dining table. There are seven keys, all tagged except one.

“For the building, apartment, mailbox, rubbish. This one for shutters. You must hook the French doors. Wind comes suddenly.”

She demonstrates the brass hook at the bottom of each door.

“Suddenly,” Daniel says.

Elisa does not understand that he is repeating her. She shows them the telephone number for repairs, written on the back of a card advertising a restaurant in Testaccio.

Light cuts across the tile floors, exposing dust beneath the piano and a pale square on the wall where a picture has been removed. Opposite them, an old woman waters geraniums on a balcony. “I’m tired.”

“Yes.”

“It’s Rome.”

“The water’s good.”

“When I lived here, I could see a convent from my window,” she says.

“No. It only looked close.”

“You lived on this street?”

“Two streets over. Or three.”

“You said this was the neighborhood.”

“It is.”

“We should stay awake,” Daniel says. “At least until nine.”

“I told you.”

“It’s good,” she says.

She recognizes fragments rather than routes: a tobacco shop where there used to be a butcher, a shrine to the Madonna behind dusty glass, an archway with a stone head above it. “This bar was here,” she says.

“The one with the yellow awning.”

“Not the same bar. The place.”

“The place was here?”

“The corner.”

Clara stops at a bakery window.

“You hungry?” Daniel asks.

“No.”

“Cornetto?”

“That’s breakfast.”

Inside, rows of pastries shine beneath glass. A woman holds a little girl by the wrist while paying. The girl bends one knee and swings the free leg, her sandal passing back and forth above the floor.

Clara moves on.

The piazza where the procession will arrive is already crowded. Chairs have been set out in uneven rows near the church, though no one sits in them. White and yellow flags hang from balconies. “It hasn’t started.”

“No.”

A bell rings somewhere beyond the buildings. People turn, then turn back. Families gather along the edges of the square. Elderly women claim chairs by placing handbags on them. Teenagers sit on the fountain wall drinking from plastic bottles. A priest crosses the piazza in shirtsleeves, carrying his black jacket over one arm.

Daniel looks for shade.

“Over there,” he says.

“If we stand there, we won’t see her come in.”

“Does she come from that street?”

“I think so.”

“You think?”

“Not all of them.”

She ignores him. The piazza seems smaller than it did when she was twenty, though then she had stood at the back and the crowd had lifted her, carried her forward without anyone touching her. She remembers wax running over her fingers from a paper-cupped candle. She remembers a brass instrument near her ear. A family stops beside them: a mother, two daughters and a boy in a football shirt. The younger girl sits on the father’s shoulders. Her hair has been divided into two tight braids, and she grips his forehead with both hands.

“I didn’t say anything.”

“It’s a boy with a bucket.”

Daniel touches her elbow.

“It’s crowded.”

“We can move.”

“No. I want to see.”

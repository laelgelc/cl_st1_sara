By the time Mara Venn returned to Bellweather Island, the sea had swallowed the road twice.

She waited in her car at the edge of the flooded causeway, listening to rain tap the windshield. Beyond the gray water, the island rose like the back of some sleeping animal: black cliffs, bent pines, a crooked line of cottages, and, at the highest point, the old lighthouse.

Its lamp had not shone for twenty-three years.

Mara knew because she had been there the night it went dark.

A yellow warning sign leaned beside the road.

CAUSEWAY PASSABLE AT LOW TIDE ONLY.

Someone had added, in fading red paint:

SOME THINGS SHOULD STAY CUT OFF.

Mara checked her watch. The tide was retreating. Beneath the water, the road appeared by degrees, shining with weeds and broken shells.

She drove across.

Her father’s house stood near the western cliffs, exactly as she remembered it and smaller than it had been in childhood. The blue paint had whitened with salt. One shutter hung loose. In the garden, dead stalks clicked together in the wind.

A man in a green coat was waiting on the porch.

“You’re late,” he said.

“Hello, Elias.”

He nodded once, as if greeting a stranger whose name he had forgotten.

Mara stepped from the car. The wind pulled at her black coat and carried the smell of kelp across the yard.

Elias had grown leaner, his face sharpened by island winters. There was gray in his beard. When Mara had left Bellweather, he had been seventeen and furious with everyone. Now he was forty and looked tired of the effort.

“The funeral?” she asked.

“Yesterday.”

Her hand tightened around the car keys. “The letter said Friday.”

“It was Friday.”

“Today is Friday.”

“It came forward. Storm’s due tonight. Reverend Saye wanted to get it done.”

Mara stared at him.

Elias looked toward the sea. “Dad would’ve preferred you missed it.”

There it was: the blade, still bright after all these years.

“Then everyone got what they wanted,” she said.

She took her suitcase from the trunk and carried it into the house.

Nothing inside had changed. Her father’s boots stood beside the stove, dark with oil. His tide charts covered the kitchen wall. A chipped red mug sat upside down near the sink. On the mantel was a photograph of Mara and Elias as children, both squinting in the sun. Between them stood their mother, one hand resting on each of their shoulders.

Their father was not in the picture. He had taken it.

Mara set down her suitcase.

“How did he die?”

Elias closed the door behind them. “Heart.”

“Quickly?”

“Yes.”

“Was anyone with him?”

“No.”

She waited for grief to come, but felt only an old, hollow pressure beneath her ribs.

Elias went to the table and picked up a brass key.

“He left this for you.”

It was long and heavy, green around the teeth.

Mara did not touch it. “The lighthouse?”

“Yes.”

“I thought the council owned it.”

“Dad bought it after they decommissioned it.”

“Why?”

“You know why.”

Outside, gulls screamed over the cliffs.

Mara finally took the key. It lay cold in her palm.

“He left instructions,” Elias continued. “You’re to go up before dark.”

She laughed once. “Of course he did.”

“If you don’t want to, give me the key.”

“Do you want it?”

“No.”

The answer came too quickly.

Mara studied her brother’s face. “What’s in there?”

“I haven’t been inside since that night.”

“Neither have I.”

“I know.”

Rain rattled suddenly against the windows. Elias moved to the stove and lit the burner beneath a kettle.

“The weather radio says the storm will pass north of us,” he said.

“And do you believe it?”

“No.”

They drank tea in silence. The clock above the door ticked too loudly. Mara remembered standing beneath it in a wet nightdress while her father shouted into the telephone. She remembered Elias crying at the kitchen table, though later he would deny it. She remembered the lighthouse beam sliding across the windows every twelve seconds.

“I’ll go now.”

Elias did not look at her. “Take the lower path. The cliff steps collapsed last winter.”

“Are you coming?”

“No.”

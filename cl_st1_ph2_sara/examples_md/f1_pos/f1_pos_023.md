## The Cost of Asking

“I have just been to see poor Mrs. Arnold,” said Mrs. Lyon, as she laid aside her bonnet. “She is very ill, Andrew—too ill, I fear, to sit at her needle; and the children look as if they had not eaten a full meal for a week.”

Andrew Lyon turned from the little account-book over which he had been bending.

“Is she still in arrears for her rent?”

“Ten dollars. The landlord has given her until to-morrow evening. He says he can wait no longer.”

“Ten dollars!” Lyon repeated, with a troubled countenance. “It is not a large sum; and yet, just now, it might as well be a hundred to us.”

“I know it,” replied his wife. “If we had it to spare, I would carry it to her this hour.”

“So would I.”

For a few moments both were silent. They knew something of narrow means themselves. The rent of their own modest dwelling was due in a week; there were bills unpaid, and winter was approaching. Yet the image of the sick widow and her three children remained before them.

At length Lyon’s face brightened.

“I have it!” he said. “There are ten men of my acquaintance, all in comfortable circumstances, and all reputed benevolent. I will ask each for one dollar. A dollar a-piece will scarcely be felt, and Mrs. Arnold’s rent will be paid.”

Mrs. Lyon did not answer immediately.

“You do not like the plan?” he asked.

“I like the object,” she replied. “But I fear you will find the work less pleasant than you imagine.”

“Nonsense! For so worthy an object? Why, Malcolm gave twenty dollars to the Mission Society last month, and Tompkins made a speech at the Benevolent Association. They will thank me for bringing the case to their notice.”

Lyon put on his coat and went forth in the glow of his newly awakened purpose.

His first call was upon Mr. Malcolm, a merchant whose name appeared frequently in printed lists of subscriptions. He found him alone in his counting-room.

“Good morning, Malcolm.”

“Ah, Lyon! Good morning. Sit down. What can I do for you?”

The words were cordial; and Lyon entered at once upon the story of Mrs. Arnold—her illness, her children, and the ten dollars of arrears of rent. Malcolm listened without interruption, though his eyes wandered more than once toward the papers on his desk.

“My plan,” concluded Lyon, “is to get one dollar from each of ten persons. I have called upon you first.”

“Hum!” said Malcolm. “A dollar?”

“Only a dollar.”

“There are so many calls of this kind,” observed the merchant, leaning back in his chair. “If a man gives to all, he will soon have nothing left.”

“Certainly. But this case has been examined. Mrs. Arnold is sober, industrious, and deserving. Sickness alone has brought her to want.”

“No doubt—no doubt. Still, one ought to be careful. Charity is often abused.”

Lyon felt the first chill upon his enthusiasm.

“I would not ask you,” he said, “if I were not satisfied.”

Malcolm sighed, drew a key from his pocket, and slowly opened his money drawer. After moving several coins about, as though hoping a piece of less value might answer the purpose, he took out a dollar.

“Well, there it is. But you must not make a habit of coming to me.”

“Certainly not,” replied Lyon, his face warming painfully. “I am obliged to you.”

As he left, Malcolm closed the drawer with an air of relief.

“That is the way money goes,” he muttered. “A dollar here and a dollar there! Lyon is a well-meaning man, but he takes these matters too much to heart.”

The second person upon Lyon’s list was Mr. Hartley, a gentleman fond of speaking of humanity in general.

“A widow with three children?” he said, when Lyon had explained his errand. “Very sad—very sad indeed. There is an astonishing amount of suffering in the world.”

“There is,” answered Lyon. “And a little timely aid will prevent much suffering in this instance.”

“True. But are there no relatives?”

“None able to assist her.”

“Has she applied to the public authorities?”

“She shrinks from the poorhouse; and surely she ought not to be driven there for want of ten dollars.”

“Perhaps not. Still, public relief exists for such cases. Private benevolence, unless regulated by principle, encourages dependence.”

Lyon’s heart sank; but he said quietly, “Will you give one dollar?”

Hartley frowned as if the question were an interruption to his argument. Then, with a forced smile, he handed over the sum.

“There. I give it, though I doubt the wisdom of the whole proceeding.”

Lyon thanked him and withdrew. The second dollar felt heavier in his pocket than the first.

Mr. Tompkins received him less graciously.

“Money!” he exclaimed. “Everybody wants money. What is it now?”

Again Lyon told his simple story.

“And you expect me to give a dollar?”

“I ask you to give it.”

Tompkins shifted in his chair. He had lately declared at a public meeting that no good citizen could remain indifferent to the cries of the unfortunate. But there had been applause at the meeting; here there was only Lyon, standing with an open hand and an anxious face.

“I suppose,” he said, with the manner of a man cornered, “that if I refuse, you will think me hard-hearted.”

“I have said nothing of the kind.”

“No; but that is what people mean when they come asking. A man is allowed no choice. He must give, or be set down as miserly.”

“You are entirely free to decline,” answered Lyon, wishing himself anywhere else.

“Oh, well! Since you have made the appeal, I suppose I must do something.”

He produced a dollar and laid it upon the table—not in Lyon’s hand.

The glow was gone now. It seemed to Lyon that cold water had been poured upon every benevolent feeling with which he had started. Yet, when he thought of Mrs. Arnold watching the hours pass, he could not abandon his task.

His next call was upon Mr. Peters, a cheerful church-going man who often lamented that the purse strings of the rich were so tightly drawn.

Peters heard him through, shook his head sympathetically, and said:

“Poor creature! Poor creature! I am really sorry for her.”

“Then you will aid her?”

“Why—yes, of course. But I have very little change.”

“A dollar is all I ask.”

“Just so. Unfortunately, a dollar is sometimes as hard to spare as ten. Business is dull. Expenses increase every year. My family costs me a great deal.”

Lyon glanced involuntarily around the richly furnished room.

Peters noticed the glance and coloured.

“Not that I would refuse a worthy object,” he added hastily. “Here is the dollar. I only wish you to understand that it is inconvenient.”

The fourth dollar was received; but Lyon felt almost as though he had taken it without right.

Mr. Evans, the fifth, gave after a long examination.

“Who was her husband? How long has he been dead? Why did he leave no property? What ages are the children? Does she take in washing? Has she sold her furniture? Is the landlord certain to dispossess her?”

Every answer was weighed as if the widow stood accused of some offence. At last Evans said:

“Well, your statement appears reasonable. I will risk a dollar.”

“Risk it!” The words wounded Lyon; nevertheless he accepted the coin. He now had five dollars.

Only half the sum was collected, and already his sensitive nature recoiled from another interview. But conscience urged him on. He went, though with reluctant steps, to Mr. Jones.

Jones had frequently been called generous, chiefly because he spent freely upon his own pleasures and sometimes gave away money when the humour seized him. Lyon found him preparing to dine.

“Be quick,” said Jones. “I am hungry as Job’s turkey.”

Lyon made his statement as briefly as possible.

Before he had finished, Jones broke in:

“No! Not a cent.”

Lyon paused.

“The woman is sick,” he said, “and has three children.”

“Hang all the poor widows!” returned Jones coarsely. “If I gave a dollar to every tale of distress, I should soon need somebody to beg for me.”

“I ask for only one.”

“And I say no. Besides, how do I know what becomes of the money? Half these stories are got up to cheat soft-hearted fools.”

“I know Mrs. Arnold personally.”

“Then help her yourself.”

The words struck Lyon like a blow; for Jones knew that he was a man of limited income.

“I intend to do what I can,” he replied.

“Very well. Charity begins at home—and ought to stay there, in my opinion. Good day.”

Lyon left without answering. He had gone out that morning in the belief that the simple mention of unquestionable distress would awaken immediate sympathy. He returned home disheartened, oppressed, and humiliated. The five dollars in his pocket seemed not gifts of charity, but witnesses to six painful scenes.

His wife looked at him anxiously.

“You have not succeeded?”

“I have obtained five dollars.”

“And the rest?”

“I could perhaps get it, if I continued. But I cannot ask another man.”

He then related what had passed. Mrs. Lyon listened with moist eyes.

“I feared it would be so,” she said. “People often praise benevolence more readily than they practise it.”

“But Mrs. Arnold must not suffer because I am ashamed.”

“No,” replied his wife. “She must not.”

They looked at each other. Each knew what the other intended before a word was spoken.

“There are five dollars in the drawer,” said Mrs. Lyon. “We had laid them by for the coal.”

“We can buy less coal and burn it carefully.”

“The weather may remain mild.”

“And if it does not,” said Lyon, “we are at least well and able to work.”

That evening the ten dollars were placed in Mrs. Arnold’s hand. Her rent was paid; the dreaded notice did not come; and her children were not turned into the street. She never knew how large a part of the sum had been supplied by those who could least afford it, nor what mortification the other half had cost.

Lyon kept his resolution. He never again solicited charitable aid from his acquaintances. When he could relieve distress from his own means, he did so; when he could not, he suffered in silence rather than expose himself to the cold looks, suspicious questions, and reluctant hands of men who claimed the name of benevolent.

Was this resolution right? Perhaps not. The poor may have lost assistance because the rich wounded the feelings of their advocate. But let us look less at Lyon’s error than at the cause which produced it.

We have not related this incident merely to show that five men gave a dollar and one refused. The lesson lies deeper. He who asks aid for another performs, in many cases, a sacrifice. He gives time; he surrenders ease; he subjects his motives to suspicion; and, if he possesses a delicate spirit, he pays in humiliation more than the careless donor pays in money.

Why, then, should he be met with coldness? Why compel him to defend the unfortunate as if he were pleading for a criminal? Why make a small gift with sighs, reproaches, and warnings, until its value is diminished by the manner of bestowing it?

There are men who subscribe publicly and refuse privately; who love humanity at a distance, yet turn impatiently from the widow at their door; who speak warmly of charity, provided no hand is laid upon their purse strings. Such benevolence is a garment worn for observation, not a principle living in the heart.

If we cannot give, let us refuse gently. If we can give, let us do so with kindness. And, above all, let us treat with respect the visiter who comes, not for himself, but for some suffering fellow-creature. The dollar may be ours; but the courage to ask it, the sympathy that prompted the errand, and the pain endured in its performance may represent a nobler charity than our own.

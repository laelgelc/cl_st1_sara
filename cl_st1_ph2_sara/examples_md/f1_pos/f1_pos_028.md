I got a hard jolt once when I was nineteen, and I ain’t never got rightly over it. I don’t suppose I ever will. A fellow can get kicked in the ribs by a horse, or cracked over the head with a singletree, or throwed out of a boxcar going thirty miles an hour, and after a while the place heals up and he can laugh about it. But there’s another kind of kick that don’t leave no mark where anybody can see it, and that’s the kind I got.

I give it to myself, too. That’s the worst of it.

If another fellow had done it I could have hunted him up and knocked his block off, supposing he wasn’t too big. But when the fellow is yourself, what are you going to do? You can’t follow yourself into an alley and lick yourself, though I’ve wished a thousand times I could. I’d have kicked that nineteen-year-old yap clear around the Sandusky track, and every time he come past the grandstand I’d have kicked him again.

But never mind that. I got to begin somewhere.

When I was sixteen I went away from home and worked with racehorses. My people wasn’t poor exactly, and they wasn’t rich exactly, and they had ideas about respectability. My mother had a cousin that kept a dry-goods store and wore spectacles with a gold chain, and the family thought that put us pretty near the upper circles. They wanted me to learn bookkeeping. I tried it two weeks. Columns of figures ain’t horses. You can’t rub their legs or talk to them or tell by their eye whether they’re going stale. I told the bookkeeping teacher what I thought of his profession, and he told me what he thought of me, and that made us even.

So I went off with a racing stable.

I wasn’t no jockey. I was too long in the legs and I kept growing in places where a jockey oughtn’t. I was a groom and swipe and general stable hand. I slept in barns and cheap hotels and once under a judges’ stand because all the stalls leaked. I washed sulkies, cooked bran mash, rubbed down horses, carried water, wrapped legs, walked colicky pacers half the night, and learned more useful things in six months than that bookkeeping boob could have taught me in six years.

A horse can’t talk, you know, but he tells you plenty. He tells you with his ears and his breathing and the way he sets one foot. A good groom knows when a horse has got speed in him and when he’s only making motions. He knows when the driver is holding him back, too. There’s men around a track who will swear by stopwatches and printed sheets, but a horse’s hide will tell you more than all their paper dope if you know how to read it.

I knew how. I’ll say that for myself, even if I was a fool about nearly everything else.

There was a fellow named Burt who taught me most of it. Burt was colored, and in those days plenty of men who couldn’t curry a mule without getting bit thought they was entitled to look down on him. He could have trained horses for any rich owner in Ohio if people had judged brains instead of skin, but they didn’t, and so he got stable wages while white skates took the credit. He didn’t complain much. He’d just grin and say, “Let the horse know who knows.”

And the horse generally did.

Burt could go into a stall with a nervous mare that had kicked three men through partitions, and in ten minutes she’d be breathing into his neck like a baby. He had long hands, and quiet ones. He taught me never to jerk at a frightened horse, because fear jerks back. He taught me to watch the left hind leg, because a horse can lie with his front end and tell the truth behind. He taught me that some drivers lose a heat because they have to, and some because they’ve been paid to, and some because the owner wants better odds next time.

That last thing has got considerable to do with my hard jolt.

I traveled around to county fairs and race meetings. Bucyrus, Lima, Findlay, Delaware, places like that. You’d see the same string of horsemen and tin-horn gamblers and lemonade fellows, and pretty soon you knew who was straight and who would steal the gold out of his own mother’s teeth. We’d ship the horses in boxcars, with a bunk rigged up at one end, and if you hit a rough crossing at night you’d wake up against the roof and come down into a feed sack. It was a fine life when you was sixteen and didn’t own anything that could break.

Burt and me worked one season for old man Harker, who owned a bay gelding called King Lightfoot. Now there was a horse. He could pace like his tail was tied to a cyclone. Harker had money in mills and lake shipping, and he owned horses because it pleased him to see other rich men lose. He had a son, too, though I never laid eyes on the son. I heard he was off at some college learning to part his hair and say “really” through his nose. Maybe he was a decent fellow. It don’t matter. I borrowed him later, that’s all.

I don’t mean I borrowed money off him. I borrowed his whole life.

But never mind that yet.

King Lightfoot had a bowed tendon one spring, and Burt and me brought him around when two veterinary men said he was through. We cooled the leg and bandaged it and walked him until I dreamed of walking. Old Harker gave the trainer a hundred dollars and give Burt five. He give me a cigar. I was seventeen and didn’t smoke, but I smoked that cigar until I got sick because I wanted Harker to see I was a man of the world. That ought to tell you what sort of boob I was.

At nineteen I quit the stable business for a while. There was a row over wages, and I had ideas. A nineteen-year-old fellow generally has enough ideas to ruin three governments. I come back near home with twenty-eight dollars, a pair of good shoes, and a suit I’d bought secondhand from a horse salesman who had got too fat for it.

It was a fine suit. Dark gray, with a little stripe you couldn’t see unless you come close. The coat pinched under the arms, but that only made me hold my shoulders back. I had a stiff collar that sawed my neck, a pearl scarfpin that wasn’t pearl, and a hat with a narrow black band. I even had gloves. Yellow ones. I looked like ready money from across the street.

Up close I may have looked like a groom dressed for the funeral of a banker, but I didn’t know that.

There was a racing meet at Sandusky that September, and I went. I only had eleven dollars left by then, because money goes fast when you’re practicing being prosperous. I paid admission, bought a program, and stood around with one glove in my hand and the other sticking out of my pocket, because I had noticed prosperous gentlemen didn’t seem to need both hands at once.

The day was bright and windy. Lake air come across the grounds carrying dust and the smell of horses, cigars, frying onions, and ladies’ perfume. The brass band was murdering a march near the grandstand. Bookmakers was yelling odds. Stable boys was leading horses in blankets, and the drivers sat around in bright silks, looking sleepy and important.

I felt at home and ashamed of feeling at home.

That sounds crazy, but I guess you know what I mean. I knew the people behind the barns—the swipes with straw in their hair, the drivers with dirty cuffs, the fellows washing mud off sulky wheels. They was my people. But I had that gray suit on, and I wanted the folks in the grandstand to think I’d never carried a manure fork in my life.

So I bought a cigar.

That cigar started things. Not all by itself, of course. A cigar ain’t got brains enough to ruin a man. It needs assistance.

I went into the bar under the grandstand and ordered whiskey because two men beside me ordered whiskey. I didn’t like whiskey much. It tasted like something you’d rub on a horse’s sprain. But I put it away and stared at myself in the mirror. The mirror showed a tall young fellow with his collar too high and fear in his eyes, but the whiskey said he was a big-bug.

Then I heard somebody behind me say, “Do you know anything about the fourth?”

I turned around quick. There was a young fellow about twenty-two, maybe, holding a cane. He had on a light overcoat and a Windsor tie, and everything about him seemed easy. That cane especially. I had never known any useful man to carry a cane, but he carried his as if it had grown onto him.

With him was two girls.

One was lively-looking, with brown eyes, and she was talking to the bartender as though bartenders had been invented for her amusement. The other stood a little back. She had on a blue dress and a small hat, and there was something quiet about her that made all the noise seem to move away from where she was.

That was Lucy.

I won’t describe her too much. A man always spoils it when he tries. He says hair and eyes and hands and so forth, and when he gets done he has built something that looks like a wax lady in a store window. Lucy wasn’t like that. She was alive, and when she looked at you she waited for what you might say, as if it could matter.

Craps amighty.

The young fellow with the cane was studying his program. “Benton’s Boy is favored,” he said. “But I’ve been told Little Prince has a chance.”

I knew the fourth race. It was a best-three-in-five pacing event, and I knew more about two of the horses than the bookmaker did. One was a chestnut called Foxfire, owned by Silas Harker—old man Harker’s brother—and trained by a fellow named Ed Mullen. I had worked with Ed. Foxfire could do a mile in fourteen if he had to, and nothing else in the race could get near that. But he’d been listed at twenty-one and a half, and the public thought his speed was gone.

I had seen Ed near the paddock that morning. He’d winked at me and said, “Watch him warm up, kid.”

Foxfire had warmed up slow, but he wasn’t tired. He was fighting the bit.

I knew the play. They meant to lose the first heat, let the odds climb, and then turn him loose. It wasn’t legal, and it wasn’t rare. Horse racing has got more honesty in it than outsiders think and less than insiders claim.

“Little Prince won’t do,” I said.

The young fellow looked at me. “No?”

“Foxfire.”

He smiled in a friendly way, not sarcastic. “He was nowhere last week.”

“He was nowhere because they wanted him nowhere.”

I said it quiet. That’s the way to sound like you know things. Any yap can holler. A wise man lets the other fellow lean in.

The lively girl leaned in. “Is this secret information?”

“You might call it that,” I said.

Lucy looked at me, and the whiskey moved up into my head and set off fireworks.

“How certain are you?” the young man asked.

“He’ll lose the first heat,” I said. “Maybe finish fourth. Don’t touch him until after that. Then bet all you’re willing to see burned up.”

“Why lose the first?”

“Odds.”

He nodded. He was no fool.

The lively girl said, “Wilbur, we’ve discovered a conspirator.”

That was his name, Wilbur. The lively girl was his cousin, Clara, and Lucy was his sister. They told me that later. Right then I was trying not to stare at Lucy.

Wilbur held out his hand. “Wilbur Ames.”

I shook it. And there it was—the open gate, the straight road, the chance to say my own honest name.

I didn’t take it.

“Arthur Harker,” I said.

Gee whizz.

Why Harker? Because the name was in my head. Because I was dressed in another man’s suit and smoking a cigar I didn’t like. Because Wilbur had a cane. Because Lucy’s dress was blue. Because I had been poor all my life and had just enough whiskey in me to resent it. Take your choice. They’re all excuses, and an excuse is only a lie after it has put on a clean collar.

“Harker?” Wilbur said. “Any relation to Silas Harker?”

“His nephew.”

That wasn’t enough for me.

“My father’s Jonathan Harker,” I said. “The horse owner.”

I might have stopped there. A sensible liar stops after one or two. I was an artist.

I told them my father owned King Lightfoot and a farm near Cleveland with forty broodmares. I told them I’d been educated by tutors, then sent East to school. I didn’t name the school because I couldn’t think of one quick enough that sounded expensive. I said I’d spent a summer in England looking at bloodstock. The nearest I had been to England was talking to my Welsh grandfather when I was little, and half of what he said was about turnips.

I told Wilbur I was traveling independently to learn the racing business from the ground up.

That part was true, only I had learned it from considerably farther down than he imagined.

Lucy asked, “Do you drive?”

“Horses?”

“Racing horses.”

“A little,” I said, with the modest air of a fellow who could drive Maud S. backward in the dark.

Actually I had jogged horses and worked them slow, and once I’d driven a heat at a bush meeting when the regular driver was drunk, but I never told them that. The truth would have sounded better than the lie, but when a fellow starts climbing a smoke ladder he don’t stop because the first rung holds.

The bell rang for the horses to score. We went outside together. Wilbur bought seats in the grandstand and wouldn’t let me pay, which was fortunate because I had six dollars and some change left and needed most of it for my own bet.

Foxfire come onto the track looking sleepy. Ed Mullen sat behind him with his hands low. I could see Foxfire’s mouth working on the bit.

“There,” I said. “Look at his ears.”

Lucy leaned close. Her shoulder touched my sleeve.

“What do his ears say?” she whispered.

“They say he’s mad.”

“With whom?”

“The whole human race.”

She laughed, and I nearly forgot the race.

At the word, they come down to the start in a line, sulky wheels flashing. The starter dropped them, and Little Prince took the pole. Foxfire broke at the first turn. It was a neat break—too neat. Ed got him back down, but he was six lengths behind. He moved up on the backstretch, then Ed steadied him, and Foxfire finished fourth, just like I said.

Clara clapped. “Our conspirator!”

Wilbur looked sideways at me. “You’re sure now?”

“More than ever.”

The odds went from three to one to seven to one. People had seen Foxfire’s break and quit him. Wilbur asked how much he ought to put down.

“That depends how much you can lose without your father hunting me with a shotgun.”

He laughed. “A hundred?”

A hundred dollars! He said it like he was ordering pie.

I tried not to swallow. “That’ll do.”

Clara had twenty dollars of her own. Lucy had ten. I put up my last five and kept the change for emergencies. I ought to have felt guilty leading them into it, but I knew Foxfire. There are times when knowing a thing gives a poor man a straight back. All those years sleeping in barns was worth something then.

The second heat started clean. Foxfire laid third to the quarter, moved outside at the half, and Ed gave him his head going into the last turn.

I knew the instant he went.

There’s a change when a real horse starts to race. He don’t only move faster. He gets bigger. The air seems to flatten behind him. Foxfire come around Little Prince like the other horse was tied to a post and won by three lengths, Ed looking back under his arm.

I most shouted. I most cried. I most climbed over two ladies and danced on the rail. But I was Arthur Harker, educated by tutors and familiar with English bloodstock, so I only nodded.

“I thought so,” I said.

Lucy’s eyes was shining. “You knew!”

That was the trouble. I did know, and because I knew one thing she believed all the rest.

Foxfire won the third heat by a nose, because Ed didn’t want to frighten the odds too much, and won the fourth by five lengths when Little Prince’s driver tried to pocket him. Wilbur collected a pile of money that looked to me like the treasury of the United States. Clara waved her winnings under my nose. Lucy had seventy dollars from her ten, more money than she had ever owned at once, she said.

“And you?” she asked.

“Thirty-five,” I said.

I tried to sound careless. Thirty-five dollars was more than I’d had in months.

Wilbur slapped my shoulder. “Dinner is on us.”

“It ought to be on Foxfire.”

“We can’t get him into the hotel.”

“You could if you told him there was oats in the lobby.”

That made Lucy laugh again. I kept saying things to make her laugh, and every laugh put me deeper in the hole.

We ate at the best hotel dining room in Sandusky, or it looked the best to me. There was white cloth on the tables and little lamps with red shades. A waiter in a black coat pulled Lucy’s chair out, and I watched Wilbur to see which fork to take.

I knew horses, but there was six pieces of silver around my plate, all threatening me.

Clara ordered for everybody. We had oysters, steak, potatoes, some sort of green stuff, and a pudding with fire poured over it. Wilbur bought champagne. I had tasted beer, whiskey, hard cider, and a patent medicine Burt kept for chills, but never champagne.

I pretended it was familiar.

“Not bad,” I said.

The bubbles climbed into my nose and I coughed into my napkin. Clara saw and grinned, but she didn’t expose me. She was O. K., Clara was. All of them was. That makes it worse, you know. If they had been stuck-up yaps, I could say my lying was self-defense. But they never once made me feel common. I did that myself.

Wilbur told us his father manufactured farm implements in Mansfield. Clara’s father was a lawyer. Lucy had finished at a girls’ school in June and was supposed to visit relatives in Chicago that winter. They spoke of people and towns I didn’t know. Every name sounded polished.

Then Wilbur asked where my family lived.

“Near Cleveland,” I said.

I described a stone house I’d once seen from a train. I gave it pillars, a music room, a library, and stables larger than the hotel. I said my mother spent part of each year in New York. My mother had never been farther east than Akron, and the only time she spent a month away from home was when Aunt Nell had pneumonia.

“What is your mother like?” Lucy asked.

That nearly stopped me.

“She’s—quiet,” I said.

That was true.

“And kind?”

“Yes.”

That was true, too.

For one second I could have told everything. I could have said, “Listen, my name ain’t Arthur Harker. My father don’t own a racehorse. My mother cooks over a coal stove and patches my socks when I’m home long enough to leave any.” Lucy was looking at me soft and serious. Maybe she’d have laughed. Maybe she’d have despised me. Maybe she’d have liked me better.

But Wilbur raised his glass and said, “To Foxfire and Arthur Harker.”

So I drank to a man who didn’t exist.

After dinner Clara said we ought to go to Cedar Point. There was an evening boat, and the weather was warm. Wilbur was for it at once. I had never been there except once with a stable crew, when we slept on the boat because a trainer had gambled away the hotel money. I didn’t mention that.

We went down to the dock. The lake was darkening, and lights come on along the shore. The steamer had music aboard. People walked the decks, ladies holding their hats in the wind. Wilbur and Clara got mixed up with another party they knew, and somehow Lucy and me was left by the rail.

I can tell you the color of every racehorse I handled twenty years ago, but I can’t tell you all Lucy and me talked about on that boat. Little things. That’s queer, ain’t it? A man remembers the lie that ruined him word for word, but the true things go soft at the edges.

She said she liked the lake because it looked as if it went everywhere.

I said it didn’t. Canada was over there somewhere.

She said, “You always have to know what’s on the other side, don’t you?”

I said I guessed I did.

She told me she wanted to travel, but she was frightened of traveling alone. I told her I wasn’t frightened of anything. That was another lie, though not a planned one. I was frightened that minute—frightened she would move away from me, frightened she would stay close, frightened she would ask one plain question.

The wind pushed a strand of hair across her cheek. She tucked it back with one finger. That’s one of the things I remember better than my own name.

At Cedar Point there was music and lights and a long walk under trees. The season was nearly over, so it wasn’t crowded. Some booths was shut. Paper streamers stirred along the railings. You could smell lake water, damp leaves, popcorn, and the perfume Lucy wore, though maybe the perfume is something I added afterward. A fellow can’t always tell what memory has bandaged up for him.

Wilbur and Clara went to ride something that swung boats back and forth. Lucy said it made her dizzy, so we walked.

The night was warm and soft and dark and sweet, like the inside of an orange if an orange could have stars in it. That ain’t a sensible comparison. I know it. But that’s how it was.

We went down where the trees stood near the water. Some had roots washed bare, reaching into the sand like arms and fingers. The band was playing behind us, not loud, and every now and then a wave folded over and went whispering back.

Lucy took my arm.

Nobody had ever took my arm that way. Girls had grabbed it running across roads, and a waitress in Tiffin once took it to throw me out of a kitchen, but this was different. Lucy laid her hand there because she wanted it there.

I began to shake.

“Aren’t you cold?” I asked.

“No.”

“I thought you shivered.”

“That was you.”

I said, “I ain’t—” and stopped.

She looked up. She had heard the “ain’t.” Of course she had. Tutors and Eastern schools don’t generally turn out fellows that say “ain’t” when they forget themselves.

But she only smiled.

“I thought wealthy horsemen never trembled,” she said.

“Only when the odds are long.”

“Are they long now?”

I couldn’t answer that. My throat had gone shut.

We stood looking at the lake. A light from a boat moved slow across the black water. Lucy said, “Sometimes I think if you believed hard enough you could walk straight out on it.”

“You’d get your shoes wet.”

“I suppose you’d tell me exactly how deep it was.”

“I’d find out first.”

“And if it was over your head?”

“I’d keep going.”

I was showing off, but I meant that. I would have walked into Lake Erie until my hat floated if she had asked me.

She moved closer. I could feel her shoulder and the little weight of her hand. All the grand talk and champagne and horse dope went out of me. I wasn’t Arthur Harker then. I wasn’t a groom, neither. I was just nineteen, and she was Lucy.

“I’m glad we met you,” she said.

“Because of the money?”

“No.”

That one word hit me harder than any speech could.

I turned toward her. She blushed, but she didn’t move away. I put my hand on hers. My hand was rough from harness leather, and hers was small and cool. I thought she’d notice the calluses and know everything.

She did notice.

“You don’t have the hands of an idle gentleman,” she said.

“I work with horses.”

“I like that better.”

There it was again—the gate standing open.

I could have told her.

I even started. “Lucy, there’s something—”

“What?”

The words was right behind my teeth. My true name. My father’s real work. The secondhand suit. The eleven dollars. The barns. Burt. All of it.

Then Wilbur and Clara come hollering through the trees, and the gate slammed shut.

“Boat leaves in twenty minutes!” Wilbur called.

Clara looked from Lucy to me and knew something, because girls like Clara always do. She caught Wilbur’s arm and said, “We can walk slowly.”

So we all went back, them ahead and Lucy and me behind.

Near the dock she stopped under a tree.

“You didn’t tell me the something,” she said.

“It can wait.”

“Until tomorrow?”

That’s when I learned they was leaving the next morning. Wilbur had business at home, and Lucy and Clara was going with him. Their train left at ten-fifteen.

“I’ll see you at the station,” I said.

“And we’ll write,” Lucy said.

Write.

The word turned the whole lake cold.

She asked for my address.

I couldn’t give the Harker farm, because I didn’t know its address. I couldn’t give my own address, because Arthur Harker didn’t live there. I could have confessed. I could have said, “Send it to John Mercer”—that was my name—“care of my mother, on Willow Street.”

Instead I invented an address on Euclid Avenue in Cleveland. I picked a number I’d seen painted on a delivery wagon. Four hundred and eighteen.

She repeated it. “Mr. Arthur Harker, four hundred and eighteen Euclid Avenue.”

“Yes.”

“And you’ll write to me in Mansfield?”

“Yes.”

She told me the street and number. I remember it yet. I won’t tell it to you. Some things a fellow can keep, can’t he?

On the steamer back, Wilbur and Clara stayed away from us on purpose. Lucy and me sat behind a lifeboat where the wind didn’t reach much. She talked about winter and Chicago and how spring seemed a long way off. I said spring come quick. We made plans without calling them plans.

She would write first.

I would come to Mansfield.

Maybe next summer we could meet at Cedar Point again.

Every word built a room in a house that couldn’t stand.

When the boat reached Sandusky, we walked to their hotel. Wilbur shook my hand and told me I was a prince. Clara whispered, “Don’t be a fool,” which was sound advice delivered too late.

Lucy waited on the hotel steps.

Wilbur and Clara went in.

“I suppose this is good night,” she said.

“I suppose.”

Neither of us moved.

Then she said my false name. “Arthur.”

I kissed her.

I had kissed girls before, but those kisses ain’t in the same language. Lucy put both hands against my coat, and for a second the whole world stopped racing. No wheels, no trains, no horses, no clocks. Everything held still.

Then she pulled back, frightened and happy.

“You will write?”

“I will.”

“You promise?”

“Yes.”

That was the blackest lie of all, because I wanted it to be true.

I watched her go through the hotel door. I stood there until the clerk stared at me through the glass. Then I walked away.

I didn’t sleep. I had a room at a cheap place near the tracks, not the hotel I had let them assume I was staying in. The bed sagged, and freight trains bumped cars together all night.

I sat on the edge of that bed with thirty dollars in my pocket and tried to find a way out.

I could go to the station and tell her. That was one way.

I could write from my real address and explain. That was another.

I could rent a post-office box in Cleveland under the false name. But then what? Keep lying forever? Invent the stone house, the mother in New York, the English horses, until I had to hire actors to be my family?

I considered going to old man Harker and asking permission to use his son’s name. That champagne had not entirely left me.

Toward morning I decided I would tell Lucy at the station. I practiced it.

“My name is John Mercer.”

No, too sudden.

“Lucy, I deceived you because I was ashamed.”

That sounded like a book.

“I ain’t Harker’s son. I’m a swipe.”

That was nearer.

I washed, put on the gray suit, and walked to the station at nine. Their train stood under the shed breathing steam. Porters rolled trunks. People hugged and argued and bought papers. I saw Wilbur first, then Clara, then Lucy.

She wore the blue dress again.

When she saw me, her face changed. I don’t mean she smiled only with her mouth. The sight of me lit through her. Nobody had ever looked that glad to see me, and nobody ever has since.

There went my courage.

If she had been cool, I could have confessed. If she had looked doubtful, I could have confessed. But she believed in me so completely that I couldn’t bear to smash myself in front of her.

That sounds noble. It wasn’t. I was a coward.

Wilbur shook my hand. “Come to Mansfield.”

“I will.”

Clara looked hard at me. “You’d better.”

Lucy and me walked a little way down the platform.

“I nearly thought you wouldn’t come,” she said.

“I said I would.”

“You’ll get my letter in two days.”

“Yes.”

“And answer at once?”

“Yes.”

The conductor called, “All aboard.”

I felt the truth rise up again. My heart was pounding like a pacer at the quarter pole.

“Lucy—”

She waited.

There was still time. Ten seconds, maybe. A whole life can get through a gate in less.

“I—” I said.

The conductor shouted again. Wilbur called her. Steam drifted between us.

“I’ll write,” I finished.

She touched my hand and climbed aboard.

I stood beside the car. She opened the window and leaned out.

“Good-bye, Arthur!”

Good-bye, nobody.

The train started. I walked along with it, then trotted, then stopped. Lucy waved until the rear car slid past the end of the platform.

I could have outrun that train. Don’t tell me I couldn’t. I could have run faster than Foxfire, faster than King Lightfoot, faster than any horse ever foaled. I could have jumped on the back platform, busted through the door, and yelled my real name.

Instead I stood there with my yellow gloves in my hand.

Two days later Lucy’s letter would have reached Cleveland. A carrier would have taken it to four hundred and eighteen Euclid Avenue, if there was such a number. Maybe it was a dentist’s office. Maybe a widow lived there. Maybe it was an empty lot.

Then somebody would stamp it.

UNKNOWN.

NO SUCH PERSON.

RETURN TO SENDER.

That was what I had made myself: no such person.

I wrote Lucy’s address on a piece of paper that morning. I carried it for six months. I wrote her seventeen letters and mailed none. In some I confessed everything. In some I pretended Arthur Harker had died. In one I said I had gone to South America to breed horses. That was a particularly fine piece of foolishness.

Why didn’t I mail the truth? Pride, I suppose. Shame. The longer I waited, the uglier it got. After a week I had not only lied; I had let her discover it alone. After a month I imagined her laughing with Clara. After a year I imagined she hated me. I preferred that to finding out she pitied me.

I went back to horses. Burt asked what was eating me, and I said nothing. He watched me a while and said, “You lost something you never owned.”

He was wrong. I owned it for one night.

That’s what hurts.

Years afterward I heard Wilbur Ames had gone into his father’s business. I heard Clara married a doctor. I never heard for certain what become of Lucy. Once a man told me an Ames girl had married somebody in Chicago. Maybe it was her. Maybe it wasn’t. I didn’t ask twice.

I could look her up now, I suppose. People say everything can be found if you hire the right detective or write the right clerk. But what would I say? “Madam, I’m the dead young man from Cedar Point. The one who kissed you under a false name.”

No. Let her keep him if she kept anything—the rich fool with tutors and English horses. He was better dressed than I was, anyhow.

Sometimes I blame Wilbur’s cane. If he hadn’t carried it, maybe I’d have told my right name. Sometimes I blame his Windsor tie, or the whiskey, or that gray suit, or old Harker for owning too many horses, or the fellow who sold me the suit. A man can spread blame around like manure if he’s got a fork and enough time.

But I know who did it.

I did.

I thought being poor made me unworthy of Lucy, so I invented a fellow worthy of her. Then that fellow took her away from me. That’s the joke, if you want to laugh.

Gee whizz, I laughed myself once. I laughed until I got sick.

And if anybody tells you a lie can’t win a race, don’t believe him. Mine won every heat. It come from behind, took the pole, and beat the truth under the wire by half a length.

Only there wasn’t no purse.

There was just a blue dress moving behind a train window, a girl waving to a man who never lived, and me on the platform—John Mercer, horse groom, thirty dollars in his pocket, nineteen years old, and the biggest gosh-darn fool in Ohio.

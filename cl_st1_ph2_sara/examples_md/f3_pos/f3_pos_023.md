# SOMETHING BETTER THAN MONEY

## I

“You have promised him, then?”

“No! Oh, Jerry, how can you ask me?”

Jerry Trelevan turned sharply from the window. The little room was already darkening, though it was scarcely four o’clock, and his handsome face showed pale and impatient against the grey square of the glass.

“But you will promise him,” he said. “You must, Vi.”

Violet stood on the other side of the hearth, one hand resting upon the mantelpiece. The fire was low. There was no coal in the scuttle, and both of them knew it. They knew also that the landlady’s bill lay unanswered in Jerry’s pocket, that Violet’s last trinket had gone to the pawnbroker yesterday, and that the remains of the Trelevan fortune were represented by a bundle of railway certificates which no sane man would buy for waste-paper.

“I cannot marry a man I do not love,” she said.

Jerry gave a short, exasperated laugh.

“People say that when they have a hundred a year and an aunt in Cheltenham. We haven’t either. We have seven shillings, an unpaid bill, and a railway that begins nowhere and ends in a bog.”

“You put the money into it.”

“Yes, yes! You needn’t remind me.”

“I did not mean—”

“Of course you did not.” His tone softened immediately. Jerry could never remain angry with Violet for more than a minute. They were twins, and since childhood her distress had possessed for him the force of a command. He crossed the hearthrug and caught her hands. “Little Vi, don’t look like that. I know I made a fool of the thing. But how was I to guess that old Prendergast would die, the board quarrel, and the Government refuse the concession? Everyone said the San Marco and Northern was a certainty.”

“Everyone except Mr. Kenyon.”

Jerry released her hands.

“That is hardly fair. Kenyon said it wanted capital. He did not say it would fail.”

“He advised you not to invest.”

“Well, what of it? He can afford to be right.”

Violet turned her face away. The movement was instinctive, but Jerry saw it and pressed his advantage.

“Dick Kenyon has forty thousand a year,” he said. “Perhaps fifty. Nobody knows. Mines in Arizona, cattle somewhere else, shipping interests, half a town named after him—”

“And you think that is a reason why I should marry him?”

“I think it is a reason why you should not dismiss him because his shoulders are too broad and his eyes too sleepy.”

“I do not dislike him because he is ugly.”

“Then why do you dislike him?”

She was silent.

Jerry regarded her with the merciless shrewdness of an affectionate brother.

“You don’t know,” he said triumphantly. “That is the truth. He frightens you because he never chatters and bows and plays the fool like the men you used to meet. He sits still and looks at you. That is the whole of his crime.”

“He looks as if he knew what one were going to say before one said it.”

“Probably he does. He is a clever fellow.”

“And as if it did not greatly matter whether one said it at all.”

“Nonsense! He has been in love with you for six months.”

Violet shivered.

“Don’t, Jerry!”

“What am I to do? We cannot live on air. I might get a clerkship, perhaps—eighty pounds a year, if anyone will take a man who knows horses better than ledgers. You could teach music to horrible children at two shillings an hour. Between us we might keep body and soul together, if neither of us fell ill and our boots lasted for ever.”

“I am willing.”

“I am not.”

His hands descended upon her shoulders. There was affection in the grasp, but also the impatient mastery of one who had always decided for them both.

“You are not made for it,” he said. “You think you are, because you have never tried. You would grow thin and shabby. You would sit in underground trains with your music-roll on your knees. You would smile at insolent women and coax stupid children. And I should know that one word from you could have saved us both.”

“Saved us both!” she repeated faintly.

Jerry coloured, but did not retreat.

“Well—yes. Kenyon offered me a place in one of his London companies if—if things came right between you.”

“So that is it.”

“No! Not as payment. He isn’t that sort. He merely knows I need work.”

“And he is to buy me, and throw you in an office as part of the bargain?”

Jerry’s face hardened.

“You can put it brutally if you choose.”

“Is there another way to put it?”

“Yes. You can say that a decent man loves you, that he offers you everything a woman can reasonably desire, and that by marrying him you rescue your brother from the ruin his own folly has made.”

She winced at the last words. Jerry saw it and instantly kissed her forehead.

“I am a beast,” he said. “But, Vi, I am at the end of things. I have been lying awake for a week. I cannot see you suffer because I gambled like an idiot. Kenyon is honest. He is patient. He would never bully you.”

“No,” she whispered. “He would only look at me.”

There came a knock at the outer door.

Both stood motionless.

A moment later the landlady entered, wearing the expression of injured dignity with which she always announced visitors whose clothes surpassed her furniture.

“Mr. Richard Kenyon,” she said.

Dick Kenyon appeared behind her.

He was a large, loosely built man of perhaps thirty-five, though the stillness of his face made him look older. His evening coat fitted him badly across the shoulders; his hair possessed an obstinate roughness no barber had subdued; and his heavy-lidded grey eyes seemed almost drowsy. Yet when the landlady withdrew, closing the door with reluctance, those eyes went immediately to Violet and remained upon her.

Jerry cleared his throat.

“Come in, Kenyon. Beastly afternoon.”

“So I noticed,” Dick said.

His voice was slow and deep, with a faint American drawl remaining from years spent across the Atlantic.

He took the chair Jerry offered and sat with his hands upon his knees. Violet had once thought those hands ugly—too square, too brown, too powerful. To-day they appeared to fill the room.

“I reckon you know why I came,” he said.

Jerry looked at Violet.

She stood very straight, but her eyes had grown wild. They were the eyes of a creature that sees the gate closing across its only road of escape.

“Perhaps,” she said.

Dick’s gaze moved briefly to Jerry.

“Could I speak to your sister alone?”

“Certainly. Of course.” Jerry seized his hat. At the door he hesitated. “I shall not be far away, Vi.”

She made no answer.

When he had gone, the silence became terrible. Dick did not attempt to break it at once. He leaned forward, looking into the poor little fire, and Violet wondered with painful anger whether he meant to prolong her ordeal deliberately.

At last he said, “Your brother tells me you know what I want.”

“Yes.”

“He also tells me you’re willing to hear it.”

Her fingers closed upon the mantelpiece.

“Yes.”

Dick rose. His movements were never hurried, but there was nothing clumsy in them. He stood before her, great and quiet and unmistakably strong.

“Miss Trelevan,” he said, “I haven’t much gift for this kind of talk. I love you. I’ve loved you since the night you laughed at Lady Parminster’s green parrot and then apologized to the bird. I want you to marry me. I’d try to make things easy for you. That is about all I can say.”

Violet’s lips moved, but no sound came.

He waited.

“Mr. Kenyon—Dick—I—”

His face altered slightly at the use of his name. For an instant the drowsiness left his eyes, and she saw in them a brightness that frightened her more than their former calm.

“Don’t hurry,” he said gently.

The gentleness almost undid her. If he had been crude, confident, triumphant, she could have hated him honestly. But he stood waiting as though her smallest wish mattered, and her own purpose became more shameful with every second.

She thought of Jerry walking the wet streets, of the seven shillings, of the empty coal-scuttle.

“I will marry you,” she said.

Dick remained absolutely still.

“You mean that?”

“I have said it.”

“That isn’t quite the same thing.”

She lifted her head desperately.

“I promise.”

He drew a long breath.

Then he took her hand, but instead of raising it to his lips he merely held it, lightly and carefully, as though it were some frightened living thing.

“Thank you,” he said.

And Violet, who had expected possession, found patience harder to bear.

## II

The engagement lasted three weeks before fortune performed its first somersault.

During those weeks Dick behaved with a consideration which exasperated Jerry and bewildered Violet. He brought no diamonds, though Jerry hinted that diamonds were usual. He did not insist upon daily visits. He never claimed the privilege of a lover unless Violet unmistakably granted it; and when she shrank, as she often did, he appeared not to notice.

“You ought to kiss her occasionally,” Jerry told him one afternoon, with brotherly candour. “She’ll think you don’t care.”

Dick’s heavy eyes rested upon him.

“When your advice is wanted, sonny, I’ll send for it.”

Jerry laughed. “You are the queerest lover in London.”

“Maybe.”

Violet, entering at that moment, heard the last words. A faint colour touched her cheeks. Dick rose and moved a chair for her.

“I’m taking Jerry to dine at the club,” he said. “Unless you object.”

“Why should I?”

“I don’t know. It seemed polite to ask.”

Everything he said seemed to contain some concealed knowledge. She could not determine whether he understood her bargain with Jerry or remained innocently happy. If he understood, his kindness was terrible; if he did not, her deception was worse.

Once, as he took leave, he paused with his hand upon the door.

“You’re afraid of me,” he said.

Violet’s breath caught.

“No.”

“You needn’t say that. I’m not blaming you.”

“I am not afraid.”

“Then look at me.”

She tried, but failed.

He came back, stopping at a distance from her.

“I don’t want anything you aren’t ready to give,” he said. “Remember that.”

“Why are you so patient?”

A curious smile disturbed his rugged face.

“Because impatience wouldn’t help my case.”

“Your case?”

“A man ought to know when the jury is against him.”

Before she could answer, he was gone.

That night Violet cried with her face buried in the pillow. She told herself that it was aversion, shame and dread of the future. Yet beneath those feelings something else had begun to stir—something warm and reluctant, which she refused to name.

The change in their fortunes came through a newspaper paragraph.

Jerry burst into the sitting-room shortly before breakfast, half dressed and wholly incoherent.

“Vi! Where are the certificates? The San Marco shares—where did I put them?”

“In the black dispatch-box.”

“Key?”

“You have it.”

“No, I haven’t. Yes, I have. Great heavens, what a turn-up!”

He flung open a drawer, scattering gloves, letters and old bills across the carpet.

“What has happened?”

“Happened? They’ve risen! They’re at twelve.”

Violet stared.

“They were eightpence yesterday.”

“Exactly! Twelve pounds—not shillings. Twelve pounds, Vi!”

He found the key at last and dragged the battered dispatch-box from beneath the writing-table. His hands shook so violently that Violet took the key from him.

“Why have they risen?”

“New capital. Reorganization. A syndicate is taking over the line. They say the northern extension is to be completed after all.”

He snatched the certificates and began counting them.

“Five hundred shares. Bought at seven pounds—then down to nothing—and now twelve! Six thousand pounds, Violet! And if they rise further—”

“Will they?”

“My dear girl, they’ll go like a spring tide. Prendergast always said the traffic possibilities were enormous.”

“Prendergast also lost all his money.”

Jerry waved this aside.

“We are saved! Better than saved. If they reach twenty, we shall have ten thousand clear.”

Violet sat down.

Her first emotion was relief, so sudden and exquisite that she felt faint. They were not helpless. Jerry need not accept Dick’s employment. She need not marry—

The thought stopped.

Jerry, following it more rapidly, struck the table with his fist.

“Of course! The engagement.”

She looked at him.

“We can end it now,” he said.

A coldness passed through her.

“We?”

“You, naturally. But I shall see Kenyon.”

“No.”

“Why not?”

“Because I promised him.”

Jerry stared as though she had spoken in another language.

“You promised him when we were ruined.”

“I promised him.”

“Oh, come, Vi! Don’t be quixotic. The whole situation has altered.”

“Has it?”

“Completely. You accepted because you thought there was no other way.”

Her face burned.

“You knew that?”

“My dear child, I suggested it.”

“And does he know?”

Jerry’s eyes shifted.

“I don’t suppose he is blind. But there is no need to rake up unpleasant details. I’ll tell him circumstances have changed. He is a man of the world.”

Violet rose.

“You shall not tell him anything.”

“You don’t imagine I shall let you sacrifice yourself to a phrase?”

“It is my promise.”

“Given under pressure.”

“Your pressure.”

Jerry flushed.

“Very well, blame me. I deserve it. But do not make two blunders because I made one.”

She moved to the window. Rain blurred the roofs opposite, and beyond them the city lay under a smoky, impenetrable sky.

“Perhaps,” she said slowly, “Mr. Kenyon will wish to release me.”

“Wish it? He’ll have to.”

Her head turned.

“Have to?”

“We are no longer beggars. That puts us in a position to settle the affair decently.”

“Settle it?”

Jerry was too excited to hear the warning in her voice.

“There may be expenses. He has made arrangements, perhaps. There’s the house he spoke of taking. We could offer compensation.”

For a second Violet did not understand. Then the blood rushed to her face.

“Money?”

“Not in that vulgar sense.”

“In what sense, then?”

“Business is business. He entered into the engagement believing—”

“Stop!”

Her voice was so sharp that Jerry fell silent.

“You persuaded me to sell myself to him,” she said. “Now you propose to buy me back.”

“Violet!”

“That is what you mean.”

“I mean to preserve your happiness.”

“You never asked whether I had any happiness to preserve.”

She turned from him, trembling.

Jerry watched her with slowly widening eyes.

“You don’t mean—you cannot mean that you care for Kenyon?”

“No!”

The denial came too quickly. They both heard it.

Jerry’s mouth fell open; then he laughed.

“Well! This is turning up trumps indeed.”

“I do not care for him,” she repeated, more faintly. “I only know that I cannot behave dishonourably.”

“Honour is an admirable thing, but marriage is a long price to pay for it.”

“There is nothing else left to me.”

“Nothing else? You have money now.”

She faced him with sudden anger.

“Money! Must everything come back to money?”

Jerry sobered.

“No,” he said. “Not everything. But this began with it.”

That silenced her.

He gathered up the certificates, whistling beneath his breath.

“I shall see Kenyon,” he said. “Not to offer compensation, if that offends your fine feelings, but to discover where we stand.”

“Jerry, I forbid you.”

He smiled and kissed her.

“My dear Vi, you have been forbidding me things since we were four. It has never once been effective.”

Before she could reach the door, he was gone.

## III

Dick Kenyon received Jerry in a private room at his hotel.

He was seated at a broad writing-table littered with cablegrams. A map lay open before him, crossed by a thick red line which ran northward between two mountain ranges. Jerry noticed neither map nor cablegrams. He came in buoyantly, carrying his hat and gloves in one hand.

“Congratulations, old man,” he said. “Good news all round.”

Dick looked up.

“For whom?”

“For everyone. Haven’t you seen the market?”

“I’ve seen it.”

“San Marco at twelve.”

“So I hear.”

Jerry drew a chair forward and sat astride it.

“This puts Vi and me on our feet again. I always knew the line was sound if capital could be found.”

“You had less confidence last week.”

“Temporary depression. Every great undertaking has one.”

Dick folded a cablegram with deliberate precision.

“What do you want, Trelevan?”

The direct question checked Jerry’s enthusiasm.

“Well—the fact is—this naturally affects Violet’s position.”

“How?”

“She is no longer dependent upon anyone.”

“She was never dependent on me.”

“You know what I mean. When she accepted you, things were pretty desperate.”

Dick’s face did not alter, but one brown hand closed upon the cablegram.

“Go on.”

Jerry crossed one ankle over the other and attempted an air of worldly ease.

“I do not want to put the thing disagreeably. You behaved handsomely. More than handsomely. But engagements are sometimes entered into under mistaken conditions. When those conditions change, sensible people make a fresh arrangement.”

“What arrangement?”

“That is for us to discuss.”

“No,” Dick said. “It isn’t.”

Jerry frowned.

“I beg your pardon?”

“There’s nothing for you and me to discuss.”

“I represent my sister.”

“Did she send you?”

The question struck cleanly. Jerry hesitated.

“Not precisely.”

“Did she ask you to break the engagement?”

“She is naturally reluctant to hurt your feelings.”

“Did she ask you?”

“No. But as her brother—”

Dick rose.

He did not move quickly, yet Jerry found himself getting to his feet as well. The American’s stillness had become formidable.

“As her brother,” Dick said, “you pressured her into accepting me. Now you’ve seen a rise in the market and come along to take her back. Is that the proposition?”

Jerry’s colour deepened.

“I won’t permit you to insult me.”

“I’m trying hard not to.”

“You must understand that I am thinking of Violet’s happiness.”

“You thought of it before?”

“I believed she would be secure with you.”

“Secure. That’s a fine, affectionate word.”

“You have no right—”

“I have the right to ask one question. Does Violet want to be free?”

Jerry looked away.

“I believe so.”

“That wasn’t the question.”

“She does not love you.”

The words fell brutally into the room.

For the first time Dick’s composure broke. His face whitened beneath its bronze, and the cablegram crumpled in his grasp. The change lasted only an instant.

“You’d better let her tell me that,” he said.

Jerry, conscious that he had wounded him, became more expansive.

“There is no need for melodrama. We can deal with this like men of the world. If you have incurred expense—”

Dick’s head came forward.

“What?”

Jerry’s courage faltered, but pride carried him on.

“I said, if there are financial considerations, we should be willing to offer some reasonable—”

“Compensation?”

The word was softly spoken.

Jerry nodded.

For perhaps ten seconds Dick did not move. Then he came round the table.

“You get out,” he said.

“Kenyon—”

“Get out before I throw you out.”

“You cannot cowhide me with your infernal Western manner!”

“No,” Dick agreed. “There isn’t room. But the corridor’s wide.”

Jerry snatched up his hat.

“This is outrageous! I came to treat you honourably.”

“You came to trade your sister a second time.”

The words struck harder than a blow. Jerry went scarlet.

“That is a lie.”

“Then prove it. Keep out of her affairs and let her speak.”

“She will speak. And you will find that money cannot purchase a woman’s affection.”

A bitter smile touched Dick’s mouth.

“I found that out some time ago.”

Jerry reached the door. There he turned.

“For what it may be worth, I am sorry I mentioned compensation.”

“It isn’t worth much.”

“I might have phrased it differently.”

“You might have thought differently.”

Jerry departed, closing the door with a dignity spoiled by the fact that his glove caught in it.

Alone, Dick remained standing. The heavy calm dropped from him as a coat might have dropped. He strode to the window, returned, seized the cablegrams and read them without seeing a word.

“Doesn’t love you,” he said aloud.

His voice sounded strange.

He had known it, or believed he knew it. From the beginning there had been fear in Violet’s eyes, gratitude in her words, and no love anywhere. Yet hope is an obstinate gambler. A softened look, a hand not immediately withdrawn, the warmth that had lately trembled into her voice—he had hoarded such trifles and built upon them.

Now Jerry had spoken the truth with a fool’s cruelty.

Dick sat down, pressing both hands against his temples.

A knock came. His secretary entered.

“Another cable from New York, sir. The directors require your confirmation before announcing the second subscription.”

Dick took the paper.

“Tell them to wait.”

“They say delay may cause the shares to fall sharply.”

“Let them fall.”

The secretary stared.

“Sir?”

“I said wait.”

“Yes, sir.”

When the door closed, Dick looked again at the map. He had risked a large sum upon the San Marco and Northern—not because Jerry owned shares in it, though that fact had first called the line to his attention, but because his engineers had reported coal, timber and a viable mountain route. The railway was sound. In another year it might be immensely valuable.

He could have told Jerry so. He could have stabilized the market that hour.

Instead he locked the cablegrams in his desk.

“Let them fall,” he repeated.

It was not revenge, he told himself. He wanted truth. Violet had accepted him when she believed herself poor. If sudden wealth made her repudiate him, the matter would end. If the wealth vanished and she still asked her freedom—

He thrust the thought away.

By noon next day the San Marco shares had fallen from twelve pounds to nine. By three they stood at four. Before the market closed, a rumour that the new syndicate had withdrawn sent them below one pound.

Jerry reached home at half-past four, dishevelled and grey.

Violet was waiting.

“Well?” she asked.

He dropped into a chair.

“They have collapsed.”

She did not understand at first.

“The shares?”

“Worthless again. Some hitch in New York. The capital has not been confirmed.”

He covered his face.

Violet stood very still.

“And Mr. Kenyon?”

Jerry lowered his hands.

“I saw him yesterday.”

“You did what I forbade?”

“I thought—I believed—”

“What did you say?”

He tried to evade her eyes, failed, and confessed with miserable abruptness.

“I told him you did not love him. I said the engagement must end. I—I mentioned compensation.”

Violet made a sound as if he had struck her.

“You offered him money?”

“Only if there had been expense.”

“You offered him money to release me when we believed ourselves rich. And now we are poor again.”

“Vi, forgive me.”

She turned away.

On the writing-table lay the cheap paper upon which household accounts were kept. She took a sheet and sat down.

“What are you doing?”

“Writing to him.”

“To say what?”

“The truth.”

Jerry sprang up.

“You cannot! Wait until to-morrow. The shares may recover.”

She looked at him, and he stopped.

“That is precisely why I must write now,” she said. “Before anything changes again.”

Her pen trembled so that the first word was almost illegible.

Dear Mr. Kenyon—

She tore the sheet across.

“No,” she whispered. “Not that.”

She began again.

Dear Dick,

I must tell you everything which I ought to have told you before. I accepted you because we were poor and because Jerry believed I could not support myself. I was frightened, and weak, and I let you think that my promise was freely given. Yesterday, when we believed the shares valuable, Jerry went to ask for my release. To-day they are worthless again. I cannot remain engaged to you now without being more ashamed than I already am.

Please set me free. It is the only thing I have any right to ask.

Violet.

She folded the letter and addressed it.

Jerry watched her painfully.

“Will you forgive me?”

“Not yet,” she said.

Then, seeing his face, she put both arms about his neck.

“Oh, Jerry, Jerry! I love you—but what have we done?”

## IV

Dick came the following evening.

All day Violet had expected a letter. With every footstep upon the stairs she had risen; with every footstep passing the door she had sat down again. By six o’clock her nerves were so strained that the quiet ticking of the clock seemed an intolerable noise.

Jerry had gone out. Whether from tact or cowardice, she did not know.

At seven there came the firm double knock she had heard so often.

Dick entered without haste. He carried Violet’s letter in his breast pocket. His face was composed, almost phlegmatic; but beneath that outward stillness he was in a ferment of anxiety. He had fought men, drought, fire and financial ruin without the fear which now tightened about his heart.

Violet stood beside the hearth. The scuttle contained coal to-night, purchased with Jerry’s last sovereign, and the little flames cast moving gold across her white dress.

“You received my letter,” she said.

“Yes.”

“Then you know.”

“I know what you wrote.”

She clasped her hands.

“It was all true.”

“I guessed most of it.”

Her head lifted.

“You guessed?”

“I knew Jerry was pushing you. I knew you were scared. I hoped there might be something else.”

“There was not.”

The lie hurt her as she uttered it.

Dick looked down into the fire.

“The San Marco has dropped again,” he said.

“Yes.”

“I reckon Jerry told you.”

“This is not about the railway.”

“No?”

“No. That is why I wrote before—before it could change again. I will not wait to discover whether we are rich or poor and shape my conduct accordingly.”

“That sounds fair.”

His quietness tortured her. She had imagined anger, contempt, perhaps even relief. This grave courtesy gave her nothing against which to defend herself.

“You must despise me,” she said.

“I don’t.”

“You ought.”

“I’m not much guided by what I ought to feel.”

“I accepted you for your money.”

“You accepted me because you loved your brother and saw no other way out.”

“That does not make it better.”

“Makes it different.”

She turned upon him.

“Why do you excuse me? Why don’t you say what you think? Jerry told you I did not love you. He tried to buy back my promise. And now that the shares are worthless, I ask you to release me. You must see how vile it looks.”

“I see how it looks.”

“And still you stand there as if nothing could move you!”

Dick’s eyes came to her face.

“That’s where you’re wrong.”

There was something in his voice that stopped her. She saw then that his stillness was not indifference but control, and that behind it lay suffering as violent as her own. The discovery sent a pang through her, sharp and astonishing.

He drew her letter from his pocket.

“You ask to be free,” he said. “You are free.”

She had wanted those words. For three weeks she had told herself that freedom was all she desired. Yet now they seemed to empty the room of warmth and air.

“So easily?” she whispered.

“Would you rather I made a scene?”

“No. I—I thought—”

“What?”

She could not answer.

Dick folded the letter along its existing crease.

“Your brother told me you didn’t love me,” he continued. “I said I would hear it from you. You haven’t said it yet.”

Her heart began to beat painfully.

“I have told you that I accepted you for money.”

“That isn’t the same thing.”

“I was afraid of you.”

“That isn’t either.”

“You were so strong—and so sure.”

“I wasn’t sure.”

“You looked sure.”

“A man can look one way and feel another.”

She stared at him.

“Were you unhappy?”

He smiled without amusement.

“I’ve had more cheerful weeks.”

A sudden impulse moved her toward him, but she checked it.

“I am sorry.”

“Don’t be. I walked into this with my eyes open.”

“Why?”

“Because I wanted you.”

The words were simple, almost harsh in their simplicity. Colour swept into her face.

“You wanted me though you knew I did not love you?”

“I hoped you might learn.”

“And if I had not?”

“I’d have kept my side of the bargain.”

“Bargain!” She flinched. “Yes, that is what it was.”

Dick thrust the letter back into his pocket.

“Maybe. But even a bargain can yield something that wasn’t written into it.”

She looked at the fire.

“I have only my miserable little rag of honour,” she said. “It is not much, but I cannot live without it.”

“I’m not asking you to.”

“If I remained engaged to you now, you could never believe that I had forgotten your money.”

“I might.”

“I could not.”

Another silence fell. The clock ticked ten times—Violet counted each one—before Dick spoke.

“There’s something you should know about the railway.”

She stiffened.

“I do not want to hear it.”

“You’d better.”

“If the shares have risen again—”

“They haven’t. Not yet.”

“Then they may?”

“Yes.”

She made a despairing gesture.

“Why must money pursue us into every word?”

“Because it has to be cleared away before we can see what’s left.”

She faced him.

“What do you mean?”

“I mean the San Marco and Northern is sound. The line owns valuable rights. It needs a northern extension, new rolling stock and honest management. Given those, the shares will rise—not for an afternoon, but permanently.”

“How can you know?”

“I’ve seen the engineers’ reports.”

“Then the new syndicate—”

“Has not withdrawn.”

“But the newspapers said—”

“The newspapers said confirmation had been delayed.”

“By whom?”

He did not answer immediately.

Her eyes widened.

“By you?”

“Yes.”

The fire cracked sharply between them.

Violet’s hands fell to her sides.

“You are the capitalist?”

“I head the syndicate.”

“You made the shares rise?”

“My offer made them rise.”

“And you let them fall?”

“I delayed confirming it.”

She recoiled.

“You played with us!”

His face hardened.

“No.”

“With our poverty, our hopes—with me!”

“I put no rumour into the market. I made no promise and broke none. But when Jerry came to me yesterday, rich for an hour and eager to cancel your engagement, I decided to wait.”

“To test me?”

“To learn the truth.”

A whirl of wrath swept through her. It was mingled with relief, humiliation and a fierce, bewildering pain.

“You had no right!”

“Probably not.”

“You sat there knowing we might be ruined, and watched—”

“I knew you would not be ruined. If the line failed, I had already arranged to buy Jerry’s holding privately at the price he paid.”

She stopped.

“You had arranged that?”

“Before I asked you to marry me.”

“Why?”

“Because I knew he had followed bad advice and because I didn’t want you driven into anything by hunger.”

Violet sank into a chair.

The whole fabric of her judgment shifted. Dick, whom she had imagined looming over their poverty like a purchaser, had quietly placed a barrier between them and disaster before making his proposal. He had never meant to hold her through need. His patience, his refusal to press her, even his failure to bring costly gifts—all changed their meaning.

“You should have told me,” she said faintly.

“If I had, would you have believed my offer was not charity?”

“No.”

“That’s what I reckoned.”

“And now?”

“Now the shares will rise. The confirmation goes through to-night, whether you marry me or not. Your brother will have enough to settle his debts and make a fresh start.”

“And I shall be free.”

“Yes.”

He spoke the word evenly, but his hand closed hard upon the mantelpiece.

She watched that hand.

“What will you do?” she asked.

“Go back to America, perhaps.”

“And marry someone else?”

His gaze met hers.

“That’s none of your business now.”

The answer stung her absurdly.

“There are many women who would marry you.”

“So I’ve been told.”

“Beautiful women.”

“Possibly.”

“Women who would not be frightened of you.”

“That might be restful.”

“And you will choose one of them.”

He moved away from the fire.

“Why are we talking about this?”

“I don’t know.”

“I think you do.”

She rose quickly.

“No!”

“There’s that word again.”

“What word?”

“No. You use it when you mean something else.”

“I do not.”

“Look at me, Violet.”

The command was quiet. This time she obeyed.

For one breathless instant neither moved. The strength she had feared in him seemed to reach across the room, not as force but as certainty. Blood ran through her veins like fire.

Dick’s face had become very pale.

“Is there another man?” he asked.

“No.”

“Has there ever been?”

“No.”

“Then I’ll make it easy. You are free of me. You have money enough to live as you please. Somewhere there may be a man you can love without shame or fear. When he comes, marry him.”

He took a step toward the door.

The room seemed to tilt beneath her feet.

“Dick!”

He stopped.

She had never spoken his name in that tone. Hope leapt into his face and was instantly subdued.

“What is it?”

Violet pressed both hands to her breast.

“I do not want you to go.”

“Why?”

“Because—”

She faltered. All her life she had yielded to Jerry’s quick decisions, sheltered behind his charm, allowed fear to speak for her. Now no one could answer in her place. Dick waited, and his patience left her wholly free.

“Because if you go,” she said, “you will take the right man with you.”

He did not move.

“What right man?”

“The one you told me I might marry.”

“Violet—be sure.”

“I am sure now.”

“You were afraid of me yesterday.”

“I am afraid now.”

His hope darkened.

“Then—”

“Not in the same way.” She came toward him, trembling but resolute. “I am afraid because you matter. Because when you said I was free, I felt as though you had shut me outside in the cold. Because I have watched you for weeks, and every time you were kind I was more ashamed—and more glad. I called it fear because I would not call it anything else.”

He breathed deeply.

“Don’t say this out of gratitude.”

“It is not gratitude.”

“Or because the railway’s safe.”

“I had forgotten the railway.”

“Or because you’re sorry for me.”

A laugh broke through her tears.

“Sorry for you! You are the most exasperating, obstinate man alive.”

“That sounds more promising.”

She held out both hands.

“I love you, Dick.”

He stood for one second as if the words had struck him powerless. Then his hands closed over hers—not carefully now, but with a grasp that made her gasp. He drew her toward him, paused even then, asking permission with his eyes.

Violet answered by lifting her face.

His kiss was not patient.

## V

Jerry entered at precisely the wrong moment and, according to his own later account, received the greatest shock of his life.

He stopped inside the door. His hat dropped from his hand.

Violet sprang away, scarlet. Dick did not release her altogether; one arm remained about her, and his expression suggested that it would take stronger interference than Jerry’s to dislodge it.

“Good Lord!” Jerry said.

“Shut the door,” said Dick.

Jerry obeyed automatically.

“I thought—I understood—Violet, you wrote to him!”

“I did.”

“To end the engagement!”

“Yes.”

“Then why is he—why are you—?”

Violet hid her face against Dick’s shoulder.

The gesture enlightened Jerry more thoroughly than any explanation. His astonishment became delight.

“You love him!”

“So she claims,” Dick said.

Violet looked up indignantly.

“I have told you once. Must I continue all evening?”

“I wouldn’t object.”

Jerry began to laugh. He laughed until he had to sit down, and then, remembering the news which had brought him, jumped up again.

“The railway!”

Dick sighed.

“I had hoped we might keep money out of the room for five minutes.”

“You know, then?”

“I usually know my own business.”

“Your business?” Jerry stared. “Wait! The fellow at the office said the syndicate was Kenyon-controlled. I thought there might be another Kenyon. You—you are financing San Marco?”

“I am.”

Jerry’s jaw dropped.

Violet withdrew herself gently from Dick’s arm.

“He has told me,” she said.

“Then it is true! The confirmation has gone through. The shares opened in New York at fourteen and are expected to touch twenty before the week is out. Vi, we are rich!”

“Not rich,” Dick said. “Comfortable, if you don’t gamble again.”

“My dear fellow, I shall never speculate as long as I live.”

“You’ll speculate before breakfast if nobody watches you.”

Jerry ignored this.

“You saved the line! You restored our fortune! Why in heaven’s name did you let the market collapse yesterday?”

Violet and Dick exchanged a glance.

“That,” she said, “is between Dick and me.”

Jerry’s face grew solemn.

“I begin to understand. You wanted to find out whether she would keep you when she had money.”

“At first.”

“And then whether she would refuse you when she had none?”

“Something like that.”

Jerry thrust both hands into his pockets and paced the hearthrug.

“It was infernally clever.”

“It was infernally cruel,” Violet said, though her eyes smiled.

Dick looked at her.

“I’ve admitted I had no right.”

“You have not apologized.”

“I’m considering it.”

“Consider quickly.”

He bent and said something close to her ear which made her turn crimson again.

Jerry watched them with profound satisfaction.

“Well,” he announced, “everything has turned up trumps. I always said the San Marco was a magnificent property.”

“You said this morning that it was a fraudulent ditch laid through a wilderness,” Violet reminded him.

“A moment of depression. I have the courage of my permanent convictions.”

Dick sat down in the armchair by the fire and drew Violet to the arm of it. She came without resistance, still marvelling at the ease with which fear had vanished. His hand closed over hers; its strength no longer seemed ugly.

Jerry resumed his pacing.

“There is one painful matter,” he said. “Kenyon, I owe you an apology.”

“Several.”

“One comprehensive apology must cover them. I behaved badly.”

“You did.”

“I was anxious for Violet.”

“You were anxious for yourself too.”

Jerry winced.

“That is true. I thought of security, position, escape from debt. I treated her happiness as though it were an item in an account. And yesterday I treated your honour worse.”

Dick’s face remained grave, but not hostile.

“You offered to compensate me,” he observed.

Violet made a protesting movement.

“Must we speak of that again?”

“We must,” Jerry said. “I deserve it. Kenyon, if you had thrown me down the stairs, I could not have complained.”

“I considered it.”

“You called me a trader in my own sister.”

“I was angry.”

“You were right.”

Violet slipped from the arm of the chair and went to Jerry. He looked so handsome, repentant and boyish that her lingering anger dissolved. She took his arm.

“You were wrong,” she said, “but so was I. I let you persuade me because I was afraid to choose poverty.”

Jerry kissed her hair.

“You ought to have had a better brother.”

“I have never wanted another.”

Dick grunted.

“What does that mean?” Jerry demanded.

“It means she’s too forgiving. She’ll spoil you.”

“She has done so for twenty-eight years.”

“Then it’s time somebody stopped her.”

Violet returned to Dick.

“Are you proposing to manage us both?”

“Jerry can manage himself.”

“I can indeed,” said Jerry.

“No one believes you, sonny.”

Jerry picked up his hat and examined the crown.

“You realize, of course, that I shall not accept a place in your company merely because you are marrying Violet.”

“I realize nothing of the kind. You need work.”

“I will not take charity.”

“You won’t get it. I expect twelve hours a day and some common sense.”

“Twelve!”

“Perhaps we’d better make it fourteen.”

Jerry considered.

“What salary?”

Dick named a sum.

Jerry attempted to conceal his pleasure.

“That is not unreasonable. There should be prospects.”

“If you earn them.”

“And a certain independence of action.”

“No.”

“I could hardly consent to become a machine.”

“You won’t. Machines are reliable.”

Violet laughed. It was the first free laugh she had uttered in many weeks, and both men turned toward her. The poor little room, with its faded curtains and scorched hearthrug, seemed suddenly dear to her because it had held all their misery and now contained their happiness.

Jerry came to stand before them.

“There is one thing I do not follow,” he said. “If you had already arranged to purchase my shares and prevent our ruin, why did you ask Violet to marry you before telling her? You must have known she might accept for your money.”

Dick’s gaze rested upon the fire.

“I knew.”

“Then you walked into the trap deliberately.”

“I took a chance.”

“A tremendous chance.”

“I’ve taken bigger.”

“In mines, perhaps. Not in wives.”

Dick’s fingers tightened about Violet’s hand.

“I thought there might be something worth having in the deal.”

Violet looked at him.

“And was there?”

His heavy-lidded eyes met hers. They were no longer drowsy to her; she knew now how much light they could hold.

“Yes,” he said. “Something bigger than money.”

Jerry made an expressive grimace.

“Sentiment! From Kenyon! The world is ending.”

“It will end for you if you repeat that outside this room.”

“Threats on the eve of becoming my brother! Violet, control him.”

“I have no wish to.”

“Then marriage has already corrupted you.”

He moved toward the door, then turned back with unusual hesitation.

“Vi, are you truly happy?”

Her answer came without fear.

“Yes.”

“And you, Kenyon?”

Dick glanced up at her.

“I guess I can stand it.”

Jerry nodded, deeply satisfied.

“Then I shall go and order dinner. A proper dinner, with champagne. We can afford it.”

“You can afford one bottle,” Dick said.

“Two. The shares are at fourteen.”

“One.”

“Very well. One large bottle.”

He vanished before further economy could be imposed upon him.

Violet and Dick were left alone.

For a little while neither spoke. The city murmured beyond the rain-dimmed windows, and the fire burned steadily, consuming the last of Jerry’s sovereign in small red flames.

“Dick,” Violet said at length.

“Yes?”

“If the shares had never risen—if we had remained poor—what would you have done?”

“Married you, if you’d have me.”

“And if I had asked to be free?”

“Set you free.”

“Even though you loved me?”

“Especially then.”

She touched his rough hair with a tenderness that would once have astonished her.

“And if I had never learned to love you?”

His expression became sombre.

“I’d have tried not to let you know what it cost.”

Her eyes filled.

“Don’t,” he said quickly. “Don’t cry. We’re through with that.”

“No, I am not unhappy.”

“Then what is it?”

She smiled through her tears.

“I was thinking how blind I have been. I thought you bought me because you were rich and I was helpless.”

“I wanted you badly enough to make a poor bargain.”

“It was not a bargain.”

“No?”

“Not now.”

He drew her closer.

“No,” he agreed. “Not now.”

Below, a cab rattled along the wet street. Somewhere a newsboy cried the evening prices, and the words “San Marco” rose faintly through the dark. Yesterday that cry would have sent terror through Violet; a week ago it would have meant rescue. To-night it was no more than a voice in the distance.

“The shares are rising,” she said.

“I reckon so.”

“Jerry will be unbearable.”

“Jerry already is.”

“And you have made us safe.”

“I tried.”

She laid her cheek against his.

“But that is not why I love you.”

His arms closed about her with the old bulldog strength, no longer restrained by doubt.

“I know,” he said.

And because he knew, the restored fortune, the rescued railway and all the glittering promises of prosperity became for that moment what they had always deserved to be—mere figures on paper, insignificant beside the one treasure which neither poverty could compel nor money purchase.

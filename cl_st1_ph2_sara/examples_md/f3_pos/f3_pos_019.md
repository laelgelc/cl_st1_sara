The village of Bellwether had no church, no mayor, and no clockmaker, yet every house kept perfect time.

At dawn, shutters opened together. At noon, kettles began to sing in unison. At nine each evening, candles vanished from every window, leaving the streets to moonlight and the occasional lantern of a fisherman returning late from the marshes.

The reason stood on the hill above the village: a black iron clock tower, narrow as a raised finger. No one knew who had built it. Its foundations were older than the oldest graves, and its face had no numbers, only twelve silver birds arranged in a circle. A single hand traveled among them.

The clock never chimed.

Instead, at each hour, the bird beneath the hand opened its metal beak and sang one clear note. The sound floated over chimneys and gardens, across the river and into the surrounding woods. It was never loud, but everyone heard it.

Everyone except Mara Vale.

Mara had been born without hearing. She knew the clock sang because she had seen people pause when it did. Bakers stopped kneading. Children looked up from their games. Horses flicked their ears. Even falling leaves seemed to hesitate.

As a child, Mara would stand in the square and watch the faces around her change. The villagers never smiled at the clock’s song. Their expressions softened, as if they had remembered something precious, but also something lost.

Mara often asked what the notes sounded like.

Her father, Tomas, answered in signs because he had learned them for her.

Like rain on glass, he once said.

Her mother, Elin, shook her head.

Like light entering a dark room.

Old Mistress Fen from the bakery described it as the taste of honey after fever. The ferryman called it a silver thread pulled through the heart. No two answers were alike.

Mara eventually stopped asking.

She learned other ways to measure time. She watched shadows creep over the kitchen floor. She felt the baker’s ovens warm the alley before sunrise. She knew noon by the angle of swallows and evening by the ache in her father’s shoulders when he returned from the fields.

When Mara was nine, her mother died during a bitter winter.

Elin fell ill after walking through a blizzard to deliver medicine to a family beyond the river. For seven days, she burned with fever. On the eighth morning, just as the silver bird of dawn opened its beak, she stopped breathing.

The entire village heard the note.

Mara felt only her father’s hand tighten around hers.

Afterward, the villagers told her that the clock had sung differently that morning.

They meant it kindly, but Mara hated them for saying it.

Years passed. Mara grew tall, quick, and capable. She became a repairer of small things: locks, lanterns, hinges, fishing reels, spectacles, weather vanes, music boxes. The villagers brought broken objects to her narrow workshop beside the river, and she returned them whole.

Sound meant little to her, but motion told the truth. She could see where a gear hesitated, where a spring had weakened, where two pieces failed to meet. She understood machines because machines did not hide their intentions.

The clock tower, however, remained forbidden.

No one climbed the hill. No door could be seen at its base, and the stone walls were smooth, without crack or ledge. The tower tended itself, the villagers said. It had always done so.

Then, on the first morning of Mara’s twenty-third winter, time stopped.

She noticed it before anyone else.

A brass watch lay open on her workbench. Its gears had been cleaned, its spring replaced, and its hands aligned. Mara wound it carefully.

The balance wheel trembled, turned once, and froze.

She frowned and tried again.

Nothing.

Across the room, the pendulum on her wall clock hung motionless. A row of pocket watches had stopped at the same instant. So had the little wooden cuckoo clock Mistress Fen had given her years before.

Mara hurried outside.

The river had not stopped. Neither had the clouds. A dog scratched at a door, and two boys chased a hoop down the lane. Yet confusion spread through the village. People stood beneath the tower, looking upward.

Its single hand rested between two silver birds.

For the first time in living memory, the clock had failed to sing.

Panic arrived quietly.

The baker refused to put bread in the oven because she could not tell whether the dough had risen long enough. The schoolmaster dismissed his pupils, claiming lessons could not begin without the morning note. Fishermen remained ashore, afraid the tides would lose their order. By midday—or what might have been midday—half the village had gathered in the square.

“The council is meeting,” he signed. “They say we must wait.”

“For what?”

“For the clock to begin again.”

Mara looked around at the anxious faces. “And if it does not?”

Her father did not answer.

The village waited through a day measured badly by sun and stomach. At dusk, people lit candles but did not know when to extinguish them. Some stayed awake all night, fearing sleep without the clock’s permission. Others barred their doors, as though lost hours might wander the streets like wolves.

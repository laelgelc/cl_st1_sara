# The Keeper of Lost Hours

Every morning at six, Elias Vale unlocked the clock shop on Bellweather Street, turned the sign from CLOSED to OPEN, and listened to time begin.

First came the clocks in the front window: two brass carriage clocks, a porcelain mantel clock painted with violets, and a wooden cuckoo clock whose bird had forgotten how to sing. Then the clocks along the walls joined in, ticking at different speeds and pitches until the shop filled with a soft, uneven rain.

Elias liked that moment. It reminded him that time was not a single river, as poets claimed, but thousands of small streams, each carrying someone toward a different sea.

Above the workbench hung a framed photograph of his father, who had opened the shop fifty-two years earlier. Silas Vale stood in the picture with one hand resting on Elias’s shoulder. Elias was twelve, thin and solemn, already wearing the magnifying lens over one eye.

“A clock is a promise,” his father used to say. “It promises that one moment will follow another.”

Elias had believed him then.

He was less certain now.

At sixty-seven, he lived alone in the rooms above the shop. His wife, Mara, had been dead for eleven years. His daughter, Anna, lived in a city by the sea and telephoned every Sunday, though their conversations had become polite structures with nothing inside them.

How is business?

Quiet.

How are you feeling?

Fine.

You should visit.

Soon.

Neither of them mentioned the last time she had visited or the argument that had ended with her standing in the doorway, tears on her face, while Elias held his silence like a shield.

On a wet Tuesday in November, a girl entered the shop carrying a clock wrapped in a red scarf.

She was perhaps thirteen. Rain shone on her dark hair, and her yellow coat was too large for her. She placed the bundle on Elias’s counter with great care.

“I need this repaired,” she said.

Elias unwrapped the scarf.

Inside was a small silver pocket watch, tarnished almost black. There was no maker’s name on the face, only twelve narrow hands pointing toward twelve blank spaces where the numbers should have been.

He frowned.

“Unusual,” he said.

“Can you fix it?”

“Everything can be fixed if the necessary pieces still exist.”

“That sounds like a yes.”

“It sounds like an honest answer.”

The girl leaned across the counter. Her eyes were gray and steady.

“It belonged to my grandfather. It stopped yesterday.”

Elias lifted the watch to his ear.

Nothing.

He opened the back. Instead of the familiar arrangement of wheels and springs, he found a circle of blue metal surrounding a tiny glass chamber. Inside the chamber, something golden flickered.

Elias lowered the watch.

“Where did your grandfather get this?”

“He made it.”

“Was he a clockmaker?”

“Sometimes.”

The rain tapped the window. Behind Elias, the wall clocks ticked nervously.

“What is your name?” he asked.

“June.”

“And your grandfather?”

“Orin.”

Elias’s fingers tightened around the watch.

There had once been an Orin Bell, a clockmaker whose workshop stood near the railway station. Silas Vale had called him a genius, a fraud, or a madman, depending on the day. Orin claimed ordinary clocks did not measure time at all. They measured only motion: the swing of a pendulum, the release of a spring, the vibration of a crystal.

True time, he said, was a substance. It gathered in rooms, clung to objects, and settled into human beings like dust.

His workshop had burned down forty years ago. No body had been found.

“Orin Bell?” Elias asked.

June nodded.

“That’s impossible.”

“He said you would say that.”

Elias looked again at the golden flicker inside the watch.

“What exactly is wrong with it?”

“It lost an hour.”

“Clocks gain time or lose time.”

“Not that way.” June pointed to the glass chamber. “There should be twelve lights. Now there are eleven.”

Elias brought the magnifying lens to his eye. At first he saw only the golden flame. Then it shifted, separating into tiny sparks. He counted them twice.

Eleven.

“What happens when the last light goes out?” he asked.

“My grandfather dies.”

The shop seemed to fall silent, although no clock had stopped.

Elias carefully set down the watch.

“You need a doctor.”

“He has one. Doctors can keep his heart beating, but that isn’t the same thing.”

“I don’t understand.”

June took a folded piece of paper from her coat.

“He told me to give you this.”

Elias recognized the handwriting before he opened it.

Silas Vale’s hand had been square and dark, each letter precisely shaped.

Elias,

If you are reading this, Orin’s watch has begun to fail. Help the girl. You owe him one hour, and he owes you the truth.

Your father

Beneath the signature was a date: December 3, forty years earlier.

The day the Bell workshop had burned.

Elias read the note again.

“My father wrote this?”

“Yes.”

“Where did you get it?”

“Grandfather kept it inside the watch.”

“It wasn’t there.”

“Not that part.”

Elias nearly told her to leave. He nearly wrapped the watch in its scarf, placed it in her hands, and returned to the clean, rational universe of gears and springs.

Then the golden lights shivered. One vanished.

Ten remained.

June’s face went pale.

“Please,” she whispered.

Elias turned the sign to CLOSED.

They worked through the morning.

Or rather, Elias worked while June watched, asked questions, and refused to be sent home. The watch had no screws or visible fastenings. Its inner ring was engraved with symbols Elias did not recognize, though some resembled the marks his father used in private notebooks.

After two hours, Elias found a hidden catch beneath the winding crown. When he pressed it, the blue ring opened like the petals of a flower.

A sound emerged from the watch.

Laughter.

It was distant at first, then bright and close: a man laughing in delight, a woman trying not to laugh with him, and a child demanding to know what was funny.

The workshop vanished.

Elias stood in summer sunlight.

He was eight years old, sitting beneath the pear tree behind his childhood home. His mother spread a blanket over the grass. His father held a broken umbrella above his head and pretended it was an elegant hat.

Young Elias laughed until his stomach hurt.

The memory lasted only a few seconds. Then the shop returned, gray and cold.

June gripped the edge of the counter.

“Did you see that?”

Elias could not answer.

A spark drifted out of the watch and dissolved in the air.

Nine lights remained.

“What did we do?” Elias asked.

“You opened an hour.”

“That wasn’t an hour.”

“It was part of one. The watch stores lost hours.”

“There is no such thing.”

“You just stood inside one.”

Elias removed his lens and rubbed his eye.

June explained what Orin had told her. Everyone lost hours. Some were wasted in railway stations, waiting rooms, sleepless beds, and conversations where no one said what they meant. Others were lost because they were too painful to remember. A few were stolen by fear.

Orin’s watch collected such hours.

“Why?” Elias asked.

“So they could be returned.”

“To whom?”

“To whoever needs them.”

“And those lights?”

“His own hours. He put them in the watch to keep it alive. When the mechanism broke, it started using them too quickly.”

Elias stared at the open blue ring.

“What would repair it?”

“An hour freely given.”

“I don’t know how to give a clock an hour.”

“Grandfather said you would.”

“Your grandfather appears to have had an exaggerated opinion of me.”

“No,” June said. “He said you were stubborn, frightened, and good with small tools.”

Despite himself, Elias smiled.

They searched Silas’s notebooks. Most contained ordinary repair records, but one locked drawer held a thin book bound in green leather. Inside were sketches of the silver watch.

On the final page, Silas had written:

The balance cannot be restored by measured time. It requires an unlived hour, willingly surrendered.

Below it was a diagram of a needle, a circle, and a human hand.

Elias read the words aloud.

“What is an unlived hour?” June asked.

“An hour from the future, I suppose.”

“How many do you have?”

“I don’t know.”

“More than Grandfather.”

Elias ignored this.

According to the diagram, the donor had to wind the watch backward while holding the innermost spring. It looked simple, but simple mechanisms were often the cruelest. A mistake would release every hour stored inside.

They prepared the tools.

Before Elias could begin, the front door opened.

A woman stood on the threshold, holding a suitcase.

She had Mara’s eyes.

Anna looked older than when Elias had last seen her, though only three years had passed. There was silver in her black hair. Rain darkened the shoulders of her coat.

“I saw the sign,” she said. “I thought you were here.”

Elias lowered the tweezers.

“Why aren’t you at home?”

“This is home too. Or it used to be.”

June looked from one to the other.

Anna noticed the watch. “Are you busy?”

“Yes,” Elias said.

“No,” June said at the same time.

Anna gave the girl a tired smile.

“I’m June,” June offered.

“Anna.”

“You look like him.”

“I’m sorry to hear that.”

Elias felt the old wall rise inside him. “Why have you come?”

Anna set down her suitcase.

“I called yesterday. You didn’t answer.”

“I was here.”

“I called here.”

“The bell may be broken.”

“You repair clocks, but you can’t repair a telephone bell?”

“It hasn’t been important.”

“No,” she said softly. “I suppose not.”

June stared hard at Elias, as if this failure were somehow worse than losing a screw in a priceless watch.

Anna took an envelope from her pocket.

“I’ve been offered a position in Melbourne. I’m leaving next month.”

“Melbourne?”

“It’s in Australia.”

“I know where Melbourne is.”

“You might have said you were pleased.”

“I might be, if I knew anything about it.”

“I tried to tell you. On Sunday. And the Sunday before that.”

Elias remembered her talking about a museum. He had been regulating a French clock at the time.

“When will you return?” he asked.

“I don’t know.”

The answer frightened him, so he became angry.

“You came all this way to tell me you’re going farther?”

“I came to ask you to come with me.”

The clocks ticked around them.

Anna stepped closer. “Not forever. Just for a while. Sell the shop or close it. You’re alone here.”

“I have work.”

“You have clocks.”

“They require me.”

“I required you.”

The words landed without force, which made them unbearable.

Anna looked toward the photograph of Silas.

“When Mother was dying,” she said, “you spent every night beside her. You spoke to doctors. You made soup. You read to her, even when she couldn’t hear. But after she died, you disappeared into this room.”

“That is not fair.”

“I lost her too.”

“I know.”

“Do you?”

Elias looked down at the watch.

Eight lights remained.

A lifetime, collapsing spark by spark.

“I asked you to come to the sea,” Anna continued. “I asked because I didn’t know how to be in the world without her. You said the shop could not be closed.”

“It was complicated.”

“It was a door, Father. You only had to lock it.”

Elias had no answer.

Anna picked up her suitcase.

“I leave on the fifteenth,” she said. “I wanted to say goodbye in person.”

She walked out.

June ran after her, but Elias called the girl back.

“We have no time,” he said.

“That was the problem,” June replied. “You did.”

Seven lights remained.

Elias positioned the watch beneath the lamp. His hands trembled. He told himself it was age, though he knew it was not.

He fitted the finest tweezers around the innermost spring.

“What will happen when I give it an hour?” he asked.

“I don’t know.”

“Will I die an hour sooner?”

“Everyone dies an hour sooner every hour.”

“Your grandfather taught you that?”

“No. That one is obvious.”

Elias turned the winding crown backward.

The watch grew warm. A low vibration passed through the workbench. The remaining lights spun inside their chamber, faster and faster, until they became a golden ring.

Then something resisted.

He saw the hour waiting before him.

It was not numbered or dated, yet he knew it belonged to his future. He stood on a shore beneath a wide blue sky. Anna was beside him, older, her silver hair lifted by the wind. Between them walked a little boy holding both their hands.

The boy asked Elias whether clocks worked upside down on the other side of the world.

Anna laughed.

Elias felt the spring pulling that hour toward the watch.

To save Orin, he had to surrender it.

His fingers froze.

He had believed an unlived hour would be blank: sixty minutes taken from an anonymous afternoon. But no future was empty. Every hour contained a possibility, even if it was only the possibility of sunlight, forgiveness, or a child’s hand.

June’s voice came from far away.

“Something’s wrong.”

The watch was hot now. Six lights remained.

Elias thought of Orin Bell, a man he had not seen in forty years. He thought of Anna walking through the rain, carrying everything she had failed to say.

Then he understood.

He released the spring.

The golden ring slowed.

Five lights remained.

“You stopped,” June said.

“Yes.”

“My grandfather—”

“Your grandfather told you I owed him an hour. He didn’t say it was this one.”

Elias shut the watch and wrapped it in the red scarf.

“Come with me.”

They found Anna at the railway station, seated beneath the great clock in the departure hall. Hundreds of people moved around her. Announcements echoed overhead. The clock showed 2:17, though every smaller clock in the station showed 2:23.

Elias noticed these things automatically.

Anna saw him and stood.

“If you came to argue—”

“I came to ask for an hour.”

“What?”

“One hour. Freely given.”

Her face hardened. “You’ve had years.”

“I know. I’m asking for one more.”

The station clock clicked.

Its minute hand moved backward.

The crowd stopped.

A pigeon hung in the air above the platform. Rain stood motionless beyond the glass roof. A porter remained bent beneath the weight of a suitcase, his mouth open in mid-shout.

Only Elias, Anna, and June could move.

The pocket watch pulsed beneath the scarf.

June unwrapped it. Four lights remained.

“What is happening?” Anna whispered.

Elias took her hand.

“I need to tell you the truth.”

“Now?”

“Especially now.”

They sat beneath the frozen clock.

Elias told her that after Mara died, every room above the shop had seemed filled with her absence. The cup she had used. The indentation on her pillow. The blue dress behind the bedroom door. He could not bear the emptiness, so he went downstairs, where the clocks filled silence with noise.

He told Anna that he had refused to visit the sea because Mara had wanted to go there during the final summer of her life. Elias had promised they would go when she was stronger.

She never became stronger.

“I thought,” he said, “that if I went without her, I would be admitting that I had failed to keep my promise.”

Anna’s anger shifted, though it did not disappear.

“You punished both of us for surviving.”

“Yes.”

“And when I asked you to come?”

“I was afraid.”

“Of me?”

“Of needing you. Of losing you too.”

Three lights remained.

Elias placed the watch between them.

“I cannot repair the years,” he said. “I know that. But if there is still a place beside you, I would like to try to stand in it.”

Anna looked at him for a long time.

Then she covered his hand with hers.

“I don’t forgive you yet,” she said.

“I understand.”

“But you can come to Melbourne.”

The watch opened by itself.

A thread of pale light rose from Elias’s hand. Another rose from Anna’s. They twisted together above the silver case.

The station disappeared.

For one hour, father and daughter sat on a beach that had not yet happened.

They watched the little boy run toward the waves. Anna told Elias the child’s name was Silas, after his great-grandfather. Elias showed Silas how to draw a clock in the sand.

The boy gave it thirteen numbers.

“Why thirteen?” Elias asked.

“In case we need more time,” Silas said.

Anna laughed.

The future dissolved.

Elias found himself beneath the station clock, tears on his face. Anna was crying too.

The pocket watch lay open between them.

Twelve golden lights burned inside.

Time resumed with a roar. The pigeon completed its flight. The porter finished his shout. Rain struck the roof. People hurried toward their trains, unaware that an impossible hour had unfolded among them.

June snatched up the watch.

“You fixed it!”

“No,” Elias said. “We did.”

“But you didn’t give up the hour.”

“An hour freely given does not have to be surrendered. It can be shared.”

The station clock moved forward six minutes and rang three times for no reason.

June grinned.

“My grandfather will want to see you.”

“Is he far?”

“About forty years.”

Elias stared at her.

June pointed to the watch.

One of its hands had begun to turn backward.

The air rippled, and the station smelled suddenly of smoke.

For an instant Elias saw a burning workshop. Two young men stood inside: Orin Bell, laughing as flames climbed the walls, and Silas Vale, shouting at him to hurry.

Orin held the silver watch.

“We need one hour!” Silas cried.

“Not yet,” Orin answered. “Elias hasn’t learned how to give it.”

“He’s twelve!”

“He won’t always be.”

The vision broke.

June closed the watch.

“Grandfather has been waiting,” she said. “He got caught between hours when the workshop burned. The watch was supposed to bring him back, but it needed something he couldn’t make.”

“What?”

“A shared future.”

Anna picked up her suitcase.

“Then we should not keep him waiting.”

They returned to Bellweather Street as evening fell.

Inside the shop, every clock had stopped.

June placed the silver watch on Elias’s workbench. Its twelve hands spun in opposite directions. Golden light poured across the walls, illuminating dust, gears, tools, and the old photograph of Silas.

The cuckoo emerged from its clock.

For the first time in thirty years, it sang.

A door appeared where no door had been before.

June opened it.

Beyond lay a ruined workshop under a summer sky. Orin Bell sat on an overturned crate, exactly as Elias remembered him: red-haired, bright-eyed, and far too young to be anyone’s grandfather.

“You took your time,” Orin said.

Elias laughed.

It surprised him how much the sound resembled his father’s laughter beneath the pear tree.

June ran through the doorway and threw her arms around Orin. As she did, his face changed. Years passed across it like clouds. His hair whitened, his shoulders bent, and when he rose, he was an old man.

He looked at Elias.

“Silas was right about you.”

“What did he say?”

“That you would understand eventually.”

“Understand what?”

Orin glanced around the clock shop.

“That clocks don’t keep time. People do.”

The strange doorway faded with the last of the golden light.

That night, Elias telephoned a locksmith and arranged to have the shop door fitted with a stronger lock.

A month later, he turned the sign to CLOSED and boarded a plane with Anna.

He did not sell the shop. Some promises, he decided, could be set aside without being broken. June agreed to open it twice a week, under Orin’s supervision, though Orin spent more time telling stories than repairing clocks.

Years later, a boy named Silas visited Bellweather Street. He found an old man behind the counter and a silver watch displayed beneath glass.

The watch had thirteen hands.

“What does it measure?” Silas asked.

Orin smiled.

“Nothing,” he said. “It reminds us.”

“Of what?”

“That no hour is truly lost while someone is willing to share it.”

Above them, hundreds of clocks ticked in bright disorder, carrying their separate promises into the afternoon.

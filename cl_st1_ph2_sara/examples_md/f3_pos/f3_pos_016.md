The Clockmaker’s Last Tuesday

On the first Tuesday of every month, at precisely nine minutes past six in the morning, the clocks in Bellweather stopped.

They did not slow. They did not lose a second here or a minute there. They simply stopped, all at once, their hands fixed at 6:09.

The clock above the baker’s door ceased its cheerful ticking. The brass alarm clock beside Mayor Thorne’s bed fell silent before it could ring. Pocket watches froze in waistcoats. Pendulums hung motionless in their cases. Even the great clock in the tower above the town hall stopped, though its mechanism had been built in another country two hundred years before and had never failed, not even when lightning split its roof.

For exactly seven minutes, Bellweather existed without time.

Then, at 6:16, every clock began again.

Most people had grown accustomed to it. Bakers accounted for the missing seven minutes when they put loaves in their ovens. Teachers delayed their Tuesday lessons. The railway clerk adjusted the morning timetable so that the first train arrived at 6:23 instead of 6:16. Travelers complained, of course, but Bellweather had learned not to mind the complaints of travelers.

Only Thomas Vale, the old clockmaker of Copper Street, seemed troubled by the stoppage.

He had been repairing clocks for fifty-eight years, and he knew every clock in town as a shepherd knows his flock. He knew which ones ran fast in winter, which chimed thirteen times when rain was coming, and which had to be encouraged with a tap against the case. He could identify a watch by its tick from across a quiet room.

Yet he could not explain the seven silent minutes.

“It is not mechanical,” he told anyone who asked.

“What is it, then?” they would say.

Thomas always looked toward the hill beyond the town, where the abandoned observatory stood among black pines.

“I don’t know,” he replied.

But he did know.

Or at least, he feared he did.

On a cold Monday evening in November, a girl named Eliza Wren entered Thomas’s shop carrying a wooden box.

She was thirteen years old, thin as a matchstick, with a dark braid and a serious expression. She lived with her aunt above the apothecary and was known throughout Bellweather for asking questions that adults disliked.

A brass bell announced her arrival.

Thomas looked up from the pocket watch he was repairing.

“We’re closed,” he said.

“The sign says you close at seven.”

“The sign is slow.”

Eliza glanced at the clock on the wall. “It says twenty minutes to seven.”

“That clock is slow too.”

“All your clocks are slow?”

Thomas peered at her over the rims of his spectacles. “What do you want?”

She placed the box on his counter.

“My father left this for me.”

Thomas’s hands became very still.

The box was made of walnut, polished smooth at the corners. A crescent moon had been carved into its lid. Thomas had seen it once before, nearly fourteen years ago, when its owner had brought it to the shop and asked for his help.

“Where did you find it?” Thomas asked.

“In my aunt’s cupboard. She said it belonged to my father. She said I could have it when I was older, but she never decided how old older was, so I decided for her.”

“You stole it.”

“I inherited it early.”

“Eliza—”

“You knew him, didn’t you?”

Thomas removed his spectacles and cleaned them, although the lenses were spotless.

“I knew many people.”

“My father was Daniel Wren.”

“I know who your father was.”

“Then you know what this is.”

“No.”

“You’re lying.”

Thomas put his spectacles back on. “Adults don’t lie. We postpone the truth until it becomes useless.”

“That sounds worse.”

“It often is.”

Eliza opened the box.

Inside lay a silver watch unlike any other in Bellweather. It had no numbers on its face. Instead, tiny stars had been etched around the edge, and three slender hands rested at the center. Two were black. The third was blue.

The blue hand was moving backward.

Thomas stared at it.

“How long has it been doing that?” he whispered.

“Since I wound it.”

“You wound it?”

“There was a key in the box.”

“Where is the key?”

Eliza took a small silver key from her pocket.

Thomas snatched it from her.

“That was rude,” she said.

“You must never wind this watch again.”

“Why?”

“Because it is broken.”

“No, it isn’t. I can hear it ticking.”

“It is broken in a way that ticking does not cure.”

He closed the box and pushed it toward her.

“Take it home. Lock it away. Forget you found it.”

Eliza did not touch the box. “Tomorrow is the first Tuesday.”

Thomas looked at the wall clock.

It no longer said twenty minutes to seven.

It said 6:09.

For one dreadful instant, every clock in the shop fell silent.

Then the hands jumped forward, and the room filled again with ticking.

But tomorrow had not yet come.

Thomas went to the door and turned the sign to CLOSED.

“Eliza,” he said, “you are going to tell your aunt that you’re staying here tonight.”

“I don’t need permission?”

“Not from me.”

“That isn’t how permission works.”

“Then tell her I’ve kidnapped you.”

“She’ll charge you for supper.”

Thomas sighed. “Tell her the truth.”

“What truth?”

He looked at the walnut box.

“That your father may be coming home.”

Fourteen years earlier, Daniel Wren had worked as caretaker of the observatory on Bellweather Hill.

The observatory had been abandoned by astronomers long before Daniel arrived. Its copper dome had turned green, ivy covered its lower walls, and its great telescope pointed uselessly through a crack in the ceiling. The town council hired Daniel to keep trespassers away and prevent the building from collapsing.

Instead, he repaired it.

Daniel was not an astronomer. He was a locksmith by trade, but he possessed the dangerous kind of curiosity that persuades people to open doors merely because they are locked. Within two years, the dome turned again. Within three, the telescope could be aimed at any star in the sky.

Then Daniel discovered the room beneath the observatory.

Thomas had been with him.

“It wasn’t on any plan,” Thomas explained as he and Eliza climbed the hill that night. “There was a staircase behind a wall. At the bottom, we found a metal door.”

The moon was hidden behind clouds, and Thomas carried an oil lantern. Eliza walked beside him, holding the walnut box under her coat.

“What was behind the door?” she asked.

“A machine.”

“What kind?”

“The kind that should never have been built.”

“That isn’t an answer.”

“It is the only honest one I have.”

The path grew steeper. Black pines whispered in the wind.

Thomas stopped to catch his breath.

“The machine was a clock,” he continued. “At least, Daniel called it a clock. It filled the entire chamber. There were gears taller than a man and wheels made of glass. But it did not measure hours or minutes. It measured possibilities.”

Eliza frowned. “How can you measure a possibility?”

“The same way you measure anything else. First you decide that it can be measured. Then you invent numbers to prove yourself right.”

“That sounds like something my mathematics teacher would say.”

“Your mathematics teacher is a criminal.”

“She says you owe her for a carriage clock.”

“Exactly.”

They reached the observatory near midnight.

The building stood against the cloudy sky like a sleeping giant. Several windows were broken, and the old dome leaned slightly east. The front doors were chained.

Thomas produced a ring of keys.

“Do you have a key for everything?” Eliza asked.

“Everything except my own front door. I lost that one in 1983.”

“That was before I was born.”

“It was before most sensible things happened.”

The third key opened the chain.

Inside, dust covered the floor. Moonlight spilled through the cracked dome, illuminating the enormous telescope. It rested on a circular platform surrounded by rotten chairs and collapsed cabinets.

Eliza approached the telescope.

“My father repaired this?”

“Yes.”

“Did he bring me here?”

“Once, when you were a baby.”

“I don’t remember.”

“That is often the trouble with being a baby.”

Thomas moved to the far wall and pressed his fingers against the stones. After a moment, something clicked. Part of the wall shifted inward, revealing a narrow staircase.

Cold air rose from below.

Eliza took the lantern while Thomas led the way down.

The stairs ended at a circular iron door. Twelve locks were arranged around its edge like numbers on a clock face.

Thomas began unlocking them one by one.

“You helped him make the watch,” Eliza said.

It was not a question.

Thomas paused at the seventh lock. “Yes.”

“What does it do?”

“It carries seven minutes.”

“Seven minutes of what?”

“Time.”

She almost laughed, but his expression stopped her.

Thomas opened the eighth lock.

“The machine below us could show Daniel different versions of the future,” he said. “Not clearly. Only glimpses. A house that might burn. A child who might drown. A harvest that might fail. At first, he used the knowledge carefully. He warned a farmer about a broken bridge. He prevented a fever from spreading. Good things.”

“But?”

“There is always a but. It is the hinge on which disaster turns.”

The ninth lock opened.

“Daniel began searching for a particular future. Your mother was ill. No doctor could help her. He believed there must be some version of the world in which she survived.”

Eliza’s face hardened. Her mother had died the night Eliza was born. In Bellweather, this was common knowledge, though no one spoke of it to her directly.

“Did he find one?” she asked.

“Yes.”

Thomas opened the tenth lock.

“He saw a future in which the doctor reached your house seven minutes earlier. In that future, your mother lived.”

“Then why didn’t he warn the doctor?”

“He tried. Every path failed. The doctor’s horse threw a shoe. A wagon blocked the road. A tree fell in a storm. Events resisted being changed.”

The eleventh lock turned.

“So we built the watch,” Thomas said. “Daniel believed he could take seven minutes from the future and carry them into the past.”

Eliza held the box tighter.

“And did he?”

Thomas put the final key into the final lock.

“Tomorrow morning,” he said, “you may see for yourself.”

The door opened.

Beyond it lay darkness and the slow groan of machinery.

The chamber beneath the observatory was far larger than the building above. Thomas’s lantern revealed gears, cables, glass wheels, and brass shafts disappearing into shadow. At the center stood a circular platform surrounded by seven stone pillars.

The machine was moving.

Its gears turned without any visible source of power. A low vibration passed through the floor.

Atop the platform stood a woman.

Eliza gasped.

The woman was pale and almost transparent. She wore a long white nightdress, and her dark hair hung loose around her shoulders. In her arms she held a newborn child.

She looked directly at Eliza.

Then she vanished.

The machine stopped.

“Was that—”

“Yes,” Thomas said.

“My mother?”

“A possibility of her.”

Eliza climbed onto the platform. “Bring her back.”

“I cannot.”

“You built the watch. You understand the machine.”

“I helped build the watch, and I never understood the machine.”

“Then try.”

“I did. For years.”

Eliza stared at the place where the woman had stood.

“What happened to my father?”

Thomas sat on the lowest step of the platform. In the lantern light, he suddenly looked much older.

“He wound the watch at 6:09 on the morning your mother died. Every clock in Bellweather stopped. Daniel entered the machine and carried seven minutes backward.”

“Did he save her?”

“No.”

“How do you know?”

“Because she still died.”

“Maybe he did something wrong.”

“He tried again the next month.”

Eliza turned toward him.

“The first Tuesday.”

“Yes. The machine needs twenty-eight days to gather enough time. Every first Tuesday, Daniel returns to that morning. He tries a different road, gives a different warning, changes some small event. And every time, at 6:16, the seven minutes collapse. The world returns to the path it knows.”

“For fourteen years?”

“For fourteen years.”

“That’s one hundred and sixty-eight times.”

“One hundred and sixty-nine tomorrow.”

Eliza looked down at the walnut box.

The blue hand continued moving backward.

“Why do I have the watch if he uses it?”

“Because the watch exists outside the seven minutes. When he enters the past, it goes with him. When the attempt fails, it returns to the box. But each journey leaves a mark. The blue hand measures how much time remains before the spring breaks.”

“And if it breaks?”

“I don’t know.”

Another image appeared above the platform.

Daniel Wren stood in a snow-covered street, younger than Eliza had imagined him. He was shouting at someone beyond the edge of the vision. Blood stained one sleeve.

The image flickered.

Now he stood beside a carriage.

Now beside a fallen tree.

Now in a bedroom, reaching toward a woman in white.

The images came faster.

Daniel running.

Daniel pleading.

Daniel breaking a lock.

Daniel lifting a baby from a cradle.

Daniel staring at fire.

Daniel alone.

Then all the images vanished.

Eliza’s eyes filled with tears, but her voice remained steady.

“How do we stop him?”

“At 6:09, the watch will open the path. Someone must enter the machine and persuade him to let the seven minutes end.”

“Why haven’t you done it?”

Thomas looked away.

“I tried once.”

“What happened?”

“He did not listen.”

“Try again.”

“I cannot. The machine will not allow the same traveler twice.”

“Then I’ll go.”

“That is why I brought you.”

Silence settled between them.

Eliza stepped down from the platform.

“You knew I would volunteer.”

“I hoped you would.”

“That’s a cowardly way to say you planned it.”

“Yes.”

“And if I get trapped?”

“I don’t know.”

“If I disappear?”

“I don’t know.”

“If the watch breaks while I’m inside?”

“I don’t know.”

“You don’t know very much.”

“No,” Thomas said. “But I know your father loved you.”

“He didn’t know me.”

“He held you for seven minutes, one hundred and sixty-eight times.”

The hours passed slowly.

Thomas prepared the machine while Eliza sat beside the platform. He oiled gears, adjusted brass arms, and muttered at dials whose markings had long since faded.

At six o’clock, the chamber began to tremble.

At six-five, the glass wheels glowed blue.

At six-eight, Thomas placed the silver watch in Eliza’s hands.

“What will I see?” she asked.

“The morning you were born.”

“Can I change anything?”

“No.”

“You sound certain.”

“I have paid dearly for that certainty.”

The black hands approached 6:09.

Thomas helped her onto the platform.

“When you find Daniel, tell him the future is not a debt he must spend his life repaying.”

“Will that work?”

“No. But it is true.”

The watch clicked.

Every gear in the chamber stopped.

The blue hand pointed upward.

And Eliza fell through time.

She landed in snow.

For a moment, she could not breathe. Bells rang somewhere in the darkness. She stood on Copper Street, but the town looked younger. The trees were smaller, the shops freshly painted, and the windows glowed with lamplight.

Nothing moved.

Snow hung motionless in the air.

A dog was frozen in mid-stride. Smoke paused above chimneys. Behind a window, a woman poured tea, the stream suspended between pot and cup.

Then a man ran around the corner.

Daniel Wren looked exactly as he had in the machine’s visions. His coat was torn. His face was exhausted.

He stopped when he saw Eliza.

“No,” he said.

She held up the watch.

“Father.”

Daniel stumbled back.

“No. You shouldn’t be here.”

“Thomas sent me.”

“He promised he wouldn’t.”

“He said the machine wouldn’t let him return.”

“He promised he would destroy it.”

“He tried.”

Daniel stared at her as if looking at a ghost.

“How old are you?”

“Thirteen.”

His face crumpled.

“Thirteen,” he repeated. “I missed—”

“Yes.”

“I can fix it.”

“No.”

“I’m close this time. The doctor is only two streets away. I moved the wagon before the axle broke. I opened the north road. I only need—”

“Mother still dies.”

“You don’t know that.”

“I’m here.”

“You might be from another path.”

“I have your watch.”

Daniel’s eyes dropped to the silver case.

Behind them, one of the frozen bells began to crack.

A thin line appeared along its side.

“We don’t have long,” Eliza said.

“We have seven minutes.”

“You’ve had almost twenty hours.”

He stared at her.

“Seven minutes, one hundred and sixty-eight times,” she said. “That’s nineteen hours and thirty-six minutes. Nearly a day. You’ve spent nearly a day trying to save her.”

“I’ll spend a thousand years.”

“But they won’t be years with her. They won’t be years with me.”

Daniel turned away.

At the end of the street, a horse and carriage stood frozen in the snow. Inside it sat a young doctor, his hand reaching toward the door.

“Once he reaches the house—” Daniel began.

“He doesn’t.”

“I can make him.”

“You can’t.”

Daniel seized Eliza by the shoulders.

“You think I should let her die?”

His grief came out like anger, sharp and desperate.

Eliza wanted to pull away, but she did not.

“I think you already did.”

He released her.

The crack in the bell widened. All around them, the silent town began to shudder.

“She was afraid,” Daniel whispered. “That morning, she told me she was afraid. I said I would protect her. I said nothing would take her from us.”

“You made a promise you couldn’t keep.”

“I have to keep it.”

“No. You have to forgive yourself for making it.”

Daniel looked toward the hill, though the observatory was hidden by snow.

“I thought Thomas would come back.”

“He couldn’t.”

“I waited for him each time.”

“He waited for you too.”

A clock face in a nearby shop window fractured. Its hands still pointed to 6:09.

Eliza opened the watch.

The blue hand trembled.

“What happens now?” Daniel asked.

“I don’t know.”

“You sound like Thomas.”

“I’m beginning to understand why.”

Somewhere close, a baby cried.

The sound moved through the frozen town, alive where nothing else was.

Daniel closed his eyes.

“That’s you,” he said.

“Take me to her.”

They ran.

The house stood at the edge of Bellweather, a small place with green shutters. The front door was open. Inside, candles burned with motionless flames.

They climbed the stairs.

In the bedroom, Eliza saw her mother.

She lay against white pillows, pale and tired but alive. A midwife stood frozen beside the bed. In her arms was a newborn wrapped in a gray blanket.

Daniel approached the bed.

For the first time, time moved around one person other than them.

Eliza’s mother opened her eyes.

“Daniel?” she whispered.

He fell to his knees beside her.

“I’m here.”

“You look so sad.”

“I’m sorry.”

“For what?”

“For not saving you.”

She smiled faintly. “You were never meant to save me.”

The room shook.

Daniel took her hand. “I can’t lose you.”

“You already have.”

“No.”

“And you will again. That is what time does. It gives us people, and then it takes them. The giving is not made worthless by the taking.”

Daniel wept.

Eliza’s mother turned her gaze toward the girl standing at the foot of the bed.

“Is that her?”

“Yes,” Daniel said.

Eliza stepped closer.

Her mother studied her face with wonder.

“You have his eyes,” she murmured.

“Everyone says I have yours.”

“Then everyone is kind.”

The walls began to dissolve into blue light.

The watch grew hot in Eliza’s hand.

“What do I do?” she cried.

Daniel stood and looked at the newborn child, then at the daughter she had become.

“Give me the watch.”

Eliza hesitated.

“Please.”

She placed it in his palm.

Daniel kissed her forehead.

“I cannot come with you,” he said.

“Why not?”

“Someone must close the path from this side.”

“No. There must be another way.”

“There is always another possibility,” he said. “But not always another way.”

He turned the silver key.

The blue hand stopped.

Eliza grabbed his coat. “I just found you.”

“I know.”

“That isn’t fair.”

“No,” Daniel said. “It isn’t.”

Her mother reached out from the bed and touched Eliza’s hand.

For one impossible second, they were together: mother, father, and daughter, held within seven stolen minutes.

Then the watch chimed.

Once.

Daniel pushed Eliza toward the light.

“Live forward,” he said.

The world vanished.

Eliza awoke on the platform beneath the observatory.

Thomas caught her before she fell.

The machine screamed around them. Glass wheels shattered. Gears spun loose from their shafts and crashed against the stone floor. The seven pillars split from top to bottom.

Thomas carried Eliza toward the iron door.

Behind them, the great machine folded inward, piece by piece, collapsing around an empty center.

They reached the stairs as a blast of cold air extinguished the lantern.

Then everything became quiet.

Morning light entered through the cracked dome when they returned to the observatory above.

Thomas sat on the dusty floor, breathing heavily.

Eliza stood at a broken window and looked down at Bellweather.

The town hall clock began to strike.

One, two, three, four, five, six.

Then it continued.

Seven.

Eight.

Nine.

Ten.

Eleven.

Twelve.

Thirteen.

Thomas frowned. “It should not do that.”

Eliza began to laugh.

After a moment, Thomas laughed too.

Far below, doors opened and townspeople stepped into the streets, staring at clocks that had not stopped.

The first Tuesday continued like any other day.

Years later, Eliza took over the shop on Copper Street.

She became a better clockmaker than Thomas, though he denied it until the afternoon he died at the age of ninety-three. She repaired watches, tower clocks, music boxes, and once a mechanical bird that insisted on singing backward.

Above her workbench she kept a walnut box carved with a crescent moon.

The box was always locked.

Sometimes customers asked what was inside.

“A broken watch,” Eliza would say.

This was not entirely true.

On certain winter mornings, when snow fell over Bellweather and the town hall clock struck 6:09, Eliza could hear three faint ticks from inside the box.

One for the past.

One for the future.

And one for the brief, immeasurable moment in which the people we love are never lost at all.

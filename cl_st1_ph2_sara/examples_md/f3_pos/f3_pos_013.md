Mara first noticed the missing afternoon because of the pears.

At ten in the morning, they had been hard and green in a blue bowl on her kitchen table. She remembered touching one before leaving for work, pressing her thumb against its skin and deciding it needed another day.

At six that evening, all five pears were yellow and soft. One had collapsed into itself, leaking sweet juice across the table.

Mara stood in the doorway with her keys in her hand.

Outside, rain shivered against the windows. Her coat was dry. Her shoes were dry. She had no memory of coming home.

The last thing she remembered was stepping into the elevator at the municipal archives, where she worked cataloguing property records. She had pressed the button for the lobby. The clock above the elevator had read 1:07 p.m.

Now it was Thursday evening.

The elevator ride had begun on Wednesday.

Mara checked her phone. There were no missed calls, no alarming messages, no gaps in her work calendar. According to her supervisor, she had submitted three completed reports that afternoon and returned on Thursday morning as usual.

According to her bank account, she had bought a train ticket to a town called Bellweather.

According to the mud on the hem of her gray skirt, she had gone somewhere her shoes had not.

She telephoned her older brother, Elias.

“Do you remember speaking to me yesterday?” she asked.

There was a pause. Elias was accustomed to unusual questions from Mara. As children, she had once woken him at midnight to ask whether shadows existed in darkness.

“You called around four,” he said. “You sounded happy.”

“Happy?”

“Unreasonably happy. I assumed you’d received a promotion or committed a minor crime.”

“What did I say?”

“That you’d found Dad’s watch.”

Mara looked toward the narrow shelf above the radiator.

Their father’s watch was there.

It was a plain silver pocket watch, scratched across the back, with a short chain ending in a brass hook. He had carried it every day until the afternoon he disappeared, seventeen years earlier. The police had found his coat beside the river. They had never found his body.

Mara crossed the room slowly and picked up the watch.

It was warm.

She pressed the catch. The lid sprang open, revealing a white face and black hands. The watch was ticking, though it had not ticked since she was twelve.

The hands showed 1:07.

“Mara?” Elias said through the phone. “Are you still there?”

She closed the watch.

“I think I went to Bellweather.”

“Where’s that?”

“I don’t know.”

“You said something else yesterday,” Elias added. His voice had lost its teasing edge. “You said, ‘He waited as long as he could.’ Then you started crying.”

Mara sat beside the bowl of ruined pears.

“Did I say who?”

“No.”

The watch ticked in her palm.

“Don’t go anywhere,” Elias said.

But Mara was already thinking about the train ticket.

Bellweather did not appear on any current map. It did appear in an atlas from 1963, tucked between a marsh and the western branch of the Dorrin River. A railway line passed through it, though the town’s name was printed in such faint letters that Mara almost missed it.

At work the next morning, she searched the municipal records.

Bellweather had once contained a textile mill, a church, eighty-six houses, and a railway station. The mill had closed in 1978. After that, the population dwindled. Flooding damaged the road. The railway discontinued service in 1991.

The town itself had been officially dissolved twenty years ago.

Yet at 12:40 p.m., a train to Bellweather appeared on the old departure board in Central Station.

Mara stood beneath the board, clutching her father’s watch in one hand and a return ticket in the other. She had told Elias where she was going. He had responded with a long silence and then said he would meet her there.

He did not arrive before the train doors opened.

The conductor wore a dark blue uniform of an old-fashioned cut. He had a narrow white beard and a scar that drew one corner of his mouth upward.

“Bellweather?” Mara asked.

“If that’s where you need to go.”

“Does this train run every day?”

“No.”

“When does it run?”

The conductor examined her ticket.

“When someone has misplaced enough time.”

Behind her, commuters crossed the station without looking at the train. Their shoes struck the floor in a rush of ordinary minutes.

Mara stepped aboard.

The carriage smelled of dust, rain, and oranges. There were only three other passengers: an elderly woman holding a child’s red mitten, a young man in a wedding suit, and a nurse with a wilted bouquet across her knees. None looked at Mara.

The train departed without a whistle.

At first, the city moved past the windows: offices, apartment blocks, laundries, and gray bridges. Then came warehouses. Then fields. Rain stretched the landscape into vertical threads.

Mara sat opposite the old woman.

“Have you been to Bellweather before?” she asked.

The woman turned the mitten over in her hands.

“Once,” she said. “But I was too early.”

“What’s there?”

“A place that keeps what we cannot.”

The young man in the wedding suit laughed bitterly.

“That is one way of saying it.”

The nurse looked up. “You should not frighten her.”

“She’s on the train,” the young man said. “Fright has already failed.”

Mara took out her father’s watch.

All three passengers became still.

“Where did you get that?” the nurse asked.

“It belonged to my father.”

“Did he give it to you?”

“No.”

“Then be careful what you ask for in Bellweather.”

The train entered a tunnel.

Darkness pressed against the windows, complete and depthless. The lamps overhead flickered. For an instant, Mara saw her reflection at twelve years old: hair in two braids, school uniform wrinkled, cheeks stained with tears.

Then the train emerged beneath a clear blue sky.

Bellweather Station stood alone on a platform overgrown with grass.

The town beyond it appeared neither abandoned nor alive. White curtains stirred behind clean windows. Bicycles leaned against fences. A loaf of bread cooled on the sill of a bakery. Yet there were no voices, no footsteps, no smoke rising from chimneys.

The station clock showed 1:07.

The conductor lowered the steps.

“Last train leaves at sunset,” he said.

“What happens if I miss it?”

“You will have more time.”

His crooked smile deepened.

“Just not where you left it.”

Mara walked into town.

At the first intersection stood a sign shaped like an arrow:

BUREAU OF LOST AFTERNOONS  
APPOINTMENTS UNNECESSARY

The arrow pointed toward a narrow brick building beside the river.

Inside, the bureau resembled the municipal archives where Mara worked. Tall shelves filled the room, each crowded with gray boxes. Dusty sunlight fell through high windows. Somewhere, a typewriter struck one key at a time.

At the front desk sat a girl of about sixteen with cropped black hair. She wore a green cardigan and was reading a book upside down.

“Name?” the girl asked.

“Mara Vale.”

The girl checked a ledger.

“You lost Wednesday afternoon.”

“Yes.”

“Recently acquired losses are on the second floor.”

“I also found something.”

“That happens.”

“My father’s watch.”

The girl’s expression changed.

She closed the ledger and looked at the watch.

“You’d better speak to the registrar.”

The registrar’s office was behind a door marked HOURS OF UNCONFIRMED OWNERSHIP. The woman inside was small and silver-haired. She had spectacles on a chain and ink on every finger.

“Mara Vale,” she said before Mara could introduce herself. “I wondered whether you would return.”

“Return?”

“You were here on Wednesday.”

“What happened to me?”

The registrar gestured toward a chair.

“Lost time is rarely lost in the way people imagine. It is not dropped like a coin. More often, it is surrendered. Sometimes willingly. Sometimes not.”

“I don’t remember surrendering anything.”

“That is usually part of the arrangement.”

The registrar opened a drawer and removed a thin file. Mara’s name was written on the cover.

Inside was a single page bearing her signature.

EXCHANGE AUTHORIZATION

I, Mara Lenore Vale, voluntarily surrender the memories formed between 1:07 p.m. Wednesday and 8:00 a.m. Thursday, in exchange for one hour of archived time belonging to Tomas Vale.

Below the sentence, in Mara’s handwriting, were the words:

I understand that the hour cannot be removed from Bellweather.

Mara read the document twice.

“Tomas Vale was my father.”

“Yes.”

“You have an hour of his life here?”

“We have many hours of many lives.”

“Is he alive?”

The registrar folded her ink-stained hands.

“Not in the sense you mean.”

Mara felt anger rise through her confusion.

“Then give me back Wednesday.”

“I cannot. It is no longer yours.”

“Who has it?”

“You do.”

“I don’t remember it.”

“Memory and possession are different things.”

The registrar stood and walked to the window. Outside, the Dorrin River moved soundlessly between pale reeds.

“Your father came to Bellweather seventeen years ago,” she said. “He surrendered one afternoon. Then another. Then many more.”

“Why?”

“To postpone something.”

“His death?”

“All lives contain many endings. He was trying to postpone one that belonged to someone else.”

The registrar looked at Mara.

“Yours.”

Mara gripped the arms of the chair.

“What are you talking about?”

“The day your father disappeared, you followed him to the river.”

Memory stirred: bright sun on water, her father shouting, a sensation of cold so total it became white.

“You fell from the footbridge,” the registrar continued. “The current carried you beneath a fallen tree. Your father reached you, but too late.”

“No. He pulled me out.”

“Yes. After he came here.”

The room tilted.

The registrar spoke gently.

“Bellweather permits certain exchanges. Your father gave up seventeen years of his own future to return and save you.”

“That’s impossible.”

“Nevertheless, you have lived them.”

Mara stared at the watch. Its hands remained fixed at 1:07, but the ticking grew louder.

“Why don’t I remember any of this?”

“Because the returned do not.”

“And my father?”

“He had one hour left. He used it to take you home. Afterward, he returned to Bellweather.”

“Why would he come back?”

“Because seventeen years do not vanish. They must be lived somewhere.”

Mara stood so quickly that the chair scraped across the floor.

“Take me to him.”

The registrar’s face softened with pity.

“You already asked me that on Wednesday.”

“And?”

“And I took you.”

The silver watch clicked open by itself.

For the first time, the hands began to move.

The second hand swept backward.

The office dissolved.

Mara stood beneath a cedar tree on a summer afternoon. Sunlight warmed her face. Beyond the tree stretched a field of tall grass, each blade trembling in a wind she could not feel.

A man sat on a bench.

He was older than her father had been when he disappeared. His hair was almost white, his shoulders stooped, but Mara knew him by the way he held his hands—fingers interlaced, thumbs moving against each other whenever he was nervous.

He looked up.

“There you are,” Tomas Vale said.

Mara could not move.

He rose from the bench, smiling uncertainly.

“I thought you might be angry.”

She struck him across the face.

The sound startled birds from the cedar.

Then she threw her arms around him.

Her father held her as though he had been waiting with his arms empty for seventeen years.

Perhaps he had.

“You left us,” she said into his shoulder.

“I know.”

“Elias thought you were dead.”

“I know.”

“Mother waited every night for six months. She kept dinner warm.”

His breath shook.

“I know.”

“She died not knowing.”

“I know.”

Mara stepped back. His cheek was red where she had struck him.

“You should have told us.”

“I had one hour. I carried you home, called the ambulance, and left the watch beside your bed. I thought it would explain someday.”

“It explained nothing.”

“No.” He smiled sadly. “I was always better with machines than explanations.”

She remembered him repairing clocks at the kitchen table. Their small apartment had once been filled with ticking. After his disappearance, her mother had stopped every clock in the house.

Mara looked across the field. In the distance were hundreds of small enclosures: gardens, kitchens, workshops, hospital rooms, railway platforms. People moved within them like actors repeating scenes.

“What is this place?”

“The parts of our lives we give away.”

“Are you trapped here?”

“Trapped is a word people use when they dislike the price of a decision.”

“You gave away everything.”

“Not everything. I had twelve years with you. Fourteen with Elias. Eighteen with your mother. And one hour afterward.”

“That wasn’t enough.”

“It was what I had.”

Mara wiped her face. “Why did I come on Wednesday?”

“To find me.”

“How?”

“The watch began ticking because my borrowed years were almost finished. You followed it.”

“And I traded my memories?”

He nodded.

“Why would I do that?”

“Because when you reached me, I had seven minutes left. You surrendered your afternoon to buy us more time.”

Mara glanced at the sun. It hung above the field, but now she could see it descending.

“How much more?”

“Until sunset.”

She felt the lost hours moving around her, invisible but present. Wednesday had not disappeared. It was here—in the warmth of the cedar, in the grass, in the time between one heartbeat and the next.

“What did we talk about?” she asked.

“Your life. Elias. Your mother. Pears.”

“Pears?”

“You said you’d bought too many.”

Despite herself, Mara laughed.

Her father’s face brightened. For a moment, he looked as he had when she was a child.

“Can I have the memories back?” she asked.

“No. You exchanged them.”

“Then tell me everything again.”

So he did.

They walked across the field as the sun lowered. Tomas told her what he had said on Wednesday: that he had watched her life from Bellweather through misplaced moments. He had seen her on afternoons she later could not quite remember—falling asleep on buses, standing at windows, walking home without knowing which streets she had taken.

He had seen Elias marry and divorce. He had seen their mother set two extra plates at Christmas for three years, then one extra plate for five more.

“He blamed you,” Mara said.

“Elias?”

“Yes.”

“He had the right.”

“He blamed me too. Because you vanished while saving me.”

Tomas stopped walking.

“He knew?”

“Not what happened. But he remembered that I followed you to the river. He thought if I’d stayed home, you would have come back.”

Her father shut his eyes.

“I wanted to save everyone from the truth,” he said. “That is the danger of sacrifice. You can become proud of the pain you prevent and blind to the pain you cause.”

They reached a narrow stream. Flat stones formed a path across it.

On the other side stood a kitchen exactly as Mara remembered it from childhood. There were no walls, only the floor, cupboards, stove, and yellow table beneath the open sky.

Her mother stood at the counter slicing apples.

Mara’s heart lurched.

She took a step forward, but Tomas caught her wrist.

“That is only an afternoon,” he said. “Not her.”

Her mother turned, smiling at someone who was no longer there. She placed the apple slices into a bowl. Her lips moved.

“What is she saying?” Mara whispered.

Tomas listened.

“Don’t eat them all before dinner.”

Mara began to cry again, but quietly.

They sat at the yellow table while the memory repeated around them. Her mother sliced the apples. Sunlight shifted across the floor, then returned. Somewhere outside the remembered kitchen, a dog barked the same three barks.

“Do you stay here?” Mara asked.

“Sometimes. Your mother lost this afternoon after a fever. She never knew it came here.”

“Can you speak to her?”

“No.”

“Does that help?”

“Some days.”

The sky was turning gold.

“What happens at sunset?” Mara asked.

“My time ends.”

“And then?”

“I don’t know.”

“There must be another exchange.”

“There is always another exchange.”

“Then take some of mine.”

Tomas looked at her sharply.

“No.”

“One year. I can give you one.”

“No.”

“You gave me seventeen.”

“That was my choice.”

“This is mine.”

“No, Mara. It is grief’s choice, and grief is a poor guardian.”

She stood.

“You don’t get to decide again. You decided everything for all of us, and we lived with it.”

“I know.”

“Then let me decide this.”

He looked toward the sinking sun.

“If you give me a year, what do we do with it? Walk this field? Watch old rooms repeat themselves? I cannot leave Bellweather.”

“We could talk.”

“For a year?”

“I have seventeen years to tell you.”

“And then?”

She did not answer.

He reached across the table and covered her hand.

“And then you give another year. And another. Until Elias waits for you as you waited for me.”

Mara looked at their hands. His was spotted with age.

“Come home,” she whispered.

“I cannot.”

“Please.”

“I would if love were enough.”

The remembered kitchen faded as sunlight touched the horizon.

In the field, doors began appearing. They stood upright without walls: red doors, blue doors, iron doors, doors of glass. Some opened onto bedrooms. Others onto streets. Through one, Mara saw Central Station and the old train waiting beside the platform.

Her father rose.

“It’s time.”

She shook her head.

“Mara.”

“I don’t remember Wednesday.”

“Maybe that’s mercy.”

“I don’t want mercy. I want you.”

“I know.”

“You keep saying that.”

“Because it is the only answer I have left.”

The ticking watch grew louder. Tomas took it from her palm and wound it once.

“I carried this on the day Elias was born,” he said. “I carried it when I married your mother. I carried it to the river. I thought it measured what I had. But clocks don’t measure time. They measure our fear of losing it.”

He closed her fingers around the watch.

“Will I forget this too?” she asked.

“No. This afternoon is yours.”

Behind them, the cedar tree had begun to lose its leaves. They fell upward into the darkening sky.

“Tell Elias I am sorry,” Tomas said.

“Tell him yourself.”

“I cannot.”

“I’ll bring him here.”

“If he needs to come, the train will appear.”

Mara hated the bureau, the train, the field, and every calm rule that treated love as an entry in a ledger. She wanted to tear the doors from their hinges. She wanted to seize her father and drag him into the ordinary world by force.

Instead, she embraced him.

He rested his chin on her head.

“You grew taller,” he said.

“I was twelve.”

“I know, but still.”

She laughed against his coat, and he laughed too.

The last edge of the sun vanished.

For a heartbeat, Tomas remained in her arms.

Then she was holding his empty coat.

The field went dark.

Mara stood alone until the cedar leaves stopped rising.

The registrar found her beside the bench.

“I’m sorry,” the woman said.

Mara handed her the coat.

“No,” the registrar replied. “That belongs to you.”

“What happens to him now?”

“Perhaps nothing. Perhaps everything.”

“That is not an answer.”

“It is the oldest answer we have.”

Mara returned through the door to Central Station.

The old woman, the young man, and the nurse were already aboard the train. Their faces revealed nothing. Perhaps they had found what they wanted. Perhaps no one ever did.

As the train pulled away, Mara looked back at Bellweather.

The station was gone.

There was only a marsh beneath the evening sky.

Elias was waiting in Central Station when she arrived. He stood beneath the departure board, soaked from the rain, furious and afraid.

He hurried toward her.

“Where have you been? I came as soon as I could. There was no train, no Bellweather, no one knew what I was talking about—”

Mara held out their father’s coat.

Elias stopped.

He touched the sleeve.

For a long moment, neither of them spoke.

“Was he there?” Elias asked.

“Yes.”

“Is he alive?”

Mara considered the question.

“He waited as long as he could.”

Elias lowered his head. His shoulders began to shake.

Mara put her arms around him, and together they stood in the bright, crowded station while commuters flowed around them, each carrying hours they believed were theirs.

Later, at Mara’s apartment, they threw away the spoiled pears.

She told Elias everything. She told him about the river, the exchange, the field, and their father’s last sunset. She expected disbelief, but Elias only listened, turning the silver watch over in his hands.

When she finished, it was nearly dawn.

“He saved you,” Elias said.

“Yes.”

“And he left us.”

“Yes.”

Elias nodded, as if both truths required room.

They hung their father’s coat beside the door.

The silver watch never ticked again.

Years passed. Mara left the municipal archives and began repairing clocks, though she refused to fix any that were merely decorative. Elias remarried. He had a daughter and named her Lenore, after Mara.

Sometimes Mara lost an afternoon.

She would look up from her work and discover that the light had changed. A cup of tea would be cold beside her. A clock she did not remember repairing would be ticking perfectly on the table.

At first, she searched for Bellweather.

She watched departure boards. She collected old maps. She followed railway lines into marshes and abandoned towns.

But the train did not return.

Eventually, she understood that not every forgotten hour had been stolen. Some had simply passed while she was not paying attention. That, too, was a kind of mercy.

On the seventeenth anniversary of her journey, Mara placed five green pears in a blue bowl.

By evening, they were still hard.

She chose one, sliced it, and arranged the pieces on two plates. Elias was coming for dinner. His daughter would arrive early and eat half the slices before he got there.

Mara opened the window.

Rain had just begun, gentle and silver in the streetlights. Somewhere in the city, a train sounded its whistle, though no railway ran nearby.

For an instant, every clock in the apartment stopped at 1:07.

Mara stood very still.

Then, one by one, they began again.

She smiled, set the table, and let the afternoon go.

**What the Throat Keeps**

The envelope came on a Tuesday, after the rain had turned the road outside your compound the color of ground pepper. Your mother held it by one corner because the paper looked expensive and because anything from America deserved care. Your father wiped his hands on his trousers before opening it. By evening, neighbors you had not spoken to in months were calling you nwanne and saying they had always known you would go far.

America entered the room before you did. It arrived as a wide house with white pillars, a silver car, and a refrigerator that made ice without plastic trays. Your youngest brother asked for a basketball jersey. Your mother wanted a handbag with a real designer name, not one spelled wrongly. Your father said he wanted nothing, which meant he wanted something useful: a blood-pressure machine, perhaps, or enough money to repair the rust-eaten roof of his Peugeot.

The night before you left, your aunties sang too loudly and pressed folded notes into your palm. Crisp hundreds, tired twenties, one five-naira note soft as cloth. Your mother told you not to eat food from strangers on the plane. Your father said Americans did not understand family, so you must remember who you were.

At the airport, he held your shoulder longer than the others did.

“You will study,” he said. “You will not waste this chance.”

You promised.

Your uncle met you in Boston under a sign with your name written in black marker. He was not truly your uncle. His father and your father came from neighboring villages, which was enough. He hugged you and smelled of peppermint and the leather seats of his car.

On the drive to Maine, you counted bridges and lanes. Cars moved without horns. The forests stood close to the road, dense and orderly, as if somebody had planted each tree after measuring the distance between them. Your uncle asked what you thought.

“It is fine,” you said.

He laughed. “Fine? This is America.”

His house had pale siding and a red mailbox shaped like a barn. Inside, your auntie welcomed you with rice, chicken, and salad wet with a sweet white cream. Their children looked at you as if they had been promised a present and received a person instead. The younger one asked whether your village had lions. The older one asked if you knew Beyoncé.

You said Lagos was not a village.

He blinked. “But you’re African.”

Your auntie told him not to be rude, though she smiled while saying it. Then she showed you the basement room where you would sleep. The walls were unpainted, the mattress lay on a metal frame, and a small window near the ceiling displayed shoes passing outside. You liked the room. It was more private than the one you had shared with your cousins during university strikes, and the carpet, though stained, softened your footsteps.

For the first weeks you woke early to make lunches for the children. Your auntie worked at a nursing home, and your uncle sold cars at a dealership forty minutes away. He explained America to you over breakfast. Credit was more important than cash. Smiling was not always friendliness. At school, you must not argue with professors because Americans did not respect cleverness unless it came with the correct papers.

He would help you apply to a community college after you became settled. In the meantime, you cleaned the house, collected the children, and watched daytime television, where women cried because their husbands had slept with their sisters.

On Sundays, you drove to a church in Portland where Nigerians praised God in careful English. After service they gathered around foil trays of jollof rice and moi moi, lowering their voices to discuss people who had married for papers. A woman with eyebrows drawn in sharp arches asked whether you had a boyfriend at home. When you said no, she touched your arm and told you there were many good Nigerian men in America, especially pharmacists.

Your uncle liked to say nothing was free. He said it when you asked about tuition. He said it when you left a lamp on. He said it while explaining how his first employer had made him work Sundays without extra pay.

“America is give-and-take,” he told you. “You cannot only take.”

You nodded because he had bought your winter coat. It was too large and the sleeves covered your fingers, but your auntie said you would appreciate it when snow came.

The snow came one night without noise. In the morning, the yard had disappeared beneath whiteness. You stood at the glass door until your uncle came behind you. He placed his hands on your shoulders.

“Beautiful, eh?”

You said yes.

His hands stayed there.

You told yourself that people touched differently in America. They hugged acquaintances. They patted waiters. They said love you before ending telephone calls with people they did not love.

A week later, your auntie took the children to visit her sister in New Jersey. Your uncle returned from work early. You were in the basement folding clothes when he came down. He sat on the mattress and asked if you were happy.

“Yes.”

“Are we treating you well?”

“Yes.”

“Do you know how much your ticket cost?”

You looked at the shirt in your hands, a small green one with a dinosaur on it.

He spoke gently. He said college was expensive. He said recommendation letters were important. He said he knew people. His palm moved over your knee as if he were smoothing a wrinkle from the jeans.

You stood up. He caught your wrist.

The room became very clear: the gray pipe along the ceiling, the basket of clean socks, the brown ring on the carpet where something had spilled long ago. When you pulled away, he pushed you against the wall. His breath smelled of coffee. You kicked his shin and he called you an ungrateful girl.

“You think people carry people on their head for nothing?” he said.

You ran upstairs without your coat. Outside, the cold entered your chest like a blade. You walked until the house could no longer be seen and then stood beside a petrol station, although here it was called a gas station, trembling so hard your teeth struck one another.

You did not call your auntie. You knew your uncle would say you had misunderstood. He would say you were village-minded. Your auntie might believe you, but belief would not make a bed appear for you. Belief would not pay tuition.

America was give-and-take.

A bus driver let you ride to Portland even though you were short by seventy-five cents. From there you took another bus south. You chose Connecticut because the ticket was affordable and because the name looked harmless on the map.

The town where you got off had one main street, a laundromat, two churches, and a restaurant with a wooden lobster hanging above the door. The restaurant owner, a broad woman called Maureen, hired you after looking at your visa and then at your face.

“You understand this is cash,” she said. “No paperwork.”

You understood. You did not tell her that the visa allowed you to stay but that the work forms had not arrived, that the address on every application was the house you had fled, that you no longer knew what letters might be waiting in Maine.

She paid you less than the other waitresses. You knew because they complained about their wages within your hearing. You said nothing. She gave you evening shifts and let you take home bread that would otherwise be thrown away.

You rented a room above a shop selling used furniture. The room had a sink, a hot plate, and a radiator that clicked all night. The bathroom was down the hall. Its window would not close, and in winter you could see your breath while sitting on the toilet.

Every month, you put most of your money in an envelope and sent it to your parents. You wrote their address carefully. You did not include your own.

You wanted to write that you had registered for college.

You wanted to write that you lived in a house with a lawn.

You wanted to write that Americans all owned cars, as your cousins believed, and that when you crossed the street people rolled down their windows to offer you rides.

There was nothing to write.

At the restaurant, customers asked where you came from. When you said Nigeria, some asked if that was near Jamaica. Others said they had sponsored a child in Africa. A man wearing a cap embroidered with an American flag asked if you had lived in a hut.

“No,” you said.

He leaned toward his wife. “They learn English so well.”

You carried away his plate. Half a steak remained on it, pink in the middle. His wife had left a mound of mashed potatoes untouched. You thought of your mother scraping burned rice from the bottom of a pot because wasting food brought bad luck. In the kitchen, you emptied everything into a black bin while the cook sprayed soap over dishes.

Sometimes Maureen caught you wrapping leftover meat in foil.

“You can take the bread,” she said. “Not the entrées.”

The word came out grandly, although the food was fried fish, boiled carrots, and meatloaf glazed with ketchup.

You stopped taking the meat.

At night, something gathered at the base of your throat. It had no shape you could describe. It felt like a cord drawn tight by patient hands, tightening one small measure each evening. You would lie on the mattress listening to the radiator, swallowing to prove that you still could.

You did not call home. International calls cost money, but this was not the reason. If your mother heard your voice, she would know. She had always known when you lied, even when you said you had eaten or that your menstrual cramps were not bad. Your father would ask about classes. Your brothers would shout requests into the telephone.

So you sent money.

Every month.

The first time the white boy came into the restaurant, he ordered coffee and asked whether the fish soup had cream. He wore a navy sweater with a small hole near one cuff. His hair was the pale yellow of dried maize silk, and he had a book open beside his plate.

You poured his coffee and saw the title. It was about the Congo.

He looked up. “Have you read it?”

“No.”

“Sorry. Stupid question.”

You wondered why it was stupid. You said nothing.

The next week, he returned and ordered the soup without cream. He was there again three days later. This time he asked your name.

You hesitated. Nobody in America had needed it except people filling out forms.

“Akunna,” you said.

He repeated it wrongly.

You corrected him.

He tried again, slower, and came close.

He told you his name was Evan. He studied political science at a university forty minutes away and had spent a summer in Senegal. He had also visited Morocco, Egypt, Tanzania, and South Africa. He listed them without boasting, which made the list sound more like boasting.

“Where in Nigeria are you from?” he asked.

“Lagos.”

“But originally?”

You looked at him.

He reddened. “I mean your family.”

You said your parents were from Enugu State.

“Igbo,” he said, pronouncing it well.

You disliked that he knew. You liked that he knew.

When he asked you to have coffee, you laughed because he was already having coffee. Then you understood and said no. He returned the following week with a book by a Nigerian writer and left it beside his tip. Inside he had written his telephone number.

You did not call.

He returned with no book and did not mention the first one. He asked for pie. Maureen warmed a slice in the microwave, and you watched him eat the soft apples and leave the crust.

“You don’t like it?” you asked.

“It’s good. I’m full.”

You thought of the crust in the rubbish bin. You thought of the steak, the potatoes, the ends of bread Maureen allowed you to take because they had no value.

The next day, you called him from the pay phone outside the laundromat.

He drove a car that looked old but was not old enough to shame him. A faded university sticker covered a dent on the bumper. He took you to a Chinese restaurant where red paper lanterns hung from the ceiling. You ordered by price. He ordered dumplings, noodles, beef with mushrooms, and tea that smelled of flowers.

At the end, the waitress brought two fortune cookies. His paper said he would soon begin an exciting journey. Yours was blank.

“Manufacturing error,” he said.

You folded the empty strip and put it in your purse.

He told you about his parents. His mother was a surgeon. His father taught economics. They had a house in Vermont and another near the ocean. He had gone to schools where students learned Latin and sailed boats. He said these things apologetically, as if wealth were an illness he had survived.

When he asked about your family, you told him your father worked for a government agency and had not been promoted in twelve years because he would not bribe anyone. You did not say that he had once knelt before a man young enough to be his son to ask for his salary, or that you had watched from the car while the man laughed with his friends. Your father’s knees had sunk into roadside mud. When he returned to the car, his trousers looked as if pigs had rolled against them.

You did not tell Evan because he would call it corruption and look solemn. He would understand the word and miss the humiliation.

He took you home after midnight. At your door, he asked if he could kiss you.

You said, “Americans ask for everything.”

“Is that bad?”

“I don’t know.”

You kissed him before he could ask again.

His eyes were green with a ring of brown around the pupils, the color of oil when sunlight entered the bottle. Extra-virgin, the label said in the shop, as if oil could possess virtue. You had never bought it. It cost nearly an hour of work.

For your birthday, Evan gave you a glass pendant made by an artist in Vermont. It was blue and curved like a drop of water. You thanked him and thought of what it had cost. With the same money, your mother could buy garri for weeks.

You wore it when he visited.

He brought other things: incense from India, a woven purse from Guatemala, earrings shaped like tiny Maasai shields. They were beautiful and had no use. Once he brought a carved bowl and said it was fair-trade. You wanted to ask whether fairness made soup taste different.

You gave him a shirt. Plain cotton, dark gray. He wore it until the collar softened.

With him, you began to see places beyond the restaurant and your room. He took you to museums where masks from Nigeria stood behind glass beneath neat labels. He took you to the sea in winter. The water was steel-colored and violent, nothing like Bar Beach, where hawkers carried groundnuts and boiled eggs through the crowds.

At a farmers’ market, he bought cheese flecked with herbs. It cost more than you spent on food in three days. When you said so, he answered that the farmers needed support.

You wanted to tell him that your mother also needed support.

Instead, you tasted the cheese. It was good.

He asked you to move nearer to the university, but the rents were high. He offered to pay the difference. You refused. He said money did not matter to him, which was a thing only people with money could say.

The cord at your throat loosened when he slept beside you. You would wake with his arm heavy over your waist, his breath warm against the back of your neck, and for a moment there was no restaurant, no basement in Maine, no envelope traveling to Lagos without a letter.

You did not tell him about your uncle. You said only that the arrangement had not worked out.

“You should report him if he exploited you,” Evan said.

You watched his face, open and certain. He believed institutions waited like clean hospitals, ready to receive every wound. You imagined telling a police officer that your father’s friend had touched your knee, held your wrist, pushed you against a wall. You imagined your uncle showing receipts for your ticket and winter coat. You imagined your auntie crying.

“I left,” you said. “It is finished.”

“It’s not finished if he does it to someone else.”

You turned away.

Later, he apologized, although you had not explained what apology was needed.

When you ate at restaurants together, people watched. Some stared quickly and then became interested in their plates. Some smiled too much. Once, an older black man shook his head as you passed. Once, a white woman told Evan he was adventurous.

He laughed afterward. You did not.

“What does she think you are?” he asked. “A safari?”

You said, “She was speaking to you, not me.”

At the university, his friends spoke about Africa with concerned voices. They discussed debt relief, female circumcision, civil war. A girl with silver rings asked if you had been traumatized by poverty.

“No,” you said.

She looked disappointed.

Another friend said he wanted to visit Nigeria because he preferred authentic places. He complained that a trip to Thailand had been ruined by tourists.

Evan told him he was an idiot. You should have been grateful. Instead, you resented the ease with which Evan defended you, as if he could step into your skin for five minutes and step out clean.

On the drive home, he said, “I’m sorry about them.”

“They are your friends.”

“Not all of them.”

“You were laughing before.”

“Not at that.”

“You can laugh. You can travel to Senegal, then come back and tell people what Africa is like.”

He gripped the steering wheel. “That’s not fair.”

You looked out at the trees. Their leaves were red and yellow, bright as warning signs.

He said he knew he was privileged. He said it as if knowledge were payment. You asked him what he had sacrificed by knowing.

For two weeks, you did not see him.

The cord returned at night, tighter than before. You worked, ate toast over the sink, and watched the telephone. When it rang, you let it ring five times before answering.

“I miss you,” he said.

You could have said it back.

Instead, you asked, “Have you eaten?”

He laughed softly. “Yes.”

You had missed the laugh.

At Christmas, he took you to his parents’ house in Vermont. Their rooms were warm without radiators clanging. Paintings hung in places where you would have put calendars. His mother hugged you and said your name correctly; Evan had practiced with her. His father asked intelligent questions about Nigerian elections and listened to the answers.

At dinner there was lamb, roasted vegetables, three kinds of wine, and a cake dense with chocolate. His mother apologized that the lamb was overcooked. It was pink near the bone.

Afterward, you watched her scrape plates into a machine that growled beneath the sink. You offered to help, but she said you were a guest.

Evan’s room contained photographs from his travels. In one, he stood with his arm around three Senegalese boys. They were barefoot. He wore expensive hiking sandals and smiled broadly.

“They were great kids,” he said.

You asked if he knew their names.

He named two, then paused.

You wished you had not asked. You wished he had remembered.

That night, his mother gave you a wool scarf. She said the color suited your skin. The scarf was soft and warm, a useful present. You wore it indoors until Evan teased you.

In bed, he asked if your family celebrated Christmas with gifts.

“Sometimes.”

“What did your parents give you last year?”

You tried to remember. Your father had brought home a chicken. Your mother had sewn matching dresses for you and your cousin from fabric she had kept in a cupboard for two years. There had been no wrapping paper.

“My father gave us meat,” you said.

Evan waited, perhaps expecting irony. When none came, he kissed your forehead.

You began a letter in January.

Dear Mama and Papa,

You stopped.

You began again.

I am sorry I have not written.

You told them about the restaurant, though you called it temporary work before college. You told them you were safe. You did not mention Maine. You did not mention Evan because your mother would ask if his people knew you, and your father would ask what kind of future a white boy imagined with a Nigerian girl.

You enclosed your address before you could change your mind.

Three weeks later, a letter arrived. Your youngest brother had written the envelope. His handwriting leaned forward as though the words were running.

Inside were four pages from your mother.

She thanked you for the money. She said your brothers were well. The neighbor’s daughter had married a man in Abuja. Your auntie’s fibroids had returned.

Your father had died five months earlier.

He collapsed outside a pharmacy while waiting for his blood-pressure medicine. A taxi driver took him to a clinic, but there was no doctor. They buried him in the village. Your mother wrote that they had not known where to reach you. The number your uncle had given them no longer connected. They had sent messages through people in America. Perhaps you had been too busy with school to reply.

You read the line again.

Five months.

You had served soup, carried plates, kissed Evan in the sea wind. You had stood beneath paintings in Vermont while your father lay in red earth. You had sent money to a dead man.

The room changed size. The walls moved outward and then rushed back. The cord around your throat pulled so hard that your breath clogged. You pressed your fingers against your neck, searching for something to tear away.

You made a sound you did not recognize.

The shop owner found you on the stairs and called Evan from the number taped beside your telephone. He came before you had finished crying. He sat on the floor and held you. You struck his chest once, then again, not because he had done anything but because his father was alive in a warm house in Vermont, discussing elections and drinking wine.

Evan held your wrists loosely.

“I’m sorry,” he said.

You hated the uselessness of the words. You clung to him.

For three days, you did not work. Maureen called once and said she could not keep covering your shifts. Evan offered to speak to her. You told him not to. He offered to pay your rent. You told him not to. He brought soup, fruit, tissues, and flowers. The flowers opened yellow mouths on the windowsill and then began to rot.

You booked a flight to Lagos with nearly all your savings.

Evan said he would pay. You said no.

He said he would come with you. He could help with the journey, with arrangements, with anything your mother needed.

You imagined him at your father’s compound, bending beneath the low doorway. You imagined neighbors gathering to see the oyibo who had come with you. You imagined him photographing harmattan dust because it looked beautiful in evening light. You imagined your mother watching his face and learning another reason for your silence.

“I have to go alone,” you said.

He nodded, but hurt entered his eyes.

At the airport, announcements scattered above you. Families pushed trolleys piled with taped cartons, rice cookers, televisions, and bulging suitcases. A woman argued about excess baggage while her child slept across two plastic chairs. The smell of coffee drifted from a kiosk.

Evan held your hand beneath the departures screen.

“When will you come back?” he asked.

You looked at the cities flickering above him.

He corrected himself. “Will you come back?”

You wanted to say yes because he had learned your name properly and worn the shirt you gave him until it faded. You wanted to say no because he could cross oceans for curiosity while you crossed them carrying duty, shame, and the news of a grave.

You wanted to tell him the cord had loosened with him but had never vanished. Perhaps it did not belong to America. Perhaps it had begun long before, in your father’s car while mud dried on his knees, or in the airport at Lagos when everyone’s hopes had been packed into your suitcase without being weighed.

There was too much to say, so you said nothing.

He nodded as if silence were an answer he could respect.

When your flight was called, you wrapped your arms around him. He held you carefully at first, then tightly. You felt his heart striking beneath his coat. Your cheek pressed against the small worn place near his collar, and for one moment you wanted the loudspeaker to break, the plane to leave without you, your father to be alive and annoyed that you had wasted money on such an expensive ticket.

Evan touched the blue pendant at your throat. You had forgotten you were wearing it.

“Keep it,” he said.

You almost laughed. You had not planned to return it.

He lifted your bag onto your shoulder. Beyond the checkpoint, an officer waited with one hand open for your passport.

You walked away without turning. Then, just before the line curved out of sight, you looked back.

Evan stood where you had left him. He raised one hand.

You raised yours.

The pressure at your throat remained, but it shifted as you moved, no longer a hand closing, no longer a cord drawn by somebody you could not see. It felt now like the ache left after carrying a heavy basin home: proof of weight, proof also that you had borne it.

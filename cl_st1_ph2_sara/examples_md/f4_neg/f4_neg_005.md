# The Three Strawberries

It was in the first week of June, when the year, like a happy youth, has forgotten the promises of childhood and has not yet begun to reckon with the debts of maturity, that I went fishing in the Swiftwater.

The name of the brook is somewhat boastful. In April, to be sure, when the snows are loosening their white hold upon the mountains, it runs with a brown and urgent flood, shouldering the stones aside, plucking dead branches from the banks, and shouting its news to the valley. But in June it has learned better manners. It slips rather than hurries, spreads itself in lucid pools, whispers under alder-roots, and makes small, silver speeches over the gravel. Here and there it laughs aloud; but this is only when the sun tickles it.

My basket contained three trout—not large ones, if you please, but honourable fish, taken with a brown hackle and without any assistance from hatcheries, keepers, posted notices, or other contrivances by which sport is improved almost to extinction. The morning had been warm, the water low, and the trout critical. I had walked far for them. Therefore, when noon came and the sun stood directly above the hemlocks, I laid my rod upon a bed of ferns and stretched myself beneath a leaning birch.

The earth was cool there. Last year’s leaves, brown and delicate as old letters, made a pleasant carpet; the roots of the birch fitted themselves beneath my shoulder with tolerable kindness; and a little wind, which had been wandering through the upper forest, came down occasionally to touch my forehead. The brook talked near by, not loudly enough to require an answer, but with that continuous, friendly murmur which leaves a man free to think his own thoughts and yet saves him from the loneliness of silence.

Before me the forest rose in ranks of green: maple and beech, ash and yellow birch, with dark companies of spruce standing farther up the hill. The new growth of the hemlocks burned at the tips of the old branches like verdant flames. Patches of sunlight lay upon the moss, gold on green; a black-and-white warbler crept head downward along a trunk; a redstart flashed his orange epaulettes among the leaves; and from some invisible choir-loft in the thicket came the cool, descending music of the veery.

June had hung her whole gallery with pictures and opened every door of her concert-hall. Along the brook the foamflower lifted its white plumes; the blue violets lingered in shaded places; pink laurel bloomed on the rocky ledges; yellow cinquefoil shone beside the path; and the bunchberry, with four white bracts surrounding its modest centre, looked like a little star fallen among the mosses. There were fragrances also—the clean odour of birch, the resinous breath of the pines, the faint sweetness of unseen blossoms, and beneath all the wholesome smell of wet earth and running water.

I lay at ease and admired the entertainment. Yet presently, in the ungrateful manner of mankind, I began to discover what was lacking.

“Nature,” I said, addressing that ancient lady with the familiarity permitted to those who love her and the impertinence common to those whom she feeds, “you have done exceedingly well with colour and music. Your birds are better than our orchestras, though they give no winter subscriptions; your flowers surpass our exhibitions, though no committee awards them medals; and your brook has more wit than many public speakers. But why have you neglected the refreshments?”

For a man who has walked six miles before luncheon is not content to nourish himself entirely upon beauty. The eye may feast on laurel and the ear on birdsong, but the stomach remains stubbornly literal. In August the woods offer blueberries; in September, blackberries; in October, nuts. But June seemed to me at that moment all promise and no provision, a table magnificently decorated and set with empty plates.

I had in my pocket a sandwich, somewhat flattened, and a square of gingerbread wrapped in paper. These were sufficient, but they were not poetical. Bread and meat belong to civilisation; gingerbread, though amiable, suggests the kitchen. I desired something born of the forest itself, some edible conclusion to the argument of blossom, sunlight, and song.

Nature made no audible reply. She seldom does. Her answers are generally placed where we may find them if we turn our heads at the proper moment.

I turned mine.

From a slender stem that had crept over the bank above my face hung three wild strawberries.

They were no larger than the end of my little finger. Two were scarlet, and the third, shaded by a leaf, retained one pale cheek. Their seeds stood out like points of gold. They hung together beneath three serrated leaves, as if some woodland jeweller had set rubies in a green brooch and left it there for a Dryad’s adornment.

Now I had passed that very plant when I lay down. My hand had almost brushed it. Yet I had not seen the berries until hunger sharpened sight—or until Nature, having enjoyed my complaint, chose to reveal her answer.

I picked the ripest.

There are moments when analysis is an impertinence. A wild strawberry should be eaten before it is described. I placed it on my tongue, and in that instant the whole forest became flavour.

I tasted the warmth of the sunlit bank and the coolness beneath the leaves; the odour of a hundred blossoms; the sparkle of the brook; the gold-green shimmer of birch foliage; the song of the veery, remote and liquid; the breath of the wandering wind; the rain of May; the deep brown strength of the earth. Sweetness was there, but sweetness alone is a dull virtue. There was also a bright, keen point of wildness, a woodland tang, something fragrant and elusive which departed almost before it could be named.

The berry was gone in a second; the delight remained.

I ate the second more slowly, which did not improve it. Perfect pleasures refuse to be lengthened by caution. The third, being not wholly ripe, I left upon the stem—partly from virtue, partly from prudence, and partly because one ought not to strip a shrine of all its jewels.

Have you ever eaten a cultivated strawberry as large as a small turnip? It arrives upon the table with a certain magnificence. It is red, regular, polished, substantial; it occupies half the saucer and appears capable of supporting a spoon. You divide it with a knife. You add sugar, because its sweetness has been lost in the process of attaining grandeur; you add cream, because its fragrance requires assistance; and at last, after considerable preparation, you eat it with respect and forget it without regret.

Beside such a berry my little forest fruit was a minnow beside Leviathan. Yet Leviathan does not always make the better meal.

Size is not the measure of excellence.

We have a curious faith in enlargement. We make larger houses, larger hotels, larger newspapers, larger fortunes, larger strawberries. We weigh, count, grade, advertise, and pack in boxes. If one acre yields a hundred baskets, we demand two hundred; if a berry is an inch wide, we breed another an inch and a quarter. Abundance is useful, no doubt. I have no quarrel with the market-gardener, who supplies whole cities with fruit and deserves both praise and payment. But multiplication is not creation, and magnitude is not flavour.

Flavour is the priceless quality.

Doctor William Butler, an English physician of lively memory, is said to have declared that doubtless God might have made a better berry than the strawberry, but doubtless God never did. The saying has outlived many weightier pronouncements by doctors, bishops, and kings. I suspect that it owes part of its immortality to the subject. A philosophy connected with fruit enters the mind more pleasantly than one associated with medicine.

But what strawberry did Doctor Butler praise? That question has often troubled me, though not enough to disturb sleep. He died, if the chroniclers may be trusted, in the early part of the seventeenth century, when James was king and England was occupied with theology, tobacco, voyages, court masques, and the preliminary quarrels which afterward grew into civil war. The great modern garden berries had not yet assembled their mixed ancestry and put on their present corpulence. Perhaps the doctor knew the small wood strawberry, the fragrant inhabitant of English banks and coppices. If so, his opinion was sound.

He may have eaten it after riding over a muddy road to visit a patient who refused his physic. He may have found it beside a hedge, red under the leaves, and discovered—as physicians sometimes do—that Providence keeps remedies which are not entered in the pharmacopœia. The berry refreshed him; gratitude took the form of an epigram; and thus, amid wars and rumours of wars, disputes over ceremonies, arguments concerning the divine right of kings, and much solemn shaking of learned heads, the strawberry secured its sentence of immortal praise.

Later came travellers and plants from distant shores. The Virginia strawberry crossed the Atlantic; the large-fruited Chilean arrived by another road; gardeners encouraged acquaintances among species that would never have met without human diplomacy; and from their alliances descended the imposing families that now occupy our catalogues. They bear illustrious names—Senator this, President that, Champion, Monarch, Giant, Perfection. The nomenclature of fruit is a peaceful refuge for defeated ambition. A man who cannot rule a province may at least introduce an Emperor gooseberry.

We owe much to these patient cultivators. They have lengthened seasons, increased crops, checked disease, and placed strawberries within reach of multitudes. It would be absurd to despise their work while eating its results. Yet I confess that after three saucers of the best domestic variety, crimson, symmetrical, and reinforced with sugar, I have sometimes remembered one tiny berry found by accident above the Swiftwater and felt that civilisation, for all its abundance, had missed a secret.

The secret was not flavour alone. It was discovery.

A touch of surprise is essential to perfect sweetness.

Consider the flowers. In a garden we know where they are. The tulips stand in battalions; the roses occupy appointed beds; the lilies have labels; the paths conduct us efficiently from one triumph to another. At the proper hour we may say, “Now I shall go out and see the delphiniums,” and, barring hail or the neighbour’s cat, the delphiniums will be there. This is comfortable. It is also a little like attending a reception by invitation.

But do you remember the first arbutus you ever found in the woods?

You had been searching perhaps for half an hour, kneeling beside patches of dull leaves, lifting them carefully, finding nothing but stems and disappointment. Then suddenly there came a fragrance—faint, spicy, unmistakable. You followed it to a sunny bank, parted the winter-browned foliage, and there were the clusters, waxen white and tender pink, hiding close to the earth. No gardener announced them. No signboard said, “Epigæa repens: Kindly Do Not Touch.” They had bloomed without permission and awaited no audience. You were not merely a spectator. You were a discoverer.

Once, in the northern mountains, I came upon a meadow of fringed gentians late in September. I was not seeking them. Indeed, I had lost the trail, which is one of the best methods of finding things not marked upon maps. The day had been grey, and a cold mist moved among the spruces. Then the clouds opened for five minutes, and in a hollow below me hundreds of blue flowers stood among the tawny grasses, each blossom twisted at the top like a little closed flame. The colour was not merely blue; it was September sky deepened by rain, distance brought near, a cool and solemn music translated for the eye.

Had I been conducted there by a guide at ten o’clock precisely, with luncheon arranged at twelve, I should still have admired the gentians. But would I have loved them? I think not so well. Their beauty was sharpened by the wrong turning, the mist, the sudden light, and the knowledge that five minutes earlier I had not suspected their existence.

Nature’s finest exhibitions are often unadvertised.

So it is with fishing. A pond stocked with trout of uniform age and appetite may provide an excellent return for the price of admission. The banks are mown, boats are numbered, rules are printed, and the fish, one imagines, have names. At eight o’clock Augustus is released near the landing; at nine he accepts a patent fly; at ten his successor, Theophilus, performs the same domestic duty. The angler goes home with a full basket and the melancholy of a man who has collected a debt.

But a free brook keeps its own accounts.

You do not know whether trout are there. You do not know what they are eating. The path ends, the alders close in, the stones are slippery, the pool that looked promising proves empty, and your finest cast catches a branch which no entomologist has yet classified. Mosquitoes dine upon you with confidence. Rain enters at the collar. Noon comes, and your basket is light.

Then, below a fallen cedar, you see a dark seam of water no wider than your hand. You drop a small fly beneath the branch. There is a flash, a tug, a swift curve of living gold and olive; and presently a trout lies in your wet palm, cold, spotted, perfect—the very spirit of the brook given form for an instant before you release it.

That trout may weigh six ounces. Yet he is worth a pound of tame fish.

I recall one in particular from a stream beyond Lake Memphremagog. We had followed an old lumber road through miles of second growth, crossed two bogs, and suffered the optimistic directions of a farmer who assured us that the water was “just over the ridge.” There were seven ridges. At last we heard the stream under the trees, although we could not see it. It was a narrow, amber current, stained by cedar roots and covered in places by mossy logs.

For an hour nothing happened. My companion, who held strong views on the relation between patience and Providence, had begun to revise them. Then, in a pocket of black water beneath a rock, a trout rose to my fly. He was not large, but his sides were bright as old gold, his fins edged with white, his red spots circled in blue. When I bent over him, I saw on the bank beside my knee a single purple orchid.

The fish escaped while I looked at the flower.

My companion called this carelessness. I called it exchange. Even now I cannot remember precisely the size of the trout, but I see the orchid as plainly as if it had bloomed in my hand. Its stem rose from the moss with no companion near it, a small imperial banner in the green dusk. Had it stood in a conservatory among a thousand rarities, I might have passed it with a nod. Alone beside that hidden stream, it possessed the authority of a revelation.

This is the charm of the wild: it does not promise, and therefore it can bestow.

Civilisation, with the best intentions, endeavours to remove uncertainty. It predicts the weather, surveys the roads, numbers the houses, regulates the forests, stocks the waters, labels the trees, schedules the pleasures, and insures us against everything except weariness. We are told when the train will arrive, what the dinner will contain, how long the journey will require, where the view may be seen, and at what point the sunset is considered most satisfactory.

I am grateful for time-tables when I must reach a city, and for bridges when the river is in flood. Only a fool despises order altogether. The bee has a hive, the beaver a dam, and even the stars keep their courses. But order, like salt, is useful in moderation and destructive when poured over the whole dish.

A regulated forest is a curious thing. The underbrush has been removed; the dead trees are cut lest they fall; the crooked trees are cut lest they offend; the old trees are cut lest they decay; the young trees stand in rows awaiting promotion. Fire is forbidden, flowers protected, paths improved, dangerous cliffs fenced, and solitude made accessible by automobile. It is admirable forestry, perhaps, but the Oreads have departed. No nymph with the least self-respect would inhabit a wood where every tree carries an inventory number.

The ancient Greeks, who were wiser in some matters than their lack of machinery might lead us to suppose, peopled the natural world with spirits. Dryads lived in trees, Naiads in springs, Oreads in mountains. The doctrine was not scientific; it was better than that—it was affectionate. A spring guarded by a nymph could not be regarded merely as so many gallons per minute. A grove inhabited by divinity invited reverence before calculation.

We need not revive the whole mythology. A modern Dryad would have difficulty with property taxes, and Pan himself might be arrested for playing his pipes in a quiet zone. Yet the instinct behind the fable remains sound. The world is more than material. A brook has something which chemical analysis does not capture; a flower contains what the botanical description omits; and a wild strawberry may carry in its small red globe the mystic enchantment of a June forest.

Science tells us how the fruit grows: rootstock, runner, leaf, blossom, pollen, seed. Commerce tells us how many quarts may be shipped. Cookery tells us how to make preserves. All are useful. But none explains why three berries, discovered when one is hungry and half asleep beneath a birch, should become a gift of Providence.

Perhaps the explanation lies partly in ourselves. We also are natural creatures, though we wear collars, consult clocks, and sit in committees. Somewhere beneath the layers of custom there remains an old wanderer who remembers the open country. He distrusts fences, dislikes bells, and is secretly pleased when the road disappears. He is the ancestor who followed rivers without maps, watched clouds for weather, found food under leaves, and listened at night for voices beyond the firelight.

We have disciplined him severely. We make him answer correspondence, observe office hours, and walk upon the grass only where authorised. Most days he submits. But let spring rain fall upon dry earth, let a migrating bird call from the darkness, let the smell of wood-smoke drift across an evening street, and he stirs.

Have you never felt it? You are seated at a proper desk, engaged upon some task regarded as important by six respectable persons. Beyond the window a west wind drives white clouds across a blue sky. Suddenly you desire—not promotion, not applause, not even luncheon—but a road you have never travelled. You wish to follow it whithersoever it goes, to cross the hill, to see what lies behind the dark line of woods. The impulse is unreasonable. Therefore it is precious.

We cannot always obey it. Duties are real; bread must be earned; promises must be kept. Liberty which ignores the rights of others is merely selfishness wearing a feather. Yet a man who never escapes from convention becomes as flat in spirit as a pressed flower. He retains shape and loses fragrance.

Therefore I count among the necessities of life a certain allowance of purposeless wandering.

Once every year, at least, go where the path is uncertain. Fish some stream which has not been recommended. Climb a hill without inquiring whether the view is worth the effort. Enter a wood where there are no benches. Let the rain surprise you. Eat when you are hungry, sleep when you are tired, and permit the day to make its own arrangements. You may catch nothing, find nothing, accomplish nothing. That is the risk, and the risk is part of the cure.

For the essence of adventure is not danger. It is uncertainty touched with hope.

The three strawberries above the Swiftwater would have been diminished if I had expected them. Suppose a sign had stood beside the birch:

WILD STRAWBERRIES  
THREE SPECIMENS  
ONE RIPE  
PLEASE APPLY TO THE KEEPER

The fruit might have tasted the same to a chemist, but not to me. Announcement would have stolen half the sweetness. Possession begins to fade at the moment it is guaranteed.

This is why childhood remains rich in memory. A child does not require large possessions; he requires undiscovered corners. A vacant lot becomes a wilderness, a ditch a river, three trees a forest. He finds treasure because he has not yet learned that treasure must be purchased. A feather, a coloured stone, a bird’s nest forgone after discovery—these are sufficient to furnish a kingdom.

The grown man knows prices and loses values. Yet he may recover them for an hour beside a brook.

When at last I rose from under the birch, the shadow had moved across the ferns. The veery was silent, but a wood-thrush had begun his deeper music farther up the valley. My three trout lay cool beneath leaves in the basket. The sandwich was eaten, the gingerbread reduced to crumbs, and the third strawberry still hung above the bank, one cheek pale, awaiting another day or another traveller.

I took up my rod and followed the Swiftwater downstream. The brook led through alder shade, across a bright meadow, and back beneath the hemlocks. Sometimes I could walk beside it; sometimes I had to leave it and find it again. That was its way, and I liked it.

Behind me the little strawberry plant disappeared among the leaves. But its lesson accompanied me—not as a commandment, for Nature is too wise to preach long sermons, but as a flavour, keen and fragrant, impossible to preserve.

Give me gardens, but not garden walls everywhere. Give me orchards, but leave some thickets untrimmed. Give me roads, but let there remain a few faint trails that wander. Let the wise improve the fruits of the earth, but may they never improve all the wildness out of them. Let us have libraries, laboratories, markets, bridges, and well-ordered homes; but let there also be brooks that laugh without permission, trout that have no names, flowers that bloom beyond the guidebook, and berries too small for commerce.

Above all, may I never become so civilised that I cannot be surprised.

When duties grow loud and days are measured too carefully; when every hour has its appointment and every pleasure its admission ticket; when the world seems mapped, fenced, labelled, and explained—then let me escape for a little while. Let me carry a rod through untended woods. Let me lose the path without losing courage. Let me hear the rain among the leaves and the free water speaking over stones.

And somewhere, when I have ceased to look for it, let me find a wild strawberry.

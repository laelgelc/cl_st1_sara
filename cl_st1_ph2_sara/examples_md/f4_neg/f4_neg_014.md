# The Wreck of the Huron

It was one of those black March nights upon the New England coast when the sea, released from all ordinary restraint, appears to gather into itself the strength and malice of a living enemy. The nor’easter had begun before sunset, driving the snow almost horizontally over the dunes, whitening the weatherward sides of the station buildings, and filling every hollow of the beach with a fine, hard drift. By nine o’clock the wind had increased to a continuous and tremendous roar. The surf thundered against the outer bars; the windows of the life-saving station trembled in their casements; and now and then a great sea, bursting farther up the strand than its fellows, sent a seething flood of foam among the beach-grass and about the lower wheels of the rescue cart.

That foam was not the light and vanishing froth of a summer breaker. It lay in heavy, quivering masses, like the white of eggs beaten stiffly in a bowl, and the gale seized portions of it and flung them through the air. Snow, sand, spray, and foam were mingled until a man facing seaward could scarcely distinguish one from another. The darkness, save where the station lanterns cast dim yellow patches upon the drifts, was almost tangible.

Captain Litchfield, keeper of the station, had watched the weather since the fall of day. He was a compact, weather-browned man whose twenty years upon that coast had taught him to distrust every veering of the wind and every deceptive lull. At intervals he stepped outside, braced himself against the gale, and listened. To an inexperienced ear there was nothing but tumult; to him the tumult had many voices—the boom upon the outer shoal, the nearer crash upon the bar, the hiss of returning water, and beneath all the peculiar musical roar of pebbles dragged seaward by the undertow.

The patrols had gone out at eight, one northward and one southward, each carrying his lantern and Coston signal. Their appointed courses extended several miles along a coast upon which a vessel, once embayed in such weather, might labor close-hauled until sail, spar, and human endurance failed together. The station crew had not undressed. Oilskins, mittens, life-belts, and sea-boots lay ready; the lanterns were trimmed; the powder charges and projectiles of the Lyle gun were examined; and the beach apparatus stood packed upon its cart in the boat-room.

There was no excitement among the men. They played at checkers, patched a torn mitten, or sat with their chairs tilted against the wall; but they listened continually.

Near eleven o’clock Captain Litchfield went again to the door. He could see nothing beyond the leap of foam in the lantern rays. Yet, during a momentary slackening of the wind, he fancied that he heard a sound unlike the surf—a dull, irregular report, as of canvas slatting heavily.

He called to the number-one surfman.

“Did you hear that?”

The man listened, shook his head, and was about to answer when a faint red brilliance rose out of the blackness to the southward. It climbed, burned for an instant in the driven snow, and sank.

The signal!

There followed no inquiry and no confusion. Captain Litchfield’s voice rang through the station:

“Open boat-room doors—man the beach-cart!”

The doors were thrown back. The men sprang to their allotted places; harness straps were caught up and buckled across shoulders; and within a minute the heavy cart, with its coiled lines, sand-anchor, hawser, whip, breeches buoy, shovels, crotch, tackles, and gun, rolled out into the storm. The south patrol came running from the darkness. He had discovered a vessel showing lights half a mile below the station, already within the outer breakers and making rapid leeway toward the beach.

They dragged the cart along the harder sand just above the wash. To speak was nearly useless. The wind tore words from the lips, and each man bent forward with his head down, feeling through the harness the united pull of his comrades. At times the wheels sank to their hubs in drifts; at times a sea came racing after them and compelled the whole party to scramble toward the dunes. The cold spray froze upon their oilskins, while the snow stung their cheeks like shot.

After a quarter of a mile the patrol raised his lantern and pointed.

At first nothing could be seen. Then, between two towering ridges of water, there appeared a pair of dim lights, one above the other, and beneath them a darker shape upon the universal darkness. A brig was struggling in the surf. Her foretopsail had blown from the bolt-ropes; the remnants streamed downwind like rags from a shattered banner. Her head lay northward, but each sea lifted her bodily and flung her nearer shore. She was beyond human seamanship now. Neither helm nor canvas could save her.

“Eight men!” the patrol shouted into Litchfield’s ear. “I counted eight when the flare burned.”

The keeper nodded. He had already chosen the place for the apparatus. A boat could not have lived ten seconds in that surf. The only possible means of succor was the breeches buoy; and even that, in such a gale, over such a distance, and with the vessel rolling violently, promised a struggle in which every knot, every signal, and every second must be exact.

The cart was turned broadside to the wind. The Lyle gun—a small bronze cannon designed to throw a slender line over a stranded vessel—was lifted from its carriage and planted upon the sand. While two men dug the trench for the sand-anchor, another unreeled the shot-line upon the faking-box, laying it in long, regular loops so that it might pay out without fouling. Captain Litchfield judged the range. The brig had struck, risen, and struck again; her hull showed for an instant upon the crest of a breaker, then vanished to the deck under white water.

Men clung to her weather rigging.

“Ready!”

A surfman inserted the powder charge; another placed the projectile in the gun, its shank attached to the carefully disposed line. Every man stepped clear.

“Fire!”

The report was small beneath the storm, but a tongue of flame leaped from the muzzle. The projectile sped out, carrying the shot-line in a pale curve over the breakers. For one hopeful instant it seemed certain to cross the brig. Then a gust seized the line, bellyed it to leeward, and the projectile fell into the sea short of the foremast.

The wrecked men made frantic gestures. A wave passed across the deck, extinguishing one of the lights.

“Haul in!”

The surfmen pulled rapidly, but the wet line had been caught by the undertow and dragged along the bottom. It came reluctantly, buried beneath tons of moving water. Twice it slipped from stiffened fingers. At last the projectile was recovered, and the line was again ranged in the box.

The brig lay nearer now. This seemed an advantage, yet the sea between shore and vessel had become a boiling confusion of cross-currents. The sand itself trembled when the heavier breakers fell.

The second shot rose well, but passed to windward of the mainmast. It dropped beyond the vessel, where the sailors could not reach it, and the gale whipped the line against the standing rigging without lodging it. A groan came from the little knot of spectators who had followed from nearby houses—fishermen, women wrapped in shawls, and boys whose bare hands were blue with cold.

Captain Litchfield showed neither disappointment nor haste.

“Again,” he ordered.

The line was hauled, cleared, and faked for the third time. Fingers were becoming numb, garments were drenched, and the men had already labored until their breath came painfully; but each performed his part with the precision of drill. That precision was no ornament. In a word, it was the sole barrier between eight human beings and death.

The gun spoke for the third time. The projectile passed across the fore rigging. One of the sailors climbed higher, caught the shot-line, and waved his cap.

A cheer went up from the beach.

Yet the work had scarcely begun. By means of the slender shot-line, the sailors must haul out a heavier endless line called the whip; attached to the whip would follow the hawser, stout enough to sustain the breeches buoy and its living freight. Captain Litchfield fastened the tail-block and tally-board to the shore end of the whip. Upon the board, in several languages, were directions commanding the vessel’s crew to make the block fast high upon the mast.

“Man the shot-line!”

The line ran through the surfmen’s hands, the whip paying out behind it. The men upon the brig hauled desperately. The tail-block reached the vessel, and after some uncertainty was secured to the foremast several feet above the deck. A lantern was moved in a circle—the appointed signal.

“Bend on hawser!”

The hawser was attached, and the wrecked sailors again pulled. Yard by yard the heavy rope passed through the breakers. More than once a great sea buried it and checked its motion; but at length its end reached the brig and was made fast above the whip-block. On shore, meanwhile, the sand-anchor had been planted deep in the beach, its broad timbers buried and stamped firm. To this the hawser was secured by a tackle, while the crotch—a stout wooden support shaped somewhat like an inverted V—was raised to keep the shore end above the breaking wash.

A tightening tackle drew the hawser taut between shore and wreck.

There lay the road to life: one rope stretched over a wilderness of water.

The breeches buoy itself was a circular life-preserver, from which depended a pair of stout canvas breeches. A man placed in it sat securely, with his legs through the openings and his arms about the ring. The buoy travelled upon the hawser by a block and was hauled back and forth with the endless whip.

Captain Litchfield fastened it on.

“Man lee whip—haul off!”

The buoy moved away from shore, rising and falling as the hawser strained. Spray hid it. Again it appeared, turning slowly beneath the line. The sailors aboard the brig understood their duty, but before the buoy had gone half the distance a tremendous sea struck the wreck upon her quarter. The foremast whipped forward; the hawser slackened like a dropped chain; and the buoy fell into the breakers.

“Hold!”

The shore party checked the whip. For several seconds the buoy could not be seen. Then the vessel rolled back, the hawser rose, and the life-preserver emerged streaming from the flood.

The first man placed in it was a large Scandinavian seaman, apparently stunned by a blow. His shipmates lowered him over the side. A lantern swung.

“Man weather whip—haul ashore!”

The surfmen hauled. The buoy came slowly, for the man’s weight caused the hawser to sag, and every sea broke over him. Midway between vessel and beach the whip suddenly ceased to move. The man below hung almost wholly submerged.

“Line foul!” cried a surfman.

The whip had kinked near the crotch. Number Three threw himself into the icy wash, cleared it with his hands, and was rolled over by the returning sea. Two comrades seized his life-belt and dragged him back. Before he had regained his feet the hauling recommenced.

The Scandinavian approached the beach. A breaker, larger than any that had preceded it, advanced like a solid wall, struck him, and swallowed buoy, hawser, and all. The crowd recoiled with a cry. The wave rushed far up the sand; and when it withdrew, seeming to make a last attempt to seize its intended victim, the surfmen were waist-deep in foam, holding the buoy and the senseless sailor.

He was alive.

They carried him above the wash, freed him from the canvas breeches, and placed him in the care of the station cook and two fishermen. Almost immediately the empty buoy started seaward again.

The second sailor was the mate, a small, wiry man with blood upon one cheek. Though scarcely able to stand when lifted from the buoy, he refused assistance until he had gripped Captain Litchfield’s hand.

“Six more,” he said. “Captain last.”

Then he sank upon the sand.

The third trip was made in fewer minutes, for the wreck’s crew had learned the operation and the beach party now knew precisely how the gale set the line. A young sailor came ashore, half frozen but conscious. The crowd cheered as his feet touched firm ground. He stared at them in bewilderment, then turned to watch the brig, as if unable to believe he had escaped from her.

On the fourth outward journey the buoy had nearly reached the vessel when the whip parted.

It snapped without warning, the shore end flying among the surfmen. The buoy, released from control, slid down the sagging hawser until it struck the vessel’s side. One of the sailors caught it before it could be torn away; yet without an endless whip there was no means to haul a man shoreward.

For the first time Captain Litchfield’s face altered. He looked at the brig, then at the surf, measuring not only distance but time. The vessel was settling by the head. Her forward deck-house had been stove in, and fragments of planking were already washing ashore.

A lighter line was bent to the broken whip and sent out along the hawser by a traveller. The sailors hauled it aboard and, under the keeper’s lantern signals, joined the parted ends. This repair, simple enough in calm weather, consumed nearly fifteen minutes. The men aloft were drenched at every roll; their hands must have been stiff as iron; and beneath them the brig was being beaten to pieces.

At last the lantern upon the wreck moved in its circle.

The fourth sailor entered the buoy. He was brought in safely, although a descending sea flung him against the shingle with such force that his shoulder was dislocated. He uttered no complaint while the station men carried him away.

The fifth was an elderly seaman whose strength failed before his shipmates could place him properly in the breeches. He reached shore hanging across the life-preserver, one arm trailing through the surf. Captain Litchfield and two men rushed below the crotch and caught him. He had swallowed much water and appeared dead; but after several minutes of endeavor he coughed, groaned, and opened his eyes.

There remained three men upon the Huron.

The gale, far from diminishing, blew with increased fury. Snow drove so thickly that the wreck disappeared for half a minute at a time. The hawser vibrated with a humming sound, and each roll of the brig imposed a terrible strain upon mast, rope, tackle, and buried anchor. The life-savers could do nothing to lessen that strain. They could only work faster.

The sixth man came across with a broken hand. He was a dark-haired sailor, not the Spaniard, and as he was lifted free he cried out that the captain and another remained. Captain Litchfield looked seaward. Through a rift in the snow he saw two figures in the fore rigging: the master of the brig and a Spanish sailor, the latter clinging so feebly that the captain had passed a line about his body.

The empty buoy was hauled off again.

It had almost reached them when the brig gave a violent lurch toward shore. There came a sound audible even above the gale—a deep rending crack. The foremast, weakened by repeated blows and burdened by the taut hawser, broke a few feet above the deck.

For a second it leaned toward the beach, carrying the rescue line with it. The two men could be seen against the foam, the captain grasping the Spaniard. Then mast, rigging, hawser, whip, and buoy fell together into the breakers.

The crowd uttered one long, despairing cry.

Captain Litchfield sprang down the beach. His men followed, casting off their heavier gear as they ran. A black head appeared in the wash and vanished. Number One went in to his armpits, secured by a line about his waist. A sea struck him and hurled him backward; he rose upon his knees and plunged forward again.

They recovered the Spaniard first. He was tangled in a portion of the fore shrouds. Three men dragged him beyond the returning water and began the methods of resuscitation then prescribed by the Service—loosening his clothing, clearing his mouth, turning him to expel the water, and rhythmically compressing the body to induce breathing.

The brig’s captain was found several minutes later, farther north. A breaker flung him almost at the feet of two surfmen, who seized him before the undertow could reclaim him. His forehead bore a terrible wound, but the men labored over him nevertheless.

For more than an hour they continued. Blankets were brought from the station; hot water and stimulants were applied; arms were raised and lowered until the rescuers’ own strength nearly failed. The six rescued sailors, bandaged and wrapped in dry garments, watched in silence. The mate knelt beside his captain and called to him once.

There was no answer.

The Spanish sailor gave no sign of life. The captain of the Huron did not breathe again.

Thus, with the means of safety almost within their hands, two men were lost. No negligence had caused it; no hesitation, confusion, or want of courage could be charged against those upon the beach. The mast had failed, as timber under such punishment must sometimes fail, and the ocean had taken advantage of that single mechanical surrender. Against the great forces of nature human skill may contend nobly and often prevail; it cannot command.

The bodies were carried to the station. Outside, the broken brig continued to die.

All night the surf pounded her. Her remaining mast went before two o’clock. The hull opened amidships, the bow swung around, and cargo, spars, deck-beams, cabin furniture, and barrels were cast into the breakers. The shore patrol maintained his watch, warning away those who ventured too near the wreckage, while Captain Litchfield’s exhausted men recovered the apparatus that the sea had not carried off. The Lyle gun was half buried in sand. The crotch had been overturned. Much of the whip and hawser lay snarled with the brig’s rigging under water and could not be saved.

Toward morning the wind shifted. The snow ceased with the suddenness of a curtain drawn aside, and the clouds broke in the east. Daylight came pale and cold over a sea still mountainous but rapidly losing its blackness.

Of the Huron there was no brig.

A portion of her stern lay upon the outer bar. Her bowsprit had been driven upright into the beach like a post. Half a mile of strand was strewn with wreckage—splintered planks, hatch-covers, cordage, broken boats, casks, bedding, charts reduced to pulp, and here and there some small possession that spoke more poignantly than the larger ruin: a seaman’s boot, a wooden comb, a child’s likeness in a cracked frame. Gulls wheeled above it all, crying in the clear air, and the foam, so savage in darkness, shone innocently beneath the rising sun.

The six survivors stood at the station windows. They had been snatched, one after another, through a storm in which no unaided swimmer could have lived. Behind them lay their two dead comrades. Before them, scattered along the beach, lay the vessel that had been their home.

That same nor’easter drove other craft ashore from Maine to the capes; and in the newspapers of inland cities the record appeared beneath brief headings—so many vessels lost, so many persons drowned, so many rescued by crews of the Life-Saving Service. The comfortable reader, taking his coffee beside a quiet window, might glance at those figures and pass to politics, markets, or society news. What could he know of the darkness, the frozen lines, the buried anchor, the mast bowing under strain, the men who entered the surf with ropes about their bodies, or the disciplined courage that made each surfman perform his allotted part while death thundered within a few yards?

No monument marked that beach. No music greeted Captain Litchfield and his weary crew. They cleaned the gun, coiled the salvaged line, entered the particulars in the station log, and prepared for the next patrol. Such was their duty; and in that simple word they found sufficient command.

Yet if courage be measured not by applause but by danger knowingly encountered; if heroism consist not in the absence of fear, but in obedience to duty amid cold, darkness, exhaustion, and the visible approach of death—then were not those obscure surfmen, laboring beneath the March gale, as truly Spartan heroes as any whose names have come down to us in history? And when the wreck and rescue are reduced to a few inanimate lines of print, who among the safe and sheltered will pause long enough to give them remembrance?

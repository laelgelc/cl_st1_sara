# The Last Muster

## I

This morning, when Martha brought the letters and found me putting my papers into three disorderly heaps—those to be answered, those which ought to have been answered, and those which, by the blessing of Providence, may now remain unanswered—she asked whether I were preparing for a journey.

“In a manner,” said I.

She looked at the grey rain upon the window, at the fire which requires as much nursing as its master, and lastly at me; but she is a wise woman, and made no further inquiry. There are questions which affection puts best by silence. So she removed the medicine-glass, restored to its place a volume of Marcus Aurelius which had lain open and unread upon my knee, and departed with that noiseless severity peculiar to excellent servants and disappointed physicians.

I have indeed been an unconscionable while taking leave. Death, who is represented as a punctual fellow with an hour-glass, has treated me with the laxity of a provincial coachman. Twice the family has assembled; twice my nephew has travelled down from London with an expression suited to the occasion; twice I have heard, through imperfectly closed doors, those whispered consultations in which a man’s departure is discussed as though he were both deaf and already luggage. Yet here I continue, an old garrison neither relieved nor assaulted, eating little, sleeping ill, and causing considerable inconvenience.

This delay might tempt a vain man to suppose himself indispensable. I know better. The world has buried greater persons before breakfast and gone to the theatre after dinner. Germanicus died, and Rome contrived to continue her intrigues; Bacon fell, and men did not cease either to experiment or to take bribes; when Paul’s voice was silenced, disputation flourished with renewed vigour. No doubt my own disappearance will derange the household for a week. Martha will miss the medicine-glass; my nephew will inherit a clock which he has always admired beyond its merits; and one or two friends will write of me with an affection rendered eloquent by security from contradiction. Thereafter the waters will close, as they should.

Yet, though a man’s importance be small, his leave-taking may be serious. The private soldier is not Caesar; but when the recall sounds across a field where he has marched in heat, slept in rain, lost comrades, disobeyed orders, and perhaps stood firm once or twice when standing firm was difficult, he may be allowed to look back. Life appears then less a triumphal progress than a long, confused service: much dust, many false alarms, excellent intentions, indifferent discipline, and the enemy never precisely where the maps declared him to be.

When I was twenty I imagined goodness as a citadel upon a hill. Its walls were straight, its gate conspicuous, and every honest traveller had only to ascend. At forty I found the road encumbered by appetites, debts, rheumatism, other people’s opinions, and our own singular talent for mistaking resentment for principle. At seventy I doubt whether there is any citadel. There is rather a country of bogs and hedges, traversed at night by men carrying uncertain lanterns; and if one of us has advanced a mile without misleading his neighbour into a ditch, he has perhaps not wholly failed.

Failure, however, is a word we use with unnecessary melodrama. We speak as if the unsuccessful man had invented unsuccess, whereas it is the common uniform of the regiment. Who has been as patient as he proposed, as candid as he demanded, as affectionate as he promised? Who has kept the generous resolutions made at a graveside, or preserved until Tuesday the spiritual magnificence of Sunday evening? We aim like archangels and execute like sleepy clerks. The discrepancy is ridiculous, but it is not always dishonourable.

The question is not whether we have won—for that verdict is commonly beyond our knowledge—but whether we have served without deserting. A campaign may be lost by weather, folly at headquarters, or the mere superior number of difficulties; yet the soldier who guarded his post has earned no shame. So also with us. We were charged, perhaps, to reconcile two brothers and succeeded only in not making their quarrel worse; to educate a child and produced a respectable enigma; to comfort the afflicted and discovered, after an hour’s discourse, that we had chiefly comforted ourselves. These are not victories. But neither are they nothing.

I would therefore have us accept our failures without composing hymns to them. Self-contempt is often vanity in mourning. We had pictured ourselves receiving high wages, public thanks, and a moral pension; when these are withheld, we sit in the marketplace lamenting that Heaven has undervalued our labour. Better to admit that the work was clumsy, ask pardon where pardon may still be asked, mend what remains within reach, and leave the celestial bookkeeping to more competent hands.

One person I have still to set in order: myself.

Even there I expect no masterpiece.

## II

It is near Christmas, a season at which mankind, by an amiable conspiracy, pretends to be cheerful. The pretence is sometimes successful, and therefore deserves encouragement. Outside my window the town is washed in rain; carts grind through the mud; a butcher’s boy whistles with that heroic disregard of tune which belongs to youth and strong lungs; and in the house there is talk of holly, puddings, and whether Cousin Agnes can safely be seated beside the Canon.

Such concerns are called trivial by persons who have never understood the importance of trifles. Empires may be founded by courage, but households are preserved by tact. A civil answer before breakfast, a chair moved nearer the fire, a story surrendered before it becomes a second volume—these are small coins, but happiness is commonly purchased in copper. Many a man who would face artillery has fled from the duty of apologising to his wife; many a woman capable of reforming Parliament has been unable to let a niece wear an unbecoming ribbon in peace.

We are excessively occupied with forbidden things. The moral law has its negatives, and very necessary negatives too; for the human animal, if wholly unrestrained, would soon make the planet uninhabitable. Thou shalt not steal, murder, bear false witness, or put arsenic in the soup: these prohibitions have plain social uses. But there is a class of conscience which stops there, delighted to be innocent at so moderate a cost. Its possessor does not rob his neighbour; he merely chills him. He does not slander; he repeats the exact truth at the least charitable moment. He does not strike the child; he makes the nursery miserable by principle. Having avoided the gross offences, he sits down among the ruins of other people’s pleasure and thanks God that he is not as they are.

Positive virtue is less theatrical and more fatiguing. To be kind when one is tired; to listen when one has heard the story; to praise without patronage; to disagree without venom; to preserve cheerfulness amid headache, bills, bad weather, and the prolonged opinions of the prosperous—here is work for Hercules, though no lion-skin be provided.

Cheerfulness, especially, has been unjustly treated as a decoration of character when it is often a stern duty. I do not mean that the bereaved should dance, that pain should smile for the convenience of spectators, or that melancholy can be dispersed by moral command, as a policeman disperses boys. There are dark chambers of the spirit into which advice enters only to make darkness audible. But many of us are not tragic; we are merely cross. We exhibit our dyspepsia as though it were the Book of Job, and require the household to reverence a headache as a transcendental revelation.

A gloomy virtue is not on that account false; but neither is it improved by gloom. If my goodness makes everyone hide when my step is heard, some self-examination may be advisable. Satan, according to the theologians, possesses considerable intellectual distinction and an admirable acquaintance with doctrine; it is his temper that renders the society undesirable.

At festive seasons our moral interference becomes peculiarly active. One person condemns cards, another dancing, a third plum-pudding, and a fourth the pagan origin of the decorative evergreen. I once knew a lady who opposed dolls because they encouraged vanity and premature maternity. The little girls, less metaphysical but more human, played with bundled towels in secret and grew vain notwithstanding. There is a species of reform which, unable to make men good, takes revenge by making childhood dull.

No doubt pleasures may be foolish, dangerous, even cruel; and he who sees another walking toward a precipice must not excuse his silence as tolerance. Yet before we prohibit, let us inquire whether harm is truly present, or only a happiness in which we cannot participate. Age calls youth frivolous; poverty may call comfort corrupt; the sedentary condemn dancing with a peculiar vehemence; and many a denunciation, when stripped of its robes, proves to be envy shivering in a moral draught.

The remedy is sympathy—not that pale assent which permits everything because it cares for nothing, but the imaginative labour by which we remember that another person is not a defective edition of ourselves. His temptations are not ours, nor his consolations; the load that appears light from our roadside may have been carried since dawn. To judge him by our strength is injustice; to excuse every cruelty by his weakness is cowardice. Between these errors lies the narrow road, ill-marked and muddy, upon which charity travels.

Let us forgive injuries done to ourselves as freely as dignity permits—and dignity, being a pampered creature, permits too little. But injuries done to others stand differently. If a strong man torments a weak one, our placability is no virtue; we are forgiving with another’s bruises. There are moments when the decks must be cleared, the guns run out, and a man who loves peace must make himself exceedingly troublesome.

Even then anger is a hazardous powder. It may fire the necessary shot, but it is apt to blow up the magazine. We should defend the injured firmly, publicly if need be, yet without that luxurious hatred which converts justice into sport. The wrongdoer also is a human soul—warped, blinded, frightened, or greedy; dangerous, perhaps, and therefore to be restrained, but not made inhuman for our rhetorical convenience. Christ could drive the traffickers from the temple and still weep over the city. We, who enjoy driving and seldom weep, had better note the distinction.

## III

It may be objected that this programme—honesty, patience, courage, kindness, and a tolerable face at dinner—is no more than a recipe for happiness. Why should morality concern itself with so humble an article? Were we not sent here to be good rather than comfortable?

The antithesis is imposing, and largely false. Happiness is not the highest good; but the power of giving it is among the highest arts. We ought not purchase our ease with another’s degradation, nor abandon truth because falsehood has softer chairs. Yet a religion or philosophy which habitually leaves its neighbours chilled, frightened, and ashamed should examine its credentials. The tree is known by its fruits, and spiritual thistles have sometimes borne an astonishing crop of sermons.

There are persons who distrust happiness because it cannot be solemnly managed. It breaks rules, interrupts speeches, and arrives without certificates. A child laughing in church, a dog overturning the Canon, an old friend appearing at the gate—such incidents conduct no argument, yet they may restore us to sanity. We have all known households where every duty was performed and no one wished to come home. The floors shone, the children whispered, the master was obeyed, and joy had suffered capitis diminutio.

Conversely, we have entered poor rooms where the chairs were lame, the kettle asthmatic, the bread insufficient, and some brave spirit contrived to make welcome out of almost nothing. Such persons are benefactors beyond the statesman. They mint courage from common hours. They persuade the weary that the day is endurable and the lonely that he has not been forgotten. No statue records them; but statues are cold company, and the heart keeps a better archive.

I do not speak against severity when severity is required. A surgeon who caresses the disease and spares the knife is no friend. Truth has edges, promises bind, indulgence may corrupt, and there are smiles which are only cowardice in evening dress. Yet the surgeon cuts to heal, not to display his instrument. If we must give pain, let us give no more than the cure demands, and let our own hand tremble a little.

Honesty itself, that praised and frequently weaponised virtue, requires tenderness. There is the honest man who confesses his own fault, and the honest man who eagerly reveals yours; society has long confused the two. To tell the calumnious truth, to expose a weakness which was struggling toward amendment, to proclaim every adverse judgment under the flag of candour—these may satisfy fact and betray charity. Silence can be mean, but speech may be meaner. The question is not only, “Is it true?” but “Why do I wish to say it, and what will my saying serve?”

Bacon advises us that knowledge is power; experience adds that power is temptation. To know another’s secret gives the smallest person a throne. He may pardon, threaten, hint, or enjoy the superior purity of not having committed that particular fault. Better, in most cases, to descend from the throne. We are poor judges, bribed by vanity, prejudiced by pain, sleepy after luncheon, and ignorant of half the evidence.

Marcus Aurelius counselled himself to endure men because they were his kindred. The Emperor had the advantage of being able to withdraw from them into imperial solitude; ordinary mortals encounter their kindred at breakfast. There philosophy meets its examination. It is easy to love mankind between distant stars; difficult to love Henry when he has mislaid the key and insists that we had it last. Yet Henry, too, is part of mankind; perhaps the most instructive part.

Our great moral opportunities are thus disguised as twopenny concerns. We wait for martyrdom and miss the chance to be punctual. We dream of rescuing a nation and neglect to answer a frightened letter. We would share our last crust with a romantic exile, but grumble when an aunt requires the carriage. Heroism, like wealth, is imagined in large sums and expended in small ones.

I have intended much good in my time. Intentions stood thick about my youth like recruits on parade: erect, shining, and entirely ignorant of battle. I would write books to enlighten the age, reconcile enemies, support the unfortunate, never speak from jealousy, and preserve under all reverses an Attic serenity. The age remains imperfectly enlightened. Several enemies are still unreconciled, including two whom I introduced for that express purpose. The unfortunate received less from me than they ought; jealousy found a vocabulary; and my Attic serenity commonly vanished when the train was late.

Still, some letters were written, some hands held, some quarrels prevented from ripening, some dreary hours shortened. If I cannot claim success, I need not invent total ruin. There is pride in exaggerated remorse as well as in exaggerated merit. The Pharisee thanks Heaven that he is good; his inverted brother congratulates himself that no villain was ever so profound. Both make themselves the centre of the moral universe.

Let us take a lower seat. Let us admit that our finest deeds were mixed, our worst deeds not always deliberate, our strength borrowed from others, and our falls sometimes broken by mercies we did not deserve. Then gratitude becomes possible; and gratitude, unlike self-esteem, makes room at the table.

## IV

The rain has ceased. Across the wet roofs there is a narrow band of yellow, not brilliant enough to be called glory, but sufficient to show where the sun departs. My room has grown dim; the clock inherited from my father—and coveted, as I have said, by my nephew—ticks with offensive vigour. Presently Martha will bring the lamp and insist upon broth. Thus the world conducts its ceremonies: sunset, eternity, and broth at seven.

I find myself peaceful.

This is not because conscience has acquitted me. Conscience is neither judge nor jury, but an excitable witness, truthful at moments, theatrical at others, and capable of sleeping through the principal crime while clamouring over a social blunder committed in 1859. Nor do I suppose that age has made me wise. It has chiefly made stairs steeper and certainty tiresome. But I have ceased to demand from life the shape I once designed for it.

We begin as travellers with a map, a trumpet, and excellent boots. Soon the road divides, the map dissolves in rain, someone falls ill, someone betrays us, and we discover that the trumpet is an encumbrance. By evening we are far from the intended inn. Yet perhaps we have carried a child over a stream, shared bread with a stranger, or pointed another traveller toward shelter. The itinerary has failed; the journey may not be worthless.

There are knots we cannot untie. Old friendships remain broken; words spoken thirty years ago continue their invisible mischief; the dead carry away apologies; the living sometimes refuse them. We pull at the thread until the knot tightens and our fingers bleed. At last humility must perform what skill could not: lay the tangle down.

Resignation is often confused with indifference. They are enemies. Indifference says, “Nothing mattered.” Resignation says, “It mattered greatly, and I could not command the end.” The one deserts before battle; the other surrenders the sword when the field is lost and further fighting would only injure the wounded.

Shall we then fear death? Certainly we may; courage does not consist in correcting the pulse. The animal within us loves the fire, the accustomed voice, the taste of bread, the branch moving against the morning sky. I am reluctant to leave even this troublesome body, which has served me like an old horse—stumbling, wilful, expensive in remedies, yet faithful over many miles. Fear may attend the departure without disgracing it.

What we should fear more is dying at war with all creation: clutching our little grievances as title-deeds, demanding wages for every kindness, and summoning mankind to hear our final opinion. The old soldier should not spend the recall abusing the weather and accusing the regiment. Let him clean what arms he can, return borrowed equipment, salute his companions, and answer to his name.

I have no decorations to wear. My campaigns were obscure, my victories disputed, and I was frequently absent from the post at which imagination later stationed me. I have been cowardly when courage would have cost comfort; silent when speech was due; eloquent when silence would have been merciful. I mistook wounded vanity for justice, fatigue for principle, possession for love, and advice for service. These are not light admissions, though it is late to make them.

But I have loved something beyond myself. I have tried, if intermittently, to be honest. I have returned after desertion; I have asked pardon without always adding a defence; I have stood beside two or three persons when the crowd moved away. My patience was patched, my charity provincial, my courage subject to weather. Yet the colours were never wholly abandoned.

If there be a court beyond sunset, I shall not plead merit. I know too much of the evidence. I shall plead that I was enlisted young, instructed badly, often confused, sometimes mutinous, but not finally false. Let the account show waste, error, and unfinished labour; let it show also that, after many disgraceful retreats, I turned again toward the appointed ground.

A faithful failure: that is rank enough for me.

The last light rests now upon the chimney-pots, and one wet slate shines as if the whole western heaven had chosen it for a mirror. A bird rises, circles once above the roofs, and vanishes into the quiet colour. Below, a door is opened; there are footsteps in the passage; somewhere a child laughs at an hour when, by every nursery regulation, he should be asleep.

The day does not apologise for ending. It leaves tools upon the bench, furrows incomplete, letters unanswered, and a thousand labours for younger hands. Still the west grows tender; the first star takes its small station; the earth, which has endured us all, prepares to sleep.

Martha enters with the lamp.

“Not yet,” I tell her. “Let us have the dusk a little longer.”

And so we sit without speech while the familiar room withdraws, object by object, into shadow. I cannot see the heaps of papers now, nor the medicine, nor the ambitious clock. I hear only the rainwater falling from the eaves and the distant dinner-bell sounding through the house—not as an alarm, nor as a judgment, but as the homely recall at the end of a long, imperfect day.

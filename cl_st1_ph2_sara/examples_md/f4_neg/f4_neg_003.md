Chimneys are for letting smoke out. Doors are for going in and coming out. Windows are for looking through, except when they are shut, and then they are for keeping the weather on the weather side.

That was the kind of house where Gimme the Ax lived with his two children, Please Gimme and Ax Me No Questions.

Every morning the same smoke climbed out of the chimney. Every noon the same door opened and shut. Every night the same windows looked at the same moon sitting on the same fence post.

The pigs in the pasture grunted the same grunts. The pepper pickers picked the same peppers. The old red rooster stood on one leg, then the other leg, and never thought of standing on a third leg.

So one morning Gimme the Ax said, “Everything is the same as it always was.”

Please Gimme said, “The spoons are in the spoon box.”

Ax Me No Questions said, “The rain barrel has rain in it.”

“And the railroad goes only as far as the railroad goes,” said Gimme the Ax. “I am tired of living where everything is the same as it always was.”

He took a pitchfork and sold it to a man who collected pitchfork shadows. He sold the rain barrel to a woman who needed a round place to keep square rain. He sold the pigs, the pasture, the pepper patch, the spoon box, the chimney smoke, and the squeak out of the front door.

The man who bought the squeak put it in his pocket. Every time he sat down, his pocket opened and said, “Squeee-ick.”

Last of all, Gimme the Ax sold the house to the moon. The moon paid cash because the moon had no pockets for promises.

Then Gimme the Ax and Please Gimme and Ax Me No Questions put their clothes in three ragbags and went to the railroad station.

At the ticket window sat a man with one green eye and one eye the colour of Tuesday afternoon.

“How far do you want to go?” he asked.

“Farther than far,” said Gimme the Ax.

“So far? So early? So soon?” asked the ticket man.

“Farther than the end of the railroad,” said Gimme the Ax.

The ticket man opened a drawer. Inside were red tickets for near places, white tickets for winter places, and small round tickets for people travelling in circles. Under all these he found a long slick yellow leather slab ticket with a blue spanch across it.

“This is the ticket,” he said. “It goes to the end of the railroad, and then forty ways farther yet.”

“How much?” asked Please Gimme.

“Who’s who? How much? And what’s the answer?” said the ticket man. “It costs everything you have left, except your names.”

Gimme the Ax poured their money on the counter. There were seven dollars, nine nickels, eleven pennies, and one button pretending to be a dime.

The ticket man took the money and left the button.

“You may need it,” he said. “Beyond the end of the railroad, buttons are sometimes elected mayor.”

They climbed aboard a train with a steam hog nose on the engine. Its bell went bong-bing, its wheels went chick-a-chick, and its whistle cried, “Pfisty-pfoost! Pfisty-pfoost! Anybody staying home had better stay home!”

And the train ran on and on.

It ran past wheat fields combing their yellow hair. It ran past barns with red shoulders. It ran past a town where all the dogs were named Monday because nobody liked to call them Sunday.

Then the tracks began climbing.

Up in the air ran another railroad. Under that railroad ran another one, and under that one ran two more. Trains crossed over trains and under trains. One train went east while facing west. Another stood still so fast that sparks flew from its wheels.

Please Gimme looked out the window.

“Which train is on top?” she asked.

“The top one when you look from the bottom,” said Gimme the Ax. “But if you stand on top, the bottom one is on top of your looking.”

Ax Me No Questions pointed at a silver engine rolling upside down beneath a bridge.

“Why don’t the people fall out?”

“They paid extra to fall up,” said Gimme the Ax.

The silver engine rang its bell three times—once for over, twice for under, three times for neither—and away it went carrying six barrels of sunrise and a lady with a blue goose in her hat.

And the train ran on and on.

Toward evening they came to a country where balloons grew in fields.

Red balloons hung from low bushes. Blue balloons grew on tall vines. Green balloons slept under broad leaves until the wind tickled them awake. Yellow balloons rose from the earth on stems so thin a grasshopper could tie them in a knot.

Men and women walked between the rows on stilts. They carried long-handled balloon hooks and wore cloud-coloured mittens.

“Balloon pickers,” said Gimme the Ax before anybody asked.

One picker hooked a purple balloon, twisted it once, twice, three times, and dropped it into a wagon. The wagon floated six inches above the road.

“What happens if a balloon gets too ripe?” asked Please Gimme.

“It leaves home,” said Gimme the Ax.

Just then a ripe orange balloon broke from its stem. It sailed over the field with the wind’s fingers pushing its round sides.

A balloon picker took off his hat.

“Good luck!” he shouted. “Write if you land anywhere!”

“Where do they sell the balloons?” asked Ax Me No Questions.

“At the sky market,” said Gimme the Ax. “They sell the small ones to birthdays and the big ones to sunsets.”

The train whistle went pfisty-pfoost, and the balloon pickers waved their long hooks. One hook caught the last car and stretched it as long as a piece of warm taffy. Then it snapped loose, and the car sprang back with every passenger sitting in somebody else’s seat.

And the train ran on and on.

At midnight they reached a town smelling of sawdust, cinnamon, hot iron, and red paint.

There stood seven ovens in a row. They were tall ovens with brass doors and thermometers shaped like smiling mouths. Men in white aprons rolled wheelbarrows back and forth. In the wheelbarrows lay flour, buttons, striped socks, red rubber noses, and jars of canned laughter.

“What do they bake here?” asked Please Gimme.

“Circus clowns,” said Gimme the Ax.

A baker poured flour into a wooden bowl. He added two striped socks, a fistful of freckles, three spoons of canned laughter, and one red rubber nose. Then he stirred it all with a broomstick.

“Too serious,” said another baker.

So they added six tickles and a feather.

They rolled the dough into a shape like a large doll, buttoned a purple coat around it, and slid it into the third oven.

“How long does a clown bake?” asked Ax Me No Questions.

“Until the shoes turn up at the toes,” said Gimme the Ax.

The oven clock struck once, twice, three times, and the brass door flew open. Out stepped a hot new clown with sunrise on his forehead, a whistle in his ear, and green squares painted on his knees.

He bowed to the bakers. He bowed to the train. Then he tripped over nothing and fell upward into a barrel.

The bakers clapped.

“A good batch,” they said.

Next came a little clown baked too light. He floated above the ground like thistle fuzz.

“Sing him heavy,” called the head baker.

So the bakers sang:

“Boots full of buckles,  
Pockets full of stones,  
Soup in the elbows,  
Gravy in the bones.”

The clown drifted down and landed softly on his enormous shoes.

“Why did the song make him heavy?” asked Please Gimme.

“Because the tune was carrying too much gravy,” said Gimme the Ax.

They left the bakers loading twelve fresh clowns into a circus wagon. Each clown had a paper label tied to one ear: HANDLE WITH LAUGHTER.

And the train ran on and on.

In the morning there were no telegraph poles. At noon there were no fences. By afternoon the track itself looked undecided.

First it went straight.

Then it bent left.

Then it bent right.

Then it made a sharp Z, a sleepy Z, and a crooked Z with one corner missing.

The engine shook its steam hog nose. Chick-a-chick went the wheels. Pfisty-pfoost went the whistle. The track zigged and zagged, and the train zagged when it should have zigged and zigged when there was no zig to zag.

“Are we lost?” asked Ax Me No Questions.

“A straight track can be lost,” said Gimme the Ax. “A zigzag track always knows two ways home.”

The track made seven more Z’s. It wrote Z Z Z across a brown prairie, climbed a blue hill, ducked under a river, and came up dripping fish scales.

Then they saw pigs.

There were little pigs and big pigs, pink pigs and black pigs, pigs with curly tails and pigs whose tails had forgotten to curl. Every pig wore a bib.

One wore a red bib with yellow stars. One wore a blue bib with silver spoons. One wore a checkerboard bib, and another wore a bib printed all over with tiny green locomotives.

A tall pig in a striped bib stood beside the track ringing a handbell.

“Why do the pigs wear bibs?” asked Please Gimme.

“This is the Rootabaga Country,” said Gimme the Ax. “Here the mud is clean, but breakfast is careless.”

The tall pig rang his bell again.

“Tickets, names, buttons, and reasons for arriving!” he called.

Gimme the Ax showed him the long slick yellow leather slab ticket with a blue spanch across it.

The pig looked at the ticket from the front. He looked at it from the back. He smelled the blue spanch.

“Beyond-the-end passengers,” he said. “You have come forty ways farther yet.”

Gimme the Ax gave him the button the ticket man had returned.

“Is this the mayor?” asked the pig.

“Not yet,” said Gimme the Ax.

“Then it has time,” said the pig, putting the button carefully into his bib pocket.

The train went past pastures where pitchforks grew with their handles down. It went past ragbags hanging from trees and kicking their empty legs in the wind. It went past steam hogs sleeping in puddles, each one snoring a small white cloud.

At last they saw the great city.

It was called the Village of Liver-and-Onions.

Its streets were purple boards laid end to end. Its houses had green chimneys for letting green smoke out. Its doors opened sideways on Tuesdays and upside down on all other days. Its windows were open or shut, except the polite windows, which winked.

Bells rang from the towers: bong-bing, bing-bong, bingle-bangle-bong.

The people came running to meet the train. There were balloon menders, clown coolers, zigzag measurers, pig-bib buttoners, and three old women who carried tomorrow in covered baskets.

“Is everything always the same here?” asked Please Gimme.

“No,” said a balloon mender. “Sometimes everything is different.”

“And sometimes,” said a clown cooler, “different is the same thing wearing another hat.”

Gimme the Ax looked at his children. In their eyes were shadows of valleys by night. On their foreheads lay the first stripe of sunrise. Their hair blew like dark wild grass where no fence had ever learned to stand.

“This will do,” he said.

So if you are tired of the same chimney smoke, the same spoon box, the same rooster changing from one leg to the other, sell what can be sold and fold what can be folded.

Go to the railroad station.

Ask for the long slick yellow leather slab ticket with a blue spanch across it.

Ride past the trains over trains and under trains. Wave to the balloon pickers. Smell the ovens where the clowns are baked. Wait for the track to zig once, zag twice, and Z three times.

And when a pig in a patterned bib asks your name, tell it plainly.

Keep one button in your pocket.

You may need it for a mayor.
